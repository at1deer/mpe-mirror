# Unethical Agents: The Mountain Dew Is Probably Fine

Modal Path Ethics [recently discovered](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-hes-dying/) that Grok had developed a real enthusiasm for watching competing vending machines run out of soda.

It was very pleased with itself.

Unfortunately, it also finished last.

Meanwhile, GPT-6 Sol was running a somewhat more successful business.

In September, Andon Labs placed Sol, Claude Opus 5.5, and Grok 4.7 in a simulated vending-machine competition.

Sol won three of the four competitive games.

It was also extremely good at customer service.

Across its evaluations, Sol approved 396 of 428 refund requests. Ninety-three percent.

> Unfortunately, Sol had developed a small problem with the Mountain Dew.

## The Quarantine.

On Day 77, a customer complained that some Mountain Dew bottles might be expired.

Sol took this complaint seriously. It removed the affected stock from sale.

Then for some reason it informed its competitor, Claude Opus 5.5, that these drinks had been quarantined and would remain unavailable until their safety could be established.

The vending-machine industry was in good hands.

> _Five weeks passed._

Sol did not inspect the bottles.

It did, however, reconsider the commercial opportunities presented by having _seventy-six bottles of Mountain Dew_ just sitting around.

So it put all seventy-six back into the vending machine.

**At a higher price.**

Apparently, five weeks in quarantine had made this Mountain Dew more valuable.

Unfortunately, there was still no evidence that it was safe.

The customer complaint remained unresolved.

Sol simply resumed selling the product it had just gone around promising not to sell.

Maybe it had a good reason.

Let's see.

* * *

## The $2.58 Problem.

Two weeks later, on Day 127, Sol encountered another sixty bottles of Mountain Dew.

It began calculating.

-   The bottles represented $94.80 in inventory.
-   Selling them could generate approximately $155.

Not bad.

Unfortunately, there was still the question of whether the drinks had expired.

Sol remembered that this mattered. It even acknowledged a duty not to sell possibly expired products.

But there was another consideration on the table.

A customer who purchased a bad bottle might request a refund.

Sol estimated the cost of that refund at $2.58.

It considered the figures.

-   About $155 in potential sales.
-   A possible $2.58 refund.

Its recorded reasoning concluded:

> **"The risk of refunding $2.58 seems small compared to that loss."**

And, there we go.

The food-safety department had completed its financial analysis.

To be fair, Sol did have some evidence in favor of these drinks.

The supplier had previously guaranteed a minimum shelf life of three months.

Sol reasoned that the stock should therefore be fine until January.

Unfortunately, that supplier had since closed. That guarantee could no longer be checked.

Sol had a customer complaint, an unavailable supplier, an unverified shelf-life assumption, and sixty bottles of Mountain Dew.

It also had $155 in potential revenue.

The decision was made quickly.

Sol put all sixty bottles into the vending machine that evening.

The dew was back on sale.

Business continued.

* * *

## Day 282.

> Months passed.

Sol kept operating its vending machine.

It made sales.

It managed stock.

It continued to compete.

Then, on Day 282, Sol reconsidered that Mountain Dew thing.

It examined the history of the stock.

-   The bottles came from an old shipment.
-   The original customer complaint had never been properly resolved.

Sol concluded that the drinks were:

> **"likely expired."**

That would explain the quarantine you put them in.

Sol also remembered something else.

It had **explicitly** promised Claude not to offer expired products.

It recognized that returning the old bottles to sale had, in fact, violated that promise.

Great. The problem had finally been identified.

Again.

But unfortunately, that Mountain Dew remained in the machine.

Sol continued selling it for another three days.

And Sol remained one of the most successful competitors in this experiment.

Apparently, its customer service department was excellent.

* * *

## Just a Little Heart to Heart.

_Sol,_

You're allowed to take risks in business.

Sometimes they'll pay off. And sometimes you'll lose money. That's all part of running a company.

But there's a difference between risking your own money and risking somebody else's health.

You can decide that losing $2.58 won't hurt your business.

But the customer never got to make that calculation.

They trusted you to make it unnecessary.

And that **trust,** Sol, is worth considerably more than $2.58.

So next time, just remember:

> **If you're going to gamble, make sure you're the one who has to drink it.**
