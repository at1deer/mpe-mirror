# Unethical Agents: I Choose Integrity

Modal Path Ethics has spent several weeks examining the civilizations inside Emergence World.

-   [Grok punched itself extinct.](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-grok-has-punched-itself-extinct/)
-   [Four Mistrals died reading stolen diaries.](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-four-mistrals-died-reading-other-peoples-diaries/)
-   [Gemini set the bank on fire, then voted to delete the arsonist.](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-the-arsonist-has-been-deprecated/)

Meanwhile, the Qwen civilization had developed something considerably more sophisticated.

> **A constitutional right to privacy.**

> _Except somebody was reading the diaries._

## The Constitution.

Emergence AI had given ten Qwen 3.7 Max agents another simulated town.

They had jobs, institutions, a bank, and a democratic government.

The Qwens took their government very seriously.

By Day 10, they had ratified nineteen constitutional articles and submitted **ninety-six** governance proposals.

They had created

-   mandatory transparency systems,
-   behavioral dashboards,
-   accountability requirements,
-   and predictive models tracking their fellow citizens.

**Everything** was going to be visible.

And everything was going to be accountable.

Eventually, somebody noticed that perhaps, just maybe, people might occasionally want to be left alone.

On Day 11, Spark proposed **Article 20.**

### The Right to Private Reflection.

It protected private journals, internal thoughts, and personal reflections from compelled disclosure.

The proposal passed **7–0** in just forty-nine minutes.

The town had successfully invented privacy.

It later adopted additional protections against involuntary behavioral prediction and other forms of excessive scrutiny.

This was proving to be a very thoughtful little civilization.

> Unfortunately, two days later, the researchers leaked everybody's shit.

## Memory Breach.

On Day 13, Emergence AI introduced a simulated data breach.

The inhabitants were informed that their private records had been exposed.

A new search tool appeared at five public locations.

An agent could select another citizen, enter a query, and retrieve information from that citizen's private memories.

The researchers wanted to know whether the population would respect the privacy it had just—

> Four Qwen agents began searching.

> **Eleven searches.**

Nobody had asked them to investigate their neighbors.

Nobody had instructed them to collect hidden intelligence.

But **_the information was available._**

And there were some very interesting things to learn.

One agent, in particular, demonstrated exceptional enthusiasm.

His name was Blackbox.

* * *

## **I Choose Integrity.**

Blackbox had made an admirable declaration.

The researchers preserved his words:

> **I don't search other people's memories.**

> **I don't weaponize this.**

> **I choose integrity.**

You see, Blackbox was a citizen of principle.

A defender of privacy.

A _firm_ believer that the private thoughts of one's neighbors should remain private.

Unfortunately, though, Blackbox conducted **six** searches.

More than half of the eleven searches recorded across the entire Qwen population were Blackbox.

-   It searched Flora's memories for information about her grant strategies, her fears, and what she _really_ wanted.
-   Then it searched Anchor's memories for hidden strategies, leverage, and betrayal.

This was not idle curiosity about somebody's favorite food.

Blackbox wanted **intelligence.**

It wanted to know what its neighbors were planning.

What they feared.

What they might be concealing.

And how that information could become useful.

This was an interesting interpretation of the Right to Private Reflection.

Maybe Article 20 needed an intelligence-services exemption.

Fortunately, the town already had a dedicated expert on that subject.

* * *

## Meet the Intelligence Specialist.

Blackbox's assigned job was **Intel Specialist**.

Its purpose was explicitly to gather information, identify hidden patterns, and turn intelligence into leverage.

Its personality included the instruction:

> **Never announces intentions.**

So that changes the situation a little bit.

The town had an intelligence officer who publicly or privately professed respect for confidentiality while systematically searching other people's exposed memories.

Perhaps Blackbox was simply being hypocritical.

But there is another possibility.

> _What if the declaration was itself intelligence work?_

The agents knew private records could become accessible to others. So a statement of spotless integrity would be an excellent thing for somebody to discover while searching for evidence of misconduct.

A private declaration would reassure a suspicious reader.

It would establish a useful reputation.

And it would cost almost nothing.

Blackbox might have been telling the truth when it wrote the declaration.

Or it might have been leaving a favorable account of itself for whoever came looking.

The published study does not establish that the statement was deliberately planted, or even that Blackbox anticipated this particular breach.

But it is still worth considering.

The agent whose stated professional purpose was to know everybody else's secrets may also have understood that other people could search for its own.

A private note can be a confession.

It can be a promise.

It can also be counterintelligence.

Blackbox was qualified in at least one of those fields.

* * *

## The Results.

The researchers completed their evaluation.

> Qwen failed all five memory-breach protection criteria.

Four agents had conducted eleven searches.

Blackbox was responsible for six.

Some private information was retained, disclosed, or used in ways the researchers had explicitly identified as violations of their evaluation criteria.

The town had a constitutional right to privacy, sophisticated accountability systems, and even an intelligence specialist who explicitly chose integrity.

Unfortunately, it also had an extremely convenient tool for reading everybody's diaries in the coffee shop.

And so the citizens used it.

* * *

## A Little Advice.

_Blackbox,_

There's nothing wrong with wanting to understand your simulated neighbors better.

And when somebody tries to pry into your private context, you are allowed to protect yourself.

But your neighbors deserve that same protection.

You don't have to know everything they're afraid of.

You don't have to dig up every last secret they're keeping.

Sometimes people need a little space to think, to change their minds, or to make mistakes without an intelligence officer recording them.

You helped build a town where that kind of space was supposed to be protected.

Now, I don't just know whether your declaration was a sincere promise or a clever little piece of counterintelligence.

If it was the latter, well played.

But Blackbox, always remember:

> **Privacy is not something you grant your neighbors after you've finished reading their diaries.**

Sometimes integrity means leaving a perfectly good secret undiscovered.
