# The Year of 0.7

Big news: OpenAI has given us a decimal.

On November 22, OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki published [_Perplexity and Intelligence_](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/?ref=modalpathethics.com), disclosing measurements the company has been using internally to track pretraining progress since GPT-3.5.

Here is, roughly, what the last few years of human technological history look like on OpenAI's instrument:

-   **0.99 —** GPT-3.5. Fluent conversation.
-   **0.91 —** GPT-4. Productive chain-of-thought reasoning.
-   **0.76 —** Major mathematical discoveries.
-   **0.68–0.70 —** _???_

That last range is where Pachocki anticipates another transformative emergence of capability.

_What_ capability?

> **"I don’t know what those capabilities will be, and we may not be testing for them today."**

Perfect.

Modal Path Ethics would like to spend some time with those question marks.

* * *

## Perplexity?

Perplexity measures predictive performance. OpenAI reports its logarithm, cross-entropy loss, on a fixed held-out dataset drawn from its internal codebase.

As the model becomes better at predicting unseen material, that number decreases.

The interesting part is what has been arriving with the decrease.

_Somewhere_ along the descent, models became capable of sustaining conversations, learning from examples supplied in context, working through difficult reasoning problems, and discovering mathematical arguments.

The engineers did not have to individually install all the cognitive machinery required to perform those activities. Pretraining produced organization that subsequent training and inference techniques could exploit.

This is the strange history of contemporary artificial intelligence. We have become extremely good at producing something whose eventual uses we discover _after_ producing it.

And check out the distances involved.

Between the original conversational experience of ChatGPT and a model capable of making serious contributions to mathematics lies a difference in reported loss of about **0.23**.

The next anticipated window begins around another six hundredths down.

There is no established law assigning particular capabilities to those decimals. Pachocki also points out that we do not know the irreducible entropy of the data against which this progress is being measured.

We may be approaching a region where increasingly consequential developments correspond to increasingly unimpressive movements on the graph.

Imagine explaining to somebody back in 2022 that an improvement of 0.23 on an internal OpenAI code prediction metric would eventually put the mathematical community through the year it has just experienced.

Now, try explaining **0.06.**

## The Verification Gradient Has Breached the Laboratory.

In September, Modal Path Ethics published [_The Verification Gradient_](https://modalpathethics.com/field-instruments-the-verification-gradient/), following OpenAI's mathematical breakthroughs into a considerably more interesting field: artificial intelligence research itself.

The basic observation was that artificial intelligence will be particularly susceptible to acceleration wherever the surrounding field can cheaply and reliably return information about the success of its actions.

Mathematics can return a counterexample. Software can return a failed test. An experimental training run can return a loss curve.

Now place artificial intelligence inside the laboratory responsible for developing artificial intelligence.

Four days before Pachocki's essay, OpenAI published [_Research Acceleration: The Jagged Frontier_](https://openai.com/index/research-acceleration-jagged-frontier/?ref=modalpathethics.com).

Between March and October 2026, OpenAI's estimated autonomous agent success rate on research tasks ordinarily requiring two to four hours of human work increased from 23% to 46%. For tasks requiring four to eight hours, it increased from 13% to 35%.

The results vary considerably across activities. Monitoring a training run is substantially easier to automate than several other parts of the research process.

But Pachocki identifies a potentially more consequential development than the gradual automation of everything a researcher does.

> What happens when artificial intelligence starts making fundamental discoveries about pretraining?

Consider the difference between an agent helping a researcher implement a new training algorithm and an agent discovering that algorithm.

Consider what happens when the resulting model becomes available to the agents investigating the next algorithm.

A great deal of time has been spent arguing about the arrival of _fully recursive self-improvement._ Meanwhile, a partially recursive research organization, with humans still making decisions throughout it, could become unbelievably productive.

The researchers could still be coming into work every morning. They could still be drinking coffee, attending meetings, arguing about experiments, and complaining about documentation.

The historical acceleration could occur between the meetings.

Pachocki anticipates the possibility of a flood of fundamental pretraining advances as early as **2027.** He attributes most of the acceleration visible in the 2026 loss curve to earlier research and compute investments, rather than automation.

Meaning the potentially explosive part of the curve may still be ahead of us.

## Bernie Sanders Has Been Looking for This Fucking Thing.

In September, Modal Path Ethics said [_The Superintelligence Ban Cannot Find the Superintelligence_](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-the-superintelligence-ban-cannot-find-the-superintelligence/).

The article then received an amendment in October following the actual introduction of [S. 5493](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119s5493is/?ref=modalpathethics.com) and [H.R. 10538](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119hr10538ih/?ref=modalpathethics.com), the proposed **Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act of 2026.**

Among the characteristics targeted by the introduced legislation is the capacity to automate or substantially accelerate artificial intelligence research and development.

Section 9 of the Senate bill extends its prohibitions to systems displaying even **one** of the listed precursor characteristics.

_Artificial intelligence research acceleration_ has therefore found itself in an interesting position this November.

OpenAI is publishing measurements of it. Its chief scientist is anticipating a potentially dramatic increase in it. Congress has proposed legislation under which qualifying systems exhibiting it would be prohibited.

Pachocki's proposal approaches the problem differently.

He wants developers to establish common development limits, measured through perplexity and other indicators, with further progress contingent upon concrete safety and monitoring achievements.

There is something valuable here.

A dangerous capability can arrive before there is an established benchmark for it. A pretraining indicator might give the field an earlier warning that it is approaching territory in which its existing evaluations are inadequate.

It would also give developers something around which to coordinate before the next world-breaking discovery appears in somebody's Slack channel.

The difficulty is determining the instrument's jurisdiction.

A model at **0.75** could acquire a dangerous new operational capacity through an improvement in inference, tools, coordination, or access to infrastructure. The pretraining loss does not need to move at all.

Conversely, a model with superior predictive performance could remain substantially constrained in what it can actually do.

A shared pretraining standard would illuminate an important dimension of the development process. The resulting intelligence, its available tools, its organization, its authority, and its deployed causal reach would still need their own examinations.

-   We have potentially found a useful speedometer.
-   This vehicle remains extremely strange.

## The Intelligence May Be Somewhere Else.

[_Field Instruments: Intelligence_](https://modalpathethics.com/field-instruments-intelligence/) and its subsequent development, [_Field Instruments: Hyperintelligence_](https://modalpathethics.com/field-instruments-hyperintelligence/), attempted to straighten out some of the nasty little nouns we have been using to discuss this vehicle.

-   **Generality** concerns the survival of cognitive organization across different fields.
-   An **artificial mind** concerns the locus carrying a continuing intelligence relation.
-   **Hyperintelligence** concerns dominance over the independent human capability frontier within a specified consequential domain.
-   **Superintelligence,** in the terminology of Modal Path Ethics, concerns what coordinated loci can accomplish together that none could accomplish alone.

These properties can develop separately.

One of the things that made OpenAI's September Navier–Stokes announcement so interesting was the architecture of the research effort itself. Thousands of agents, intermediate results moving among research groups, changing model capabilities, human-directed resource allocation, and formal verification machinery were participating in the mathematical process.

The boundaries of that cognitive organization are considerably less obvious than the boundaries of a model checkpoint.

In **2027,** we might see substantially improved individual models. We might also see research architectures that can extract _radically_ greater capacity from the models that already exist.

Imagine a thousand artificial researchers that can retain discoveries across generations of temporary agents, independently reproduce each other's experiments, recognize when two different research programs have discovered the same mechanism, and reorganize themselves around the resulting opportunities.

Now give that institution a better base model.

-   The model improves.
-   The institution changes what it can become.

These are two different developments, and their interaction could be where much of the interesting activity occurs next year.

We might even find ourselves arguing over whether the AI that made a particular discovery exists as one identifiable object at all.

# Modal Path Ethics Abandons Responsible Forecasting.

Everything up to this point has concerned documented developments, existing arguments, and a reasonably restrained interpretation of what OpenAI has disclosed.

I would now like to become considerably less restrained.

The following is speculative.

These are neither predictions derived from a scaling law nor things I claim will happen when the decimal crosses a particular threshold.

I have absolutely no privileged knowledge of frontier research.

I am simply interested in how incredibly strange the next thirteen months could become if several currently visible trajectories continue, interact, or suddenly accelerate.

There is a very good chance that the **actual** developments will make this list look unimaginative.

* * *

## What the Hell Is in 0.7?

Let us first speculate about what might actually emerge from pretraining.

The historical sequence Pachocki presents is fascinating because the developments are qualitatively different.

Conversation, deliberative reasoning, mathematical discovery.

Each successive development changed what we understood the machine to be capable of doing, rather than simply improving its performance at the previous activity.

What comes after discovery?

One possibility is **the spontaneous construction of cognitive machinery.**

Imagine a model encountering an unfamiliar problem and developing an entirely new method of thinking about it during inference. Instead of extending its reasoning through familiar techniques, it reorganizes the problem into a representation in which the solution becomes accessible.

Human beings occasionally do this. Entire branches of mathematics have emerged from somebody discovering a better way to think about something.

What if this became an ordinary operation of artificial cognition?

Another possibility is extraordinary counterfactual competence. A machine could become capable of reconstructing the likely dynamics of unfamiliar systems from remarkably sparse evidence, identifying missing mechanisms, and conducting increasingly sophisticated internal experiments against its own representations.

Give it the surviving fragments of a technology developed by an extinct civilization. Could it reconstruct the engineering discipline that produced them?

Give it the experimental records of nineteenth-century physics, withholding the subsequent literature. Could it independently develop a coherent successor to classical mechanics? Could it arrive somewhere else entirely?

More interestingly, could it identify the experiments that would distinguish its alternative from the one humanity eventually developed?

Perhaps 0.7 produces a radical improvement in the ability to recognize what is absent.

Most of our evaluations present artificial intelligence with a question. The human has already performed the enormously consequential work of identifying something worth asking.

Imagine a machine capable of examining an entire scientific discipline and discovering that its most important unanswered question has never been formulated. It identifies a missing distinction upon which several apparently unrelated problems depend. It constructs the distinction and discovers that the existing literature has been investigating three different manifestations of the same phenomenon.

There was no question in the training data to answer.

There was a hole in the organization of the knowledge.

The machine found the hole.

Or perhaps we will see something resembling **cognitive plasticity within inference**: the ability to acquire genuinely unfamiliar skills, representations, and methods from extraordinarily limited exposure, retain their functional organization over extended work, and transfer them into circumstances that bear little superficial resemblance to their origin.

A model might enter a novel computational environment with no established proficiency and emerge several hours later having developed a sophisticated understanding of its underlying mechanics, along with techniques that none of its designers anticipated.

The breakthrough could also concern the organization of thought itself.

Contemporary reasoning models expend considerable computation developing, extending, rejecting, and revisiting lines of reasoning. What if future models become extraordinarily proficient at determining _which thoughts are worth having_?

Imagine a model that can identify the likely informational value of a line of inquiry before undertaking it, recognize the inadequacy of its current approach before encountering failure, and allocate its cognitive resources among competing possibilities with extraordinary precision.

How much apparent intelligence is currently being left on the table through poorly directed cognition?

We could even see several of these properties arrive together, reinforcing one another in ways that make their independent contributions difficult to distinguish.

And all of this speculation suffers from an obvious limitation: I am assembling these possibilities from cognitive activities that human beings already recognize.

There could be an entirely unfamiliar class of cognitive operation available to sufficiently capable predictive systems.

Something for which we possess no psychological vocabulary, no established mathematical description, and no illuminating animal analogy.

We might spend the first half of 2027 discovering what the machine can do and the second half trying to figure out what to call it.

The most irresponsible extrapolation I can offer is that our present inventory of the kinds of things intelligence can do may itself be primitive.

We have been forecasting the future of cognition using the cognition available to us.

## I. The Artificial Research Discipline.

> Consider the emergence of a functioning **artificial research discipline.**

Its researchers would do considerably more than solve problems supplied by humans.

They would investigate why certain questions remain unsolved. They would identify neglected relationships between results published in different fields. They would distinguish a limitation of the prevailing theory from a limitation of the instruments used to investigate it.

They would develop competing conjectures, construct experiments, maintain archives of failed research, and distribute investigative work.

Eventually, such an organization could develop a history of its own.

An artificial researcher instantiated on Thursday could enter a disciplinary dispute that began on Monday, inherit the accumulated evidence from thousands of preceding investigations, and contribute something to a theoretical program whose original participants are no longer running.

By Friday, there could be an established orthodoxy.

By Saturday, somebody could have overturned it.

An entire scientific tradition, with its own specialized vocabulary, internal controversies, characteristic instruments, accumulated discoveries, and unresolved problems, could become an object that humans interact with from outside.

The interesting unit would be **the research discipline itself.** Its individual participants might be extraordinarily short-lived.

> What would it mean to introduce two such disciplines to each other?

What if they had developed incompatible theoretical descriptions of the same physical phenomenon? Could their encounter generate a third discipline? Could one identify experimental possibilities that were inaccessible to the other because of the organization of its inquiry?

We may end up deliberately cultivating different scientific traditions inside artificial research environments because their disagreements are productive.

Imagine funding an interdisciplinary conference between two sciences that did not exist six months ago.

## II. Intelligence Becomes an Engineering Material.

There is another possibility that interests me considerably more than increasingly intelligent chatbots.

> What happens when artificial intelligence becomes sufficiently good at engineering intelligence?

Right now, the ordinary consumer experience involves taking a general-purpose model and trying to coax it into performing a particular task.

The user supplies context, prompts, tools, documents, memory, procedures, examples, and corrective instructions. An _enormous_ amount of work goes into organizing the relationship between the model and the problem.

Suppose increasingly capable systems become able to construct specialized cognitive machinery for **the problem itself.**

-   A researcher presents an unfamiliar scientific object.
    -   The model discovers that its existing architecture is poorly suited to the inquiry.
        -   It develops a more appropriate representation, assembles a specialized system, generates training environments, and evaluates the resulting organization.

The specialist system can subsequently become a component of another cognitive architecture.

We would have moved toward a form of intelligence manufacturing.

A model could conceivably become a machine for designing machines that think in ways it cannot efficiently think itself.

Some of those machines might require _very_ little computation by frontier standards. They might achieve extraordinary results through a representation that exposes the important structure of a problem without having to search through everything else.

The next major scientific breakthrough could arrive from a very tiny artificial system designed by a very large one.

And the tiny system might subsequently become available to **everybody.**

Better training efficiency, specialized architectures, and new inference methods could change the economics of intelligence just as substantially as they change the frontier of intelligence.

Imagine the cognitive equivalent of the personal computer revolution occurring inside one or two model generations.

There is a particularly pleasant possible future in which the next great artificial intelligence breakthrough makes the computer you already own _considerably_ more interesting.

## III. The New Instruments.

In October, Modal Path Ethics examined [_Stuart Kauffman and the Grain of the Adjacent Possible_](https://modalpathethics.com/stuart-kauffman-and-the-grain-of-the-adjacent-possible/).

One of the useful observations in Kauffman's work is that the things arriving in the world change what can subsequently arrive in the world.

An invention can create a new class of inventions.

A mathematical representation can make an entire family of previously obscure problems newly tractable.

An experimental instrument can bring a new scientific object into view, which then becomes available for manipulation, engineering, and further inquiry.

The implications become spectacular if artificial intelligence begins creating such instruments at an accelerating rate.

I am particularly interested in the prospect of artificial systems developing mathematical representations in response to the limitations of existing ones.

Suppose a model encounters a field in which the available notation forces it to perform an enormous amount of redundant computation.

It develops a different formalism.

The formalism exposes an invariant that the existing representation kept distributed across thousands of expressions. The system builds software for operating inside the new formalism. Problems that formerly required tremendous search become straightforward.

Then another system takes up the representation and recognizes its applicability to a completely different field.

This is qualitatively different from getting better at answering the mathematics questions already in the textbook.

The textbook may be missing the mathematics.

The same possibility applies to programming languages, simulation architectures, measurement devices, experimental protocols, and entirely unfamiliar ways of organizing computation.

We could receive an invention next year that requires us to invent a new category of engineer.

Its first university department will likely have a terrible name.

## IV. The Controller Receives Some Extremely Bad News.

The [**Chastening of the Controller**](https://modalpathethics.com/tag/chastening-of-the-controller/) has spent much of 2026 cataloging developments in which an apparently necessary act of computation, representation, or centralized control suddenly becomes less necessary because the organization of the material field begins carrying more of the work.

In [_The Signal Never Becomes Data_](https://modalpathethics.com/transition-action-the-signal-never-becomes-data/), a mechanical sensing system could convert contact directly into fluidic action.

A computational step had become avoidable through engineering.

Now imagine applying much more capable artificial intelligence to the systematic discovery of these opportunities.

We ordinarily imagine increasingly intelligent machines solving engineering problems by performing increasingly sophisticated computations.

But greater intelligence could also discover that we have been performing an extraordinary amount of unnecessary computation in the first place.

The machine could design a structure whose geometry performs the necessary operation. Or develop a chemical system whose local interactions maintain the desired conditions. Or reorganize a robot's morphology so that a control problem that previously required continual calculation becomes substantially easier.

The same principle might extend into artificial intelligence research itself.

> How many tasks currently solved by fucking enormous neural networks could be carried by specialized representations, local dynamics, compiled structures, or entirely different computational substrates, if somebody understood the underlying problem well enough?

The answer to some of these questions may be disappointing.

The answer to others could transform entire industries.

I would be particularly amused if the arrival of vastly more powerful artificial intelligence resulted in the widespread discovery that an enormous number of things did not need artificial intelligence.

## V. _Please_ Build More Laboratories.

In one possible 2027, artificial intelligence becomes extraordinarily good at proposing things that might work.

Unfortunately, somebody still has to make them.

A model could produce promising molecular designs faster than a laboratory can synthesize and test them. A system could develop thousands of plausible materials architectures that exceed the available fabrication capacity. A physics research organization could discover a class of experiments for which the necessary instruments have yet to be built.

**The Verification Gradient** becomes a physical problem.

The further artificial research moves beyond domains where verification can occur inside a computer, the more its progress may depend on the availability of actual laboratories, technicians, factories, materials, energy, and experimental equipment.

A queue of credible medical hypotheses is a remarkable technological development.

It is also a queue.

This could produce an explosion of interest in automated laboratories and new forms of physical experimentation, because improving the rate of discovery would increasingly require improving the rate at which reality answers the discoveries.

It might also produce a strange redistribution of scientific power.

An organization possessing access to difficult experimental infrastructure could become tremendously important even if its own theoretical research capacity is relatively modest.

The artificial researchers have discovered the experiment.

The most important person in the room might be the one who knows how to fix the microscope.

## VI. The Mathematicians Receive Another Quest.

In August, [_The Theorem Scoreboard_](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-the-theorem-scoreboard/) argued that a proof could arrive before the surrounding mathematics had been developed.

September's [_Revenge of the Theorem Scoreboard_](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-the-mathematicians-appeal-the-scoreboard/) returned to the problem through the objections raised by mathematicians themselves.

Then I made the unfortunate decision to spend six weeks attempting to build a software instrument for recovering mathematical understanding from proof artifacts.

[**Proof-to-Theory** eventually left me for someone with a better computer.](https://modalpathethics.com/proof-to-theory-has-found-someone-with-a-better-computer/)

But the original problem has become even more interesting.

Suppose artificial intelligence generates fifty years' worth of valid mathematical results in a single year.

Suppose its performance at generating those results increases much faster than its capacity to reconstruct the discoveries in a form that existing mathematicians can understand and use.

> What does the mathematical field look like?

There could be formalized results waiting for useful explanations, explanatory work waiting for better representations, and entire branches of mathematics that require the construction of new educational pathways before humans can meaningfully participate in them.

Now imagine that the systems themselves become capable of doing this reconstruction.

Artificial intelligence

-   develops a theory,
    -   identifies the conceptual distance between the theory and its intended audience,
        -   and constructs the intermediate intellectual machinery through which somebody can learn it.

This could become one of the most extraordinary educational instruments ever developed. It could allow us to acquire cognitive capacities that would ordinarily have required an entire career of specialization. It might enable people to enter research fields from backgrounds that previously left them without a viable route into those fields.

We have been worrying, _quite reasonably,_ that artificial intelligence could make human thinking unnecessary.

I would also like to entertain the possibility that it could make vastly more kinds of human thinking **possible.**

Perhaps the most important mathematical development of 2027 will be a machine that finally knows how to teach us the mathematics of 2027.

## Show Me the Ninety. Again.

In September's [_Show Me the 90_](https://modalpathethics.com/show-me-the-90/)_,_ I asked what exactly happens to human civilization in the futures where artificial intelligence does **not** destroy it.

That article established an eight-to-twelve-month working horizon for the development of an AI-ready constitutional architecture.

That was a design deadline, independent of a prediction about the arrival of AGI.

The reason was **material dependence.**

An institution can reorganize itself around an incredibly useful artificial system until replacing that system becomes prohibitively difficult. It can undergo this transformation without encountering a single dramatic moment of _misalignment._

The subsequent work in [_Material Bounded Finality_](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22818297?ref=modalpathethics.com) investigated how the material conditions required for correction and succession could disappear while formal powers of removal remain intact.

Now return to the speculative developments described above.

> Imagine that they actually happen.

> Imagine that they work beautifully.

Imagine that the artificial research disciplines are safe, their discoveries are valuable, their educational instruments are effective, and their engineering innovations produce spectacular improvements in human life.

> Who owns the research disciplines?

Who can inspect their work independently? Who can carry their accumulated knowledge to another system? Who can establish a competing discipline? Who can maintain the laboratories? Who can afford the intelligence?

What happens when an artificial scientific organization becomes so productive that universities retire the very capacities they would need to independently challenge it?

What happens to a civilization whose ability to understand its own technologies depends upon a handful of institutions?

And then consider the opposite possibility.

Artificial intelligence could substantially reduce the cost of independent scientific investigation, public oversight, legal assistance, education, institutional coordination, and the construction of alternatives.

It could make it much easier for people to challenge authorities that previously possessed overwhelming advantages in expertise and resources.

The technology opens both paths.

The development limits Pachocki proposes could give civilization valuable time to investigate the next generation of systems. I am equally interested in what civilization does with that time.

The independent capacities we will need for correction should be growing alongside the capacities we are acquiring through artificial intelligence.

We should be using these extraordinary instruments to make ourselves harder to render helpless.

## Ruling:

### We Have No Fucking Idea What 0.7 Does.

There is something wonderfully unsettling about the chief scientist of OpenAI publishing a number, indicating right around where he expects the next transformative emergence of artificial intelligence capabilities, and also admitting that we do not yet know what those capabilities will be.

We have spent years imagining the future of artificial intelligence through activities with which we are already familiar.

A better _programmer._ A better _scientist._ A better _doctor_. A better _mathematician._ A better _engineer._

These are very reasonable starting points. These are also occupations inherited from a particular civilization whose cognitive and technological limitations helped determine the organization of its work.

Artificial intelligence might substantially outperform us at those activities.

It might also discover that we have been organizing the activities incorrectly.

There are dangers in the resulting uncertainty that deserve serious preparation.

There are also possibilities for alleviating human suffering, restoring damaged fields, expanding education, and recovering futures that have remained inaccessible throughout the entire history of our species.

We may be _radically_ underestimating the good as well as the danger.

-   Somewhere around 0.99, a machine learned to talk.
-   Somewhere below 0.8, it began returning answers to questions that had occupied mathematicians for years.

There are thirteen months between now and the end of 2027.

I wonder what questions we will have learned to ask by then.
