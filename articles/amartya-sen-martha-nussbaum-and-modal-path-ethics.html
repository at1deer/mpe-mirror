Amartya Sen, Martha Nussbaum, and Modal Path Ethics

Published 2026-11-09 · Updated 2026-11-09 · Aidan Lawson
Tags: Engagement · Modal Path Ethics · Locushood
Mirror generated 2026-11-13T07:09:15.800Z

Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum were already in the original book. They appeared in the objection asking whether Modal Path Ethics was the capability approach under another vocabulary.

It was a reasonable question then. It is a better question now.

  • Both approaches refuse to confuse a permission with a usable freedom.
  • Both investigate what people can actually do under the conditions in which they live.
  • Both can recognize deprivation beneath apparent prosperity, dependence beneath apparent choice, and serious damage before a life visibly collapses.

Those similarities deserve more than a ceremonial acknowledgment before I announce that my terminology is deeper.

They also do not establish that the theories make the same claim.

The question today is whether the capability approach already supplies the constitutive account of harm Modal Path Ethics has been defending: an actual, qualifying impairment of an extant bearer’s organized continuability is pro tanto harm to that bearer, including where its significance does not depend on a separate person’s deprivation or an unrealized future benefit.

What Sen Established.

Sen’s starting distinction is between what a person achieves and what that person genuinely has the opportunity to achieve. Functionings concern doings and states of being; capability concerns the real freedom to achieve relevant combinations of them. Resources alone do not tell us that. People can possess comparable means while differing greatly in what those means enable.

Consider an educational opportunity.

Admission, tuition assistance, transport, safety, time, and the ability to leave other obligations do different work. A university’s willingness to enroll someone does not establish that the person can attend.

Modal Path Ethics recognizes this immediately. It has no claim to have invented this problem.

Sen’s account also reaches beyond personal welfare. His distinction between well-being and agency allows a person’s purposes to include commitments whose achievement is not equivalent to that person’s comfort or advantage. Nor does his moral analysis require every conflict to resolve into a complete numerical ranking. Plural information and incomplete comparison are already part of the machinery.

An easy comparison would give Sen a static checklist and then defeat it with history, relationships, and uncertainty. This would be a victory over the checklist’s author.

Sen also resists an authoritative, permanently fixed list of capabilities suitable for every purpose. Selection and weighting require public reasoning responsive to the inquiry being undertaken. His objection is to closing that work in advance, rather than to identifying important capabilities at all.

There is substantial agreement here. An evaluative instrument should disclose what it attends to, what it excludes, and why those distinctions deserve authority in this case.

The disagreement will have to survive a capability account that is already plural, materially serious, and open to correction.

What Nussbaum Defends.

Nussbaum develops a more substantive political proposal. A just society must secure a threshold of central capabilities for each person. Her account connects dignity to concrete opportunities for bodily life, thought, affiliation, practical reason, political participation, and other central activities. Rights require material and institutional arrangements through which they can be exercised.

The political goal is capability rather than compulsory performance. Securing an opportunity does not ordinarily require forcing its recipient to use it. Nussbaum’s distinction between internal and combined capabilities also prevents bodily or psychological readiness from being mistaken for actual freedom: the surrounding conditions must support exercise.

Her account of animal justice extends concern beyond humanity. It attends to sentient animals’ characteristic activities and strivings, with room for individual variation. Human resemblance is not the measure of every animal’s life.

So the contrast cannot be that Nussbaum has bodies while Modal Path Ethics has environments. She definitely has environments. It also cannot be that she recognizes only humans. She does not.

The wider capability literature makes the overlap even stronger. Jonathan Wolff and Avner de-Shalit examine secure functionings and the way disadvantages cluster and propagate. Their distinctions between corrosive disadvantages and fertile functionings identify losses that damage other areas of life and capacities that help sustain them.

David Schlosberg develops capability-oriented ecological justice extending to animals, communities, and natural systems.

So any claim that capability theory is incapable of noticing insecurity, enabling relations, or nonhuman organization has selected its opponent quite badly.

The older book’s discussion needs that qualification. Resistance remains a substantive category within Modal Path Ethics. Its defense does not depend on everybody else having overlooked the difficulty of getting somewhere.

The Difference: What Constitutes Harm.

The strongest comparison begins after those agreements.

  • A capability account identifies morally important opportunities through the relevant account of advantage, agency, dignity, flourishing, or justice.
  • Modal Path Ethics investigates a different relation: what an actual transition does to the organized continuation of an existing bearer.
    • The Continuance Principle states why a qualifying degradation of that relation constitutes pro tanto harm.

This changes the ground of attribution. It is not an additional item placed at the bottom of Nussbaum’s list.

The framework does not begin by selecting an independently approved future and then treating its nonachievement as the injury. Nor does it begin with every conceivable future and count the missing ones.

It identifies an actual organization, the capacities and relations through which that organization continues, and what an actual transition has impaired.

The September and October work already made this separation explicit. Bergson’s engagement removed a pre-existing possibility inventory from the foundational ontology. Kauffman’s engagement distinguished present enablement from a predetermined future product. Nemichandra’s engagement separated presently exercised activity from the capacities and relations that sustain and renew it.

A newspaper can produce an issue while losing the means of maintaining publication. An institution can execute today’s tasks while its succession and repair capacities deteriorate. A person can complete an activity through a route that has become substantially more dangerous or burdensome.

None of these observations defeats capability theory. Many can be expressed in its vocabulary.

But

  • being expressible in another vocabulary

is not yet

  • being constituted by that vocabulary’s normative ground.

The more demanding question is whether the organizational impairment itself counts as harm to its bearer, or whether its moral significance must first be established through an additional loss borne by a person or other independently admitted subject.

That is where the recent newspaper argument becomes useful.

Van Inwagen Has Already Inspected the Newspaper.

Before discussing institutional injury, we need to know what the institutional noun commits us to.

Peter van Inwagen’s Material Beings asks when material things compose a further material object. His restrictive answer does not admit ordinary nonliving composites simply because their constituents are attached or arranged together.

The Modal Path Ethics engagement with him made an important distinction: material composition and organized continuation are different explanatory questions.

A newspaper’s editorial organization does not need to be an additional material body hovering above its journalists, records, equipment, and practical relations. Those things can constitute its entire realization. The investigation concerns whether an actual, historically integrated organization is operating through them.

That question has evidence available to it.

  • Does coordinated work persist through changes in personnel and equipment?
  • Do decisions, records, resources, and distribution participate in a characteristic process?
  • What happens when the coordination fails while many of its constituents remain?
  • Which dependencies and vulnerabilities belong to the joined activity rather than to a list of people considered separately?

These are the locus diagnostic’s existing demands. Hoel helped sharpen causal scale; Simondon made the organization’s formation part of the inquiry; Patañjali required the grain to earn its place before the desired verdict arrived. Van Inwagen then prevented that diagnostic from being mistaken for a universal answer to material composition.

Now take the strongest reductionist response. Describe every constituent and every relevant relation in sufficient detail. Preserve the actual history. Preserve the coordination.

Nothing additional needs to appear.

But the successful description has retained precisely the organization whose impairment we were investigating. It has explained how that organization is realized. It has not demonstrated that its impairment is fictitious.

Nonredundancy does not mean that no sufficiently complete finer description could contain the facts. It means that treating the other loci separately fails to preserve the relevant organized continuation. Restoring their relations to the explanation can restore the institutional fact without adding another substance.

There is a second distinction. An account of people acting together is not automatically an account of damage reducible to their individual suffering. The first describes how an organization exists. The second makes a claim about whose loss exhausts its injury.

A coordinated activity can be impaired through relations among persons without that impairment being identical to a sum of their distress, lost income, or frustrated preferences.

Kit Fine’s separate warning remains intact: evidence of organizational continuation does not settle every claim of numerical identity through replacement, reproduction, or reconstitution. Where that stronger identity matters, it needs its own support. The newspaper does not acquire immortality because the masthead survived.

These defenses are already part of Modal Path Ethics. Institutional harm does not begin today with an unexplained corporate soul.

What the Paper Route Actually Demonstrated.

Modal Path Ethics Gets a Paper Route examines the Moscow publishing organization of Novaya Gazeta. Its argument isolates the impairment of operative support for coordinated editorial activity, while keeping the surviving team, records, and remaining work in view.

The important restriction concerns what establishes the institutional claim.

The argument does not add up the journalists’ injuries until the total becomes a newspaper. It does not imagine a more fortunate history in which everybody is compensated. And it does not value a collection of investigations the newspaper might someday have published.

It follows the existing operation through an actual sequence.

  • A practical support relation participated in maintaining the work.
    • That relation became unavailable.
      • Reported substitution attempts did not establish an operative replacement.
      • Continued activity demonstrated what remained; it did not establish that the impaired relation had been restored.

The loss belongs to the organization in a particular respect: its capacity to sustain its coordinated publishing activity.

That is a narrower claim than saying the entire newspaper has disappeared. It is also a stronger claim than saying somebody dislikes its prospects.

The support relation mattered constitutively. It was part of how this organization maintained its activity. We do not need tomorrow’s article to authorize recognition of today’s impairment.

The same point addresses the weighting regress. A generator does not have to acquire all its significance from a product, which acquires its significance from a beneficiary, whose significance depends on another valuable activity somewhere farther downstream.

At some point the inquiry must remain with what was actually damaged.

The article states the descriptive relation:

Generative contraction is an actual, nontrivial impairment of the relations through which an extant locus sustains, exercises, or renews a structurally significant capacity of its own.

Then it states the normative claim:

Such an impairment constitutes pro tanto harm to that locus in the affected respect. Its attribution does not require a future product, an alternative unharmed history, or a separate injury to another bearer.

This does not make capacity nonmodal. A capacity still concerns what an organization can do. It removes the requirement that an imagined outcome or an alternative complete history supply the object and measure of the injury. Causal inquiry may still use counterfactual tests; that does not make an uninstantiated comparison bearer the recipient of the harm.

Now return to Nussbaum.

In “Women’s Capabilities and Social Justice,” she explicitly places the political goal with each person’s capabilities. Groups and institutions can deserve support because of what they do for people. The insistence protects individuals from disappearing beneath the supposed good of a family, community, or state.

Modal Path Ethics preserves that protection. It denies that it requires making every institutional harm derivative of a personal harm.

  • The newspaper’s coordinated activity can have been injured.
  • Its participants can also have been injured.

Those attributions need not compete for the status of the one real event.

Nor do they justify adding the same loss twice. Distinct bearers and distinct affected relations must be established; overlapping descriptions must remain recognizable as overlapping.

The organization does not have to be another person to be the bearer of an injury. Its injury does not make its participants less than persons.

The Normative Bridge Is Already Defended.

An obvious objection remains:

You have demonstrated an organizational impairment. Why give it moral significance?

Modal Path Ethics has already devoted the Hume–Moore, Korsgaard–Street, Mackie, and Floridi engagements to different versions of this question. The answer cannot be reduced to pointing at a causal diagram and announcing that ethics has surely occurred.

The Continuance Principle is a substantive claim about what constitutes harm. Its defense is explanatory and comparative. It identifies the bearer-relative structure through which bodily injury, coercion, deprivation, damaged reproduction, institutional impairment, and other cases become adverse changes to something that already exists. It then tests whether that account preserves the distinctions those cases require.

Several features do real justificatory work.

The account attaches adversity to the organization actually impaired. It distinguishes damage from failure to produce an additional benefit. It can recognize impairment before termination and through changes of material implementation. It identifies a determinate respect in which repair would have to work. And it retains an injury when later events benefit somebody else.

These are reasons for identifying the relation as morally significant, not consequences of defining a word conveniently.

The proposed extension beyond persons has the same burden. If a nonhuman or nonliving organization passes the bearer diagnostic, and a transition significantly impairs its own organized continuability, Modal Path Ethics treats the demonstrated adversity as a reason against that impairment in that respect. Sentience may establish additional morally important features. It does not, on this account, supply the only route by which an actual bearer can undergo morally relevant loss.

A person-centered or sentience-centered theory can reject that extension. But the disagreement is now about which demonstrated adversities count, and why. Showing that the organization is physically realized through other things does not decide it. Neither does observing that the proposed bearer cannot complain.

The defense also has exclusions. An alternative going unrealized is not automatically an injury. Ordinary development is not judged by resemblance to a frozen earlier condition. An incidental alteration is not equivalent to destruction of a constitutive capacity. A nominal loss can be offset within the affected capacity by a real substitute; an imaginary substitute cannot do that work.

These restrictions are important because greater coverage is not automatically a better theory. Calling every change harmful would explain very little. The account earns its scope by distinguishing qualified contraction from specification, transformation, inconsequential variation, and continuation carried through replacement.

That is why the weighting work matters without becoming a universal calculator. Constitutive relevance, depth, resistance, irreversibility, distribution, and related dimensions interrogate the character and significance of the actual impairment. They constrain its attribution and gravity. They do not manufacture the whole reason for concern by counting desirable future outputs.

The bridge remains contestable. It is not undefended.

The Harmful Organization Does Not Acquire a Veto.

Another familiar objection follows:

What about the organization whose continuation injures everyone around it?

The tumor, pathogen, coercive institution, and destructive network have already been through this argument. The Floridi and Kauffman engagements make the central discipline explicit: moral disapproval cannot determine whether causal organization exists.

If an organization qualifies as a locus, that fact must survive our judgment that its activity is disastrous. Conversely, qualifying as a locus cannot make its continuation the governing purpose of the field around it. The host’s continuation remains visible. So do the people subjected to an institution, the resources it takes, and the damage through which it maintains itself. Burden transfer is part of the transition’s structure.

Destroying a qualifying harmful organization can therefore register a bearer-relative harm while being justified or required. A pro tanto harm is an adverse consideration in a specified respect. It is not an absolute prohibition, an automatic right to restoration, or a warrant to force other loci to sustain the bearer.

This is not a safeguard newly borrowed from Nussbaum after the institutional claim became embarrassing. The Death of an Office already separates the continuation of a useful function from perpetual possession of that function by its incumbent carrier. The same discipline applies to the newspaper. It does not own its journalists, readers, or service providers. Its need for support cannot establish unlimited duties in everyone capable of providing it. A genuine harm can exist without deciding consent, obligation, jurisdiction, or the overall justice of termination.

There is also no automatic multiplication of moral weight as more grains become visible. An organizational injury and its personal consequences can be causally connected, partly overlapping, or distinct in different respects. The task is to preserve those relations, not to award a bonus harm every time the analyst finds another locus.

The account is demanding because it refuses two shortcuts: erasing a destructive bearer to simplify its removal, and preserving that bearer at whatever cost its continued existence demands.

Neither is necessary to reach a firm verdict.

Nussbaum Already Knows That Tragedy Leaves Something Behind.

Modal Path Ethics distinguishes the harm, the complete transition profile, and the eventual verdict. A justified decision does not retroactively remove the injuries through which it proceeded. Later repair can restore capacities without producing a history in which the damage never happened.

That is important, and not an exclusive discovery.

In “The Costs of Tragedy,” Nussbaum distinguishes deciding what to do from asking whether any available alternative avoids serious moral wrongdoing. The second question can expose defects in the social conditions that force such choices. A decision procedure can identify a course while obscuring the tragedy of having to take it.

Modal Path Ethics agrees that the least objectionable available choice need not close the moral account. Its category of Better operates precisely where a damaged field offers no clean Good.

The distinction here is scope and attribution. A harm need not involve wrongdoing, a responsible agent, or a tragic conflict between duties. A natural transition may damage a qualifying bearer. A fully justified protective act may contain an injury without thereby becoming an unjustified act.

The framework’s retained-wound argument follows what happened to the affected continuation. The successor’s achievement, historical understanding, or restored function does not answer that question by itself.

Nussbaum’s tragedy and Modal Path Ethics’ retained harm can therefore illuminate the same decision without becoming identical concepts.

The Non-Planet Is an Existing Organization.

NASA’s 2006 account of Spitzer observations in IC 1396 describes a potential planet-forming disk being stripped by a nearby massive star’s radiation and winds. The physical process removes material from an existing organized system.

The Modal Path Ethics claim does not introduce a future planet as a present victim. It does not infer the deaths of hypothetical civilizations. It examines the disk’s own organization and the impairment of a supported developmental capacity.

The locus and impairment still require evidence at a defensible grain. A mass of material does not qualify because someone hopes it becomes a planet. Nor does every loss of gas prove every planet-forming continuation has ended. The claim concerns whatever significant generative capacity the actual process can be shown to impair.

Van Inwagen’s distinction applies again. The account need not settle whether the disk is one additional composite material object in his ontology. It needs an actually instantiated, historically integrated continuation structure. The physical realization of that structure and the normative significance of its damage remain separately examinable.

Nussbaum’s animal account draws its working boundary through sentience and striving. Schlosberg’s ecological extension means we cannot attribute that same boundary to all capability-oriented thought. But an extension to this disk would still have to establish its bearer, the relevant adversity, and the normative grounds for counting the impairment.

The Continuance Principle supplies Modal Path Ethics’ answer. Its warrant is the defended structural account, applied to a qualifying organization. We do not need a being farther downstream to receive the injury on its behalf.

What Survives the Comparison.

The distinctive claim is now precise:

Qualifying impairment of an extant locus’s own organized continuability constitutes pro tanto harm to that locus.
The harm is grounded in the actual bearer–transition–continuation relation; its attribution need not be derived from a separate subject’s welfare or capabilities, a predetermined ideal of flourishing, an unrealized product, or an alternative complete history.

This is a general constitutive account of harm. It includes generative contraction: damage to the actual relations through which significant activity is sustained, exercised, or renewed.

Its bearer can be relationally constituted without becoming fictitious. Its grain can be nonredundant without introducing an extra physical substance. Its future can remain unspecifiable without making its present generative capacities unreal. Its injury can count without granting it preservation rights over everyone else.

Those are connected commitments, already developed across the recent corpus. Paper Route applies them to an institution whose impairment can be described through its actual history. It does not suddenly invent the distinction between harm and justification, or supply the framework with its first answer to the composition problem.

Could a sufficiently developed capability account express all of this?

Possibly. Capability vocabulary is not prohibited from expanding.

But a reduction would have to show how the relevant capability account’s own premises support the same constitutive attribution. Adding nonliving organizational bearers and asserting that significant impairment of their continuability is itself morally adverse is a substantive extension. Renaming the resulting relation a capability does not establish that the existing theory had already supplied its ground.

The converse discipline applies to Modal Path Ethics. A general harm thesis does not derive Nussbaum’s particular capabilities, their political thresholds, or the entirety of Sen’s account of freedom. Justice can require developing capacities and securing opportunities, including where the task cannot be exhausted by reversing an identified historical injury.

Neither approach becomes the other by changing its nouns.

A critic also needs no complete replacement theory to expose a failure in the Continuance Principle. Refutation and reduction have different burdens. The present comparison identifies a surviving theoretical difference; it does not establish historical priority over every possible predecessor.

The Ruling.

Sen and Nussbaum give Modal Path Ethics substantial agreement about genuine opportunity, material conditions, and the inadequacy of satisfaction or aggregate prosperity as exhaustive measures. Their work deserves to appear at full strength. So do the capability tradition’s accounts of secure functioning, ecological justice, and tragic choice.

The real Modal Path Ethics survives that encounter without retreating into “the same thing, plus space rocks.”

It offers a different ground for a particular moral fact: what constitutes harm to an actual bearer. Its account reaches the organization’s own impaired continuation without requiring the loss to acquire significance from somebody else’s eventual benefit. It keeps the normative premise explicit and gives reasons for it. It identifies bearers through historical and causal organization, allows the realization of that organization to be described at finer grains, and refuses to convert the resulting harm into a sovereign claim.

The newspaper’s injury does not need to become an imaginary journalist’s injury. The disk’s impairment does not need an unborn astronomer. The person’s loss does not disappear because the institution gained capacity. And an institution does not acquire ownership of the people who sustain it because we have finally understood how much its own continuation depends on them.

  • Capability theory asks what people and other admitted subjects are substantively able to do and be.
  • Modal Path Ethics asks what actual transitions have done to the organized continuations of the bearers involved, and defends qualifying impairment as morally adverse in its own respect.

Those inquiries meet repeatedly. They do not need to arrive at different practical recommendations to remain theoretically distinct.

The loss belongs to what was damaged. Recognizing that does not give the damaged thing the last word.
