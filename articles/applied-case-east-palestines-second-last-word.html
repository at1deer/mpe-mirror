Applied Case: East Palestine's Second Last Word

Published 2026-10-20 · Updated 2026-10-20 · Aidan Lawson
Tags: Inner Apocalypse · Modal Path Ethics
Mirror generated 2026-10-30T06:21:03.557Z

East Palestine has produced another last word.

  • The first took thirteen minutes.
  • The second came with a signature line.

In February 2023, East Palestine Fire Chief Keith Drabick authorized the vent-and-burn of five tank cars carrying vinyl chloride. The National Transportation Safety Board later found that the operation was unnecessary to prevent the feared chemical reaction, and that relevant dissenting expertise had failed to reach incident command.

Modal Path Ethics wrote about that in August. The chief's decision was final for the operation. It did not give him the final word about its justification. The investigation could contradict the information that had reached him, even though it could not put the chemicals back inside the cars.

Some of the residents who sought compensation now allege that the information reaching them was also incomplete.

On October 16, NewsNation reported that nearly 900 residents had sued lawyers involved in the $600 million Norfolk Southern settlement, along with physician Arch Carson, public-relations firms, and East Palestine Justice, an advocacy organization associated with Erin Brockovich. The residents allege that information about contamination was withheld or misrepresented while they were encouraged to accept payments in exchange for releasing future personal-injury claims.

These are allegations. The lawsuits are not findings that the lawyers, Carson, the firms, or the advocacy organization committed fraud or conspired to do so. NewsNation said those it contacted had not responded to its requests for comment.

If those allegations are supported, they would expose a second defective decision process in the same town.

  • An emergency commander authorized the release of chemicals.
  • Residents later signed releases of claims.

The question in both cases is what evidence was able to reach the person whose answer would become binding.

The Data Had Its Own Clock.

The settlement was announced in April 2024 and approved by the federal district court in September. It created a $600 million fund to be allocated among eligible households, businesses, and individuals, after fees and expenses.

There is an important point inside that agreement. The general class settlement did not automatically extinguish individual personal-injury claims. An eligible resident could seek an additional personal-injury payment, but doing so required a separate signed release. The release covered injuries that might be unknown at the time of signing.

The body has a different calendar from the claims administrator.

The new lawsuits center on the work of Stephen Petty, an industrial hygienist whose testing and analysis have become part of the dispute. According to NewsNation, Petty disputes optimistic assurances given to residents about long-term health risk. A June 2024 recording reported by the outlet characterizes the available contamination data as less severe than initially feared. In a video shown to residents, physician Arch Carson predicted that chemical exposure from the derailment would produce no long-term health effects in community members.

In August 2026, Petty told NewsNation that roughly 50 to 70 percent of the relevant dioxin or polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon data points he examined exceeded the cancer-screening thresholds he applied. He said further assessment was required.

An EPA screening-level exceedance is not a finding that a person will develop cancer, proof that a derailment caused a particular measurement, or an automatic cleanup order. It is a reason to keep investigating under appropriate exposure assumptions.

And the dates matter. In his September 11, 2026 presentation, Petty said raw measurements from one plaintiffs' sampling set were available in 2024 but processed in 2026. Other data sets received additional processing in 2026 as well. The completed 2026 calculations cannot simply be transported backward and treated as conclusions counsel possessed in 2024. Their later completion also does not tell us which underlying measurements, uncertainties, dissenting assessments, and limitations were known to counsel when residents signed.

That is the real evidentiary question.

  • What did the lawyers know?
    • What did they have good grounds to conclude?
  • What did residents receive?
And how much of the disagreement survived the trip from the expert's work to the person being asked to sign?

The Agreement Was Built to Close.

The April 26, 2024 settlement agreement says in Section XIII.C.3(e) that class counsel would work in good faith to encourage eligible residents to seek personal-injury payments and execute personal-injury releases.

That is an actual contractual provision. Encouraging people to accept an approved settlement is not, by itself, misconduct. These payments could provide badly needed money to people who had already endured displacement, fear, and uncertainty.

Nor does signing away a claim automatically count as harm. A resident could reasonably prefer compensation now over a lengthy lawsuit with uncertain proof and uncertain payment. Such a bargain can be a legitimate choice about the future. The question is whether the informational conditions for making it were intact.

But there is another clause.

Section XI.C gives Norfolk Southern a unilateral right to terminate the settlement if participation rates trigger thresholds set in a separate supplemental agreement. The public document says that supplemental agreement would not be filed with the court or ordinarily disclosed.

So the public agreement shows a structure in which obtaining participation mattered to the survival of the deal, while the decisive numerical conditions were kept elsewhere. It does not disclose the thresholds or prove that personal-injury opt-ins specifically controlled them.

That missing distinction cannot be supplied by outrage. It must be established.

Still, the arrangement creates a clear conflict worth examining. Counsel responsible for representing the class were committed by the settlement to encouraging personal-injury participation, while the broader deal contained participation-based termination terms. The exact effect of the confidential thresholds on the optional personal-injury releases remains to be established.

An attorney can navigate competing duties and incentives without misconduct. The structure is a reason to examine disclosure and consent, rather than a verdict about the attorneys. An informed choice requires that the person surrendering a claim understand what they are surrendering, including material uncertainties and evidence that could support a different decision.

The person who needs the settlement to work must not become the only reader of the evidence by which another person decides whether to enter it.

The Court Had Already Heard Some of This.

We do need to stop an easy story before it begins.

The warnings did not first emerge in October 2026. At the September 2024 fairness hearing, objectors raised Stephen Petty's position, contested Carson's assurances, and argued that residents lacked adequate information about future injury risk. Class counsel answered that Petty was one expert among others and that the data had been reviewed. The court heard the dispute.

In May 2026, Judge Benita Pearson denied motions to reopen the settlement judgment. She found that the movants had largely recycled objections already considered, had not shown fraud sufficient to justify relief under Rule 60, and had not demonstrated that competing expert opinions alone established misrepresentation. The court emphasized the extensive investigation, the value of timely compensation, and the residents' voluntary individual releases. Its opinion also noted that the settlement was reached before expert reports had been finalized and exchanged. That timing is part of the record, though the court rejected the inference of fraud advanced by the movants.

That is a substantial contrary ruling, and it certainly belongs in the account.

It does not adjudicate every allegation in the newly reported malpractice lawsuits. A challenge to a federal class settlement and a claim that lawyers violated duties in their dealings with clients are different legal questions. We should not announce the latter proved because the former failed, or announce it defeated because the former failed.

The judge's opinion also records a particularly important limit: the personal-injury releases were contracts between individual residents and Norfolk Southern. Alleged misconduct by the residents' own attorneys, without more, did not give the movants grounds to undo those contracts in the Rule 60 proceeding.

There is that second last word.

A resident may be able to pursue a lawyer while the release against the railroad remains binding.

That does not make correction impossible. It means correction may reach a different object from the one the resident wanted back.

The Lawyers' Clock.

Another part of the court record deserves attention.

The agreement allowed rapid payment of legal fees. In November 2025, the Sixth Circuit upheld most of the fee arrangements, while remanding a narrow allocation dispute. But Judge Amul Thapar wrote separately about the quick-pay provision, which placed $162 million in attorney fees and $18 million in expenses on an accelerated fourteen-day payment timetable while distributions to claimants depended on the later resolution of appeals.

Thapar warned that accelerated payment can distort incentives and undermine public confidence. His criticism concerned fee timing, incentive alignment, and oversight of allocations among law firms. It was not a finding that attorneys concealed contamination data from residents. His concurrence did not change the judgment.

The institutional detail is still remarkable.

  • The fee system had a clock measured in days.
  • The court process affecting residents' payments could continue for months or years.
  • The health consequences residents feared might not become knowable for decades.

Three clocks inside one settlement.

None of those clocks is inherently illegitimate. Legal teams deserve compensation for work performed. Residents need payments before litigation consumes the rest of their lives. Courts need judgments to settle disputes. Delay can itself be a serious harm.

But a settlement is supposed to allocate the cost of resolving the disaster. Its own architecture should not create a new information problem about who gets to close which future and who must live with the closure.

Which Correction Can Still Arrive?

In Bounded Last Words, the problem of finality was never whether someone should be allowed to make binding decisions. Without last words, emergencies become paralysis and litigation can become another form of attrition.

The question was which path the last word closes, and whether the authority behind it still remains reachable through evidence, review, remedy, and amendment.

East Palestine's first case concerned a fire chief who could authorize an irreversible operation without acquiring the authority to define the chemical facts afterward.

The second concerns a legal instrument that can settle claims, while the people using that instrument retain an interest in correcting how the settlement was obtained.

There is no reason to call the entire settlement apparatus sovereign. The residents are suing. Courts have heard objections. Independent experts are speaking publicly. Correction routes exist.

The narrower danger is that these routes do not necessarily reconnect to the original injury claim. Even a successful malpractice claim might provide damages for deficient representation without automatically reviving a released personal-injury claim against Norfolk Southern. A new scientific report might strengthen the case for monitoring or cleanup without reopening a settlement already made final. A finding that an earlier health assurance was too confident might change the public record while leaving a signed release intact.

Each correction matters. They are different remedies reaching different things.

The condition is structurally familiar: a right can remain formally legible after its material path has narrowed. What has to be tested now is the exact reach of the surviving remedies, rather than treating the existence of a lawsuit as proof that nothing was lost or treating the difficulty of suing as proof that everything was captured.

The allegations against the lawyers must be proved. The health evidence must be assessed with its dates, methods, baselines, and limits intact. The courts must distinguish their different tasks. And the residents must remain capable of making the material behind those tasks matter.

The Ruling.

In 2023, technical dissent did not reach the official who would authorize the vent-and-burn.

In 2024, residents signed releases whose consequences could last beyond the period in which long-term exposure effects could be assessed. Some residents now say they did so without evidence that should have been placed in front of them. A court has already rejected significant objections to the settlement. The new claims against those who helped arrange it remain allegations.

Those facts can all be true at once.

  • A settlement can be valuable and final.
  • A lawyer can deserve payment.
  • A court can have strong reasons to resist reopening a mass settlement.
    • A resident can still have a legitimate complaint about what was communicated before signing.

No one should have to pretend the first three settle the fourth.

The first East Palestine article ended with a bounded last word: the chief could decide the operation without owning the later account of its necessity.

Now the test has moved downstream.

The people who help close a case do not thereby acquire the last word about what the people they represent were entitled to know.

And if the evidence arrives after the release, there is one more question the existence of a lawsuit cannot answer by itself:

Can the correction still reach the future that was signed away?
