Applied Case: They Didn't Put the Newspaper in Prison

Published 2026-11-02 · Updated 2026-11-02 · Aidan Lawson
Tags: Applied Case · Security Instruments · Epistemic Instruments
Mirror generated 2026-11-12T20:21:41.629Z

On October 30, 2026, the Russian newspaper Novaya Gazeta published its own death notice.

The editors called it “We Have Been Killed.”

On November 9, they plan to switch off their servers, turn out the lights, and leave the Moscow newsroom they have occupied for nearly thirty years.

The portraits of dead colleagues are coming off the walls. The museum dedicated to murdered journalist Anna Politkovskaya, maintained in her former office, is closing. Books, archives, photographs, and decades of accumulated institutional memory are being packed for removal.

The newspaper has lost its legal registration, access to its principal website, its printing arrangements, and now its office and the donation-processing system through which readers financed its work.

Its editor-in-chief, Sergei Sokolov, says the remaining staff can no longer sustain the operation.

The newspaper's final issue is still to come.

Russia's Constitution, meanwhile, remains in excellent condition.

Article 29 guarantees freedom of mass information and explicitly prohibits censorship.

The constitutional provision has survived. The newspaper has not.

There are two particularly interesting details in the newspaper's announcement.

  • The first is that its editors attribute the closure to a coordinated campaign by Russian authorities and security services.
  • The second is how the final interruption occurred.

The newsroom was told to vacate its building. Banks and payment processors stopped supporting its fundraising infrastructure. When journalists asked why, the financial providers invoked internal corporate rules.

Sokolov believes those decisions were imposed from outside the banks. The precise command path has not been independently established. The operational result is documented: the newspaper lost the financial channel it depended upon and could not obtain a workable replacement.

The state did not have to send soldiers in to confiscate every article.

The newsroom could simply become impossible to maintain.

Twenty-seven years earlier, one of its most important journalists had written a book about what happens when a security apparatus enters the ordinary relationships of a society and begins determining their permitted futures.

He had even identified the security departments of banks and private firms as part of the problem.

His name was Yuri Shchekochikhin.

His book was called Slaves of the KGB.

They Didn't Put Him in Prison.

In Рабы ГБ. XX век. Религия предательства — Slaves of the KGB: The Twentieth Century, the Religion of Betrayal — Shchekochikhin recounts a visit from a young man who had something to confess.

It was the spring of 1992. Shchekochikhin was working in journalism, years before he joined Novaya Gazeta in 1996.

The visitor was preparing to leave Russia for Israel. He had telephoned the journalist late at night, asked for an urgent meeting, and appeared at the newspaper office the following morning.

He declined to sit down.

Five years earlier, he explained, he had informed on Shchekochikhin to the KGB.

The journalist remembered the incident.

He had given a lecture at the Gorky Film Studio about Soviet youth movements, particularly the informal associations developing outside the official Komsomol structure. Soviet young people were discovering ways to organize, dress, speak, and associate that the existing political apparatus had considerable difficulty understanding.

A young employee of the studio attended the lecture. He liked it.

Then he told his KGB handler about it.

The handler demanded a written account of what Shchekochikhin had said.

The young man resisted initially. According to his later confession, he was assured that the information was only for the security service and would have no consequences for the journalist.

He complied.

Shchekochikhin was subsequently summoned before Moscow Communist Party authorities and questioned about his remarks.

When the young informer discovered what had happened, he confronted his handlers.

They had promised him nothing would happen.

According to the account he gave Shchekochikhin, the officers laughed.

One answered:

“We didn't put him in prison.”

There is an entire theory of government in that answer.

  • The journalist had been observed, reported, entered into a disciplinary record, summoned, and warned.
  • The informer had been recruited through a relationship of confidence, induced to betray someone he had listened to, and discovered that the limits promised by his handler did not actually bind the security service.

Both men carried consequences.

The handlers had a different accounting system.

Nobody had been imprisoned. Therefore, the complaint was apparently absurd.

The prison door had become the institution's chosen threshold for recognizing its own harm.

Everything short of that threshold could disappear into the ordinary conduct of state security.

The confession is Shchekochikhin's recollection of what the young man told him, not an independently authenticated transcript of the KGB officers' conversation. But the distinction between the account and an official record is part of what makes the episode useful.

Shchekochikhin had received an institutional consequence.

He did not know the route by which it had arrived.

Only years later did someone outside the security apparatus supply him with a different understanding of the event.

The Protection Story.

The young man had not originally been recruited to report on journalists.

He was Jewish. So was the security officer who approached him.

The officer invoked Pamyat, the emerging ultranationalist movement whose antisemitism posed a real concern for Soviet Jews.

The proposed intelligence cooperation was presented as a way to protect Jewish youth from extremist provocation and possible foreign manipulation.

Pamyat was real. Its antisemitism was real. The protection story began with a recognizable danger.

But after recruitment, the original mission receded.

The handlers wanted information about professors, political jokes, foreign radio broadcasts, conversations, and social environments having little apparent connection to the threat used to justify the recruitment.

Eventually, an ordinary lecture became actionable information.

This is the crucial movement.

An intelligence service can have legitimate reasons to investigate violent extremism, espionage, organized criminal activity, or dangers to public safety.

Those functions may require informants, confidentiality, secrecy, and operational judgment.

A justified investigative function does not provide unlimited jurisdiction over every relationship accessible to an informant.

The young man entered a system whose actual demands extended well beyond the purpose presented to him. And when he objected to the consequences of one report, the handlers decided what counted as a consequence.

The protector had acquired authority over the meaning of protection.

The institution could determine when intelligence gathering was necessary, what information belonged in its files, where the information traveled, and whether its use had harmed anyone.

Its own restraint became evidence of its innocence.

Nobody had been put in prison.

This was apparently a successful outcome.

The Zone Survived the Soviet Union.

Shchekochikhin's book was not confined to the history of Soviet informers.

He wanted to understand the continuation of the practices that had made political informing possible.

His closing reflections contain a particularly disturbing observation.

In 1998, while investigating a St. Petersburg firm, Shchekochikhin says that he and his colleagues discovered a dossier compiled on him.

It described his habits, attachments, friends, vulnerabilities, and personal relationships. It also reportedly contained methods for influencing or compromising him, alongside a price list for journalists who could be paid to publish hostile articles about him.

This was no longer the old Soviet KGB gathering reports through one familiar political hierarchy. It was a privately held collection of information and instruments of pressure.

Shchekochikhin briefly wondered who among his acquaintances might have been involved. Who had sat with him? Who had visited his home? Who had supplied the information?

Then he decided he did not want to know.

Someone, he reasoned, must surely have refused.

In the book's final reflections, he observes that the old security system had fractured into many smaller structures, including security departments operating inside banks and commercial firms.

The institutional forms had changed.

The capacity to gather compromising information, construct dependencies, and use relationships against people had not necessarily disappeared with the Soviet state.

Shchekochikhin called the condition the Zone.

He borrowed the language of confinement to describe a society in which the boundaries of surveillance and coercion extend beyond prisons and official security buildings.

People can live ordinary lives inside such a field.

They can work, publish, form friendships, travel, marry, and argue.

The boundaries become visible when some activity touches the interests of the machinery that monitors them.

What had once seemed an available continuation acquires a hidden condition.

An informal conversation becomes a security report.

A professional relationship becomes a channel of personal information.

A journalist's reputation becomes a purchasable instrument.

The person can continue acting, but the field through which that action proceeds has changed.

Shchekochikhin was writing at the threshold of the twenty-first century.

In 2026, the newspaper he later helped lead says its payment providers can no longer process the donations that keep it alive.

The banks cite their internal rules.

The editors believe those rules carry the demands of state security.

The correspondence is striking, although it cannot establish that every financial refusal was directly ordered by the FSB.

The narrower fact matters enough.

A newspaper can possess readers willing to support it while losing the operative means by which that support can arrive.

The money remains imaginable. The donation remains voluntary. The financial route no longer works.

  • Shchekochikhin had been concerned with institutions gaining access to private relationships.
  • His newspaper's final crisis concerns the withdrawal of institutional support from a public relationship between a publication and its readers.

Both cases expose the power to determine which social continuations remain practically available.

The Slow Closing.

Novaya Gazeta was founded in 1993 with support from former Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev.

It became one of Russia's most prominent investigative newspapers, reporting on corruption, organized crime, the Chechen wars, abuses by officials, and other matters involving powerful institutions.

Shchekochikhin joined in 1996 and became deputy editor. His investigations pursued the relationships among organized crime, commercial interests, prosecutors, and security officials.

He died in July 2003 after a sudden illness.

His colleagues and family suspected poisoning. The official explanation involved a severe allergic reaction, and investigations did not establish a conclusive account of intentional poisoning. The circumstances remain disputed.

Anna Politkovskaya was murdered in 2006. Other journalists and contributors associated with the newspaper were also killed.

Novaya Gazeta retained their photographs, reporting, memory, and institutional history. It continued publishing.

Then came the full-scale Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.

The Russian government introduced new restrictions on reporting about the armed forces. Independent outlets faced administrative and criminal penalties for material authorities considered false or discrediting.

On March 8, 2022, the European Court of Human Rights issued an interim measure calling upon Russia to refrain from blocking or terminating Novaya Gazeta's activities.

On March 28, following warnings from the Russian media regulator Roskomnadzor, the newspaper suspended publication. Its print registration was subsequently revoked, and its online operations faced blocking and further legal restrictions.

The organization did not disappear in 2022.

Journalists established Novaya Gazeta Europe in exile. Staff remaining in Russia continued forms of reporting and publication under increasingly restrictive conditions.

The paper still had people, readers, editorial competence, professional memory, and stories worth investigating.

The organization was adapting to the damage.

Then, on February 11, 2025, the European Court of Human Rights delivered its judgment in Novaya Gazeta and Others v. Russia.

The court found violations of freedom of expression arising from Russia's restrictions on reporting and peaceful criticism concerning the war. It also concluded that Russia had disregarded the earlier interim measure concerning Novaya Gazeta, violating the right of individual application.

An external institution had identified an unlawful interference.

That was important, and also insufficient to restore the newsroom's material capacity inside Russia.

The legal judgment and the ability to make that judgment consequential had become entirely separate.

By 2026, the newspaper was still operating under the accumulated restrictions.

On April 9, investigators raided its Moscow office in connection with a criminal case involving allegations of unlawful access to computer information and misuse of personal data. Investigative reporter Oleg Roldugin was arrested and later placed under house arrest.

On September 4, authorities designated the newspaper's editorial operation a foreign agent.

On October 15, officers searched editor-in-chief Sergei Sokolov's home.

Late in October, the remaining editorial operation confronted two further losses. Its office lease was being terminated and its reader-funding mechanism was no longer available.

Sokolov explained in an interview published by Meduza that the newsroom received formal termination notices and refusals from banks.

He said the building belonged to Moscow's city government. Financial providers cited internal rules. He interpreted their collective refusal as the result of outside pressure.

His explanation of coordination is an allegation from an affected participant. The loss of the premises and payment services is independently corroborated.

And the combination was enough.

The remaining newsroom could no longer continue in its existing form.

On October 30, it announced that the operation would end on November 9.

A long sequence of restrictions had produced a terminal operational condition.

The newspaper's journalists still knew how to report.

The newspaper no longer possessed the stable material arrangements needed to remain that newspaper.

The Constitution Is Still There, By The Way.

Article 29 of the Russian Constitution guarantees freedom of thought and expression, the ability to seek and disseminate information by lawful means, and freedom of mass information.

Its fifth paragraph prohibits censorship.

This is establishes a formal commitment against a recognizable danger.

The conflict arises when we ask what this commitment can still do.

Can a newspaper publish when its registration has been revoked?
Can it sustain itself when payment processors refuse its donations?
Can its reporters investigate sensitive subjects when legal restrictions expose them to criminal prosecution?
Can a court's judgment restore an editorial operation when the surrounding institutions will not implement the remedy?
Can a newsroom remain materially independent when every institution through which its work must pass can become unavailable?

The answers cannot be supplied by rereading Article 29.

The existence of a right does not establish the continued reachability of the activity that right was supposed to protect.

A newspaper is an organized field of people, practices, records, trust, funding, equipment, editorial procedures, readers, and relationships with sources.

The ability to publish is generated through that organization.

When enough of the generating structure is damaged, a newspaper can retain its journalists, archives, legal arguments, and historical identity while losing the practical capacity to continue.

Some of those losses are direct harms to individual people.

Other losses affect the newsroom as an integrated institution.

Still others fall upon the surrounding public, whose access to investigation, evidence, contradiction, and independent correction becomes more restricted.

These harms should not be added together as though they were interchangeable units. They belong to different loci and relations.

Nor does the analysis imply that every newspaper must survive forever. Publications can close because their work ends, their readers leave, their resources are exhausted, or their organizations change.

The issue here is the documented sequence of interventions and dependencies that progressively removed the means of independent continuation.

The newspaper possessed legally describable routes of survival that were increasingly difficult, dangerous, or impossible to use.

And the institution most directly affected by this process was one whose work included investigating the powers responsible for its regulation.

The capacity for external correction was itself being contracted.

They Can Still Write.

There is an important limit to the newspaper's death notice.

The journalists have not all disappeared.

Novaya Gazeta Europe continues operating outside Russia as a separate publication. Other former staff are distributed across independent outlets and countries.

Sokolov has said that the remaining team retains its knowledge, memory, and relationships, and hopes to restore the original newspaper when circumstances permit.

The demise of the Moscow newsroom does not establish that Russian independent journalism has ceased to exist everywhere. Nor does it prove that the institutional history of Novaya Gazeta has become unrecoverable.

Some records can be moved. Some reporting can continue elsewhere. Some professional relationships can survive displacement.

The newspaper has already returned from earlier interruptions.

A later return remains conceivable.

But conceivability does not restore present reachability.

A journalist working in exile cannot necessarily reproduce the information networks, local access, protection, sources, public relationships, or operating conditions of a functioning newsroom inside Russia.

An archive can preserve articles without preserving the institution that could investigate their consequences.

A former newsroom can preserve its name without possessing the materials, authority, personnel, safety, and resources required to resume publication.

These are different continuations.

Some remain reachable.

Others have been closed or heavily burdened.

The Correction That Could Not Arrive.

There is a second reason Shchekochikhin's story belongs here.

His book shows that the information-gathering apparatus could be wrong about the meaning of its own actions.

The young man who reported on him returned years later because the security officers' answer had failed to settle the matter.

They said that the journalist had not been imprisoned. But the young man knew something had happened that required an apology.

He went to Shchekochikhin's office, described his participation, and asked to be forgiven.

Shchekochikhin was taken aback.

He remembered being unable to summon the furious response the encounter seemed to invite.

He eventually told the man to leave peacefully and go on with his life.

The apology did not erase the report or reverse the Party summons.

But the confession created a route through which the people involved could confront the event without accepting the handler's account as final.

It was a small correction outside the institution that had arranged the original harm.

Novaya Gazeta institutionalized a much larger version of that possibility.

It brought evidence about powerful organizations before readers, witnesses, critics, courts, and other institutions. It could make an official account answerable to material it did not control.

A society needs ways for an institution's claims about its protective work to be tested against the consequences borne by people outside it.

That does not make journalism infallible. A reporter can make mistakes. An independent paper can publish bad evidence, injure innocent people, or mishandle sources.

The capacity for external correction must extend to the newspaper itself.

But correction of journalistic error is different from a system in which the institutions subject to investigation can disable the routes through which investigation continues.

This is the structural danger Shchekochikhin's book had spent its pages examining.

The informer had no meaningful control over the use of his report.

The journalist had no independent access to the record that had produced his summons.

The handlers controlled the decision and the interpretation of its effects.

In 2026, Novaya Gazeta faced a much wider collection of institutional dependencies: publication, licensing, legal exposure, funding, premises, access to information, and the protection of its staff. No single restriction needs to explain the entire closure. The combined field does.

A constitutional protection becomes materially fragile when the institutions capable of denying the means of its exercise are insulated from effective correction.

The Ruling.

Slaves of the KGB documented the conversion of ordinary relationships into instruments of political surveillance.

Shchekochikhin also recognized that these practices could continue outside the organizational form that had first made them familiar.

His later reflections described security structures extending into the commercial and institutional life of post-Soviet Russia.

In October 2026, the newspaper where he had worked announced that it could no longer survive the combined effects of legal restrictions, attacks and investigations affecting staff, lost publication infrastructure, financial exclusion, and eviction.

The editors attribute the outcome to Russian authorities and security services. Some elements of that attribution remain allegations rather than independently demonstrated command relationships.

The contraction itself is visible.

The right, the judgment, the evidence, the people, and the capacity to act do not automatically travel together.

When the institutional structure connecting them is destroyed, a society can retain the language of correction long after the means of correction have ceased to function.

Shchekochikhin's informer had been assured that his report would do no harm. When he discovered otherwise, his handlers laughed.

They hadn't put the journalist in prison.

Decades later, the newsroom responsible for preserving the journalist's work is preparing its last issue.

No one had to imprison the newspaper.
