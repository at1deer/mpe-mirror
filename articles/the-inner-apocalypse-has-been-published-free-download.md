-   Large technological systems do not simply give human beings tools.
    -   They acquire requirements of their own.

A technical civilization needs engineers, administrators, specialists, predictable schedules, standardized processes, compatible skills, coordinated behavior, large institutions, and people able to perform narrow functions inside systems whose whole operation none of them understands.

So it produces them.

School trains the child. The profession trains the adult. The firm trains the employee. The market trains the firm. Competition trains the market participant.

The bureaucracy trains the citizen to become administratively visible.

Standards train whatever must cross between institutions into compatible form.

The human learns to survive by becoming useful to arrangements **nobody** in particular chose.

Kaczynski understood this unusually well.

He also understood the nastier part: **the people apparently in charge are trapped inside it too**.

A business can dislike a technology and adopt it because a competitor did.

A government can dislike an infrastructure and depend upon it because the country already runs through it.

A worker can hate a system and learn it because the rent is still coming.

An institution can recognize a disastrous incentive and obey it because refusing alone would cause the institution to lose.

Nobody has to want the resulting civilization.

Everybody only has to make the locally survivable move.

That is enough.

The machine can be assembled from solved problems.

Kaczynski eventually concluded that the technological system could not be brought under durable human control. His later account became even less personal. Societies generate self-propagating systems: organizations, firms, governments, political movements, churches, networks, institutions. They compete for resources, members, legitimacy, territory, attention, money, technical capacity, and survival. The systems that fail to reproduce disappear. The ones that survive teach their participants what successful participation requires.

You do not need a conspiracy when selection will work.

You do not need anybody to order the whole population to become compatible with the machine.

You make incompatibility expensive.

Human beings figure out the rest.

Kaczynski saw the trap.

Then he decided that the machine had to be destroyed.

That was where the analysis became another machine.

He compressed living people into representatives of technological civilization, selected bodies through occupational categories, and gave himself final authority over a transition everybody else would have to survive.

He attacked a civilization that turned people into functions by turning people into functions of his revolutionary argument.

The Collapse Machine collapsed.

-   Ted had found something real.
    -   **He had no way through it.**

## We Were the Parts.

Put Ted and Marty together and something very ugly comes into focus.

> **For most of civilization's history, the machine could not run without using human existence as its substrate.**

A rule needed a human being to remember it.

An archive needed a human being to maintain it.

An institution needed human beings to translate between incompatible systems.

A company needed people to count, file, compare, schedule, inspect, approve, deny, reconcile, supervise, calculate, report, transport, persuade, enforce, and remember.

A profession needed years of a human life converted into specialization.

A state needed people capable of seeing reality through its categories.

A market needed people whose access to survival made them responsive to prices.

A military needed bodies trained until a command could propagate through them faster than personal deliberation.

An office needed somebody to become _the office_ between nine and five.

Civilization acquired capabilities no individual human possessed by teaching individual humans to carry tiny pieces of it.

That achievement was extraordinary.

**Look at what it cost.**

Entire lives have been spent keeping a system able to remember something.

Entire careers have been spent moving information from one institutional language into another.

People have learned to think in categories that existed because an organization needed the categories.

People have become extraordinarily competent at jobs that make sense only because three older systems cannot talk to one another.

People have spent decades proving that they can reliably perform the same transformation on reality because civilization had no other available processor.

Then we called this **adulthood**.

The administrative burden is only half of it.

The other half is blindness.

**Specialization** works by narrowing.

-   The accountant has to see the account.
-   The commander has to see the operation.
-   The market participant has to see the price.
-   The firm has to see the balance sheet.
-   The regulator has to see the regulated category.
-   The platform has to see the account.

The institution becomes capable precisely because its people learn which parts of the world belong inside their function.

Then the exclusions meet one another outside the office.

No human being can hold the whole civilization in view.

So everybody becomes excellent at one piece.

The machine continues through the gaps.

## The Market Was Never Waiting for Permission.

This is why _The Inner Apocalypse_ calls capital a constitution.

Capital does not need to sit on a throne to govern.

A society can formally guarantee astonishing freedoms while distributing the material capacity to exercise them through purchasing power, property, employment, debt, ownership, credit, and access.

The market then does something remarkable. It coordinates enormous activity without one planner knowing the whole field.

It also disciplines every participant through conditions no participant individually controls.

-   The owner answers to competition.
-   The firm answers to finance.
-   The worker answers to livelihood.
-   The town answers to a tax base.
-   The government answers to production, employment, prices, debt, supply, and institutions capable of moving elsewhere.

Everybody can sincerely dislike some result.

The result can keep reproducing anyway.

That is what it means for an instrument to acquire constitutional force. Its decisions arrive before the formal constitution gets a vote.

This is also why replacing every private owner with a public administrator would not finish the problem.

The state can turn people into functions too.

So can the party.

So can the union.

So can the cooperative.

So can the revolutionary council.

So can the church.

So can the platform.

So can the community everyone insists will solve the problem once power becomes local enough.

Every institution has work it needs performed.

Every institution can begin treating the people performing it as the conditions under which the institution is allowed to continue.

Change the owner and the machine can learn a new flag.

The constitutional problem is deeper:

> **How does an institution carry a necessary function without acquiring title over the people, resources, evidence, and future required to carry it?**

## For the First Time, the Machine May Not Need a Human Being for Every Part.

This is the possibility that lead to the book.

It can maintain records. Translate between technical languages. Search enormous archives. Compare rules. Reconcile accounts. Schedule. Route. Model. Monitor. Classify. Draft. Check. Coordinate. Preserve context across handoffs. Follow a process without becoming bored by the process.

**Carry an administrative state across time without requiring one human being to spend forty years of their only life becoming the only person who knows where everything is.**

This does not make artificial intelligence magical.

It makes artificial intelligence historically **_strange_**.

**For thousands of years, civilization solved many problems by formatting a person into the missing machinery.**

**Now we are building machinery capable of carrying some of those functions directly.**

That creates an emancipatory possibility far larger than saving time on email.

## A Post-Sovereign Constitution.

So I spent the last six weeks doing something increasingly unreasonable.

**I wrote the Constitution.**

More precisely: _The Inner Apocalypse_ contains a proposed sixteen-article amendment to the United States Constitution, accompanied by the implementing machinery required to make its promises survive contact with the world.

The proposal keeps government.

It keeps elections, courts, markets, property, firms, public administration, experts, emergency authority, defense, artificial intelligence, and binding decisions.

It does something harder to all of them.

> It makes them capable of losing.

That is the central constitutional rule.

An institution can receive enough authority to perform a function.

Success at that function does not grant ownership of the conditions through which the institution can be investigated, corrected, replaced, divided, or ended.

-   A decision can be final.
    -   The decider cannot therefore become final about its own limits.

That distinction sounds small.

Almost the entire book is what happens when you force it **all the way down**.

## The Market Gets a Boundary.

The amendment does not abolish money.

Money is useful. Markets are useful. Prices can discover real information. Property can protect independence, home, maintenance, experimentation, and exit.

The constitutional change concerns jurisdiction.

A person's continued access to the material conditions of civic and bodily existence can no longer depend entirely on winning the market's game.

The amendment creates a **material floor**: functioning access to necessary food, inhabitable shelter, essential care, water, energy, communication, mobility, learning, cognitive access, and meaningful appearance before law.

-   Purchasing power can still decide many things.
    -   It cannot hold the whole human future hostage.

That changes the labor relation too.

If losing employment no longer means losing practical membership in civilization, the worker acquires a form of refusal no employment statute can manufacture while survival remains on the other side of obedience.

**Post-capital** means the market loses its throne.

The market can keep doing its work.

## Every Important Institution Has to Know How to Die.

This may be the most important part.

Critical institutions become dangerous long before anyone formally makes them sovereign.

They become dangerous when replacing them becomes impossible.

A company performs an essential function.

It succeeds.

More systems depend on it.

Alternatives disappear.

Workers specialize around it. Records accumulate inside it. Interfaces assume it.

Other institutions stop maintaining redundant capacity.

Then everyone discovers the same thing:

-   We are still legally allowed to replace it.
    -   We just cannot survive doing so.
-   The law says the institution can lose.
    -   Reality says otherwise.

That is sovereignty with excellent paperwork.

So _The Inner Apocalypse_ requires critical-function carriers to maintain an actual route through their own replacement.

The book calls this **Material Bounded Finality**.

An institution is not truly corrigible because the Constitution contains a sentence saying it may be removed. It is corrigible when something outside it can make the removal real without dropping everyone the function was carrying.

The institution must prepare for the possibility that it loses.

Especially when it is winning.

## Correction Gets Hands.

Modern constitutions are very good at declaring things.

Reality has developed a sophisticated defense.

-   A person can win a judgment
    -   and still encounter a database that does not change.
-   An office can accept an adverse ruling
    -   while the infrastructure beneath it keeps executing yesterday.
-   A right can exist
    -   above a material chain no one has the authority, access, staffing, or money to alter.

So the amendment gives correction a material route.

Evidence can leave the custody of the institution being challenged.

Provenance has to survive translation.

Claimants need standing.

Review has to reach consequence.

Correction receives protected funding.

A bounded Civil Remedy Service can execute a specific judicial remedy when the ordinary executor refuses, conflicts, or cannot perform.

Then the Civil Remedy Service itself remains corrigible.

No guardians.

No tiny emergency sovereign hidden under the floor.

No office whose job is preventing final authority gets final authority as a perk.

## Artificial Intelligence Does Not Get the Crown Either.

The amendment applies the same rule to the new machinery.

A public institution cannot point at an automated system and make responsibility disappear inside the model.

The consequential version, inputs, relevant reasons, uncertainties, and correction route have to survive.

Independent evaluation cannot exist solely at the provider's pleasure.

No single provider, model, or identity system gets to become the sole practical route through admission, evidence, and correction for the same public function.

## The First Real Opening.

Ted Kaczynski looked at technological civilization and concluded that human beings were being forced to live inside systems they could no longer meaningfully govern. He was substantially right about the wound.

He turned collapse into sovereignty over everyone trapped inside the system.

Marty Heidegger looked at modern technology and saw a world revealed as stock, resource, orderable reserve. He saw that human beings were entering the inventory too.

Then he dug so far beneath ordinary agency that history began swallowing the people history was happening to.

Neither found the path.

We inherited the machine anyway.

And for most of history there was a terrible material reason we could not simply release the people inside it:

> **The people were how the machine ran.**

That fact is finally changing.

This is the part I do not want buried underneath another argument about whether artificial intelligence is exciting or terrifying.

**Artificial intelligence may be the first technology capable of carrying enough of civilization's inherited machine-work that civilization no longer needs to consume human existence in the same way to preserve its functions.**

That is fucking enormous.

It also means we may have one extraordinary opportunity to decide which functions deserve preservation before they become executable without us.

-   We can automate the administration.
    -   We can also automate the blindness.
-   We can release people from compulsory system-compatibility.
    -   We can also build systems so internally complete that human refusal becomes an error condition.

We can use artificial intelligence to make plural institutions cheaper to maintain, alternatives easier to operate, knowledge easier to share, work easier to hand off, and correction materially real.

We can also give every inherited sovereign instrument the perfect employee.

For the first time, civilization may not need to make machines out of people.

For the first time, it may be able to build a machine that no longer needs the people at all.

Those are the two sides of the opening.

**The future does not have to be like this.**
