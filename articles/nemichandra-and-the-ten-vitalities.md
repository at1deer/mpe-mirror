# Nemichandra and the Ten Vitalities

Modal Path Ethics has been trying to figure out how to weigh a wound.

I already have a theory of harm. I have spent months working out what an actual locus is, how its continuation becomes reachable, why a path can remain formally open after it becomes impossible to travel, and how the structures that make later life possible can deteriorate before anything visibly ends.

-   Then [Patañjali](https://modalpathethics.com/patanjali-and-the-grain-of-change/) asked which organization was doing the continuing.
-   Then [Stuart Kauffman](https://modalpathethics.com/stuart-kauffman-and-the-grain-of-the-adjacent-possible/) asked what happens when that organization makes futures that could not previously be specified.
    -   Both were extremely helpful.
    -   Neither told us how much the wound weighs.

A research program aimed at the problem has stalled out at exactly the place where it should be most difficult: the transition from identifying a damaged relation to justifying its moral significance. The existing dimensions are useful questions. They have not earned the right to become a universal scale simply because I can place numbers next to them.

There are worse outcomes than admitting this, like the outcome in which the numbers all work beautifully.

So I have gone looking for someone who approached injury long before any of these diagrams existed.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-72.png)

In a tenth-century Jain treatise, the philosopher Nemichandra gives us ten vitalities, six developmental capacities, and a remarkably direct attempt to distinguish degrees of injury.

* * *

## Ten Vitalities.

Jainism is not one book, one school, or one inventory of ethical rules. It is a living religious and philosophical tradition with distinct Digambara and Śvetāmbara lineages, intricate debates about souls, matter, karma, nonviolence, discipline, knowledge, and liberation. It takes injuries to living beings seriously enough to make care in ordinary conduct a demanding spiritual practice.

Nemichandra cannot stand in for all of it. His _Gommaṭasāra_, however, is an excellent place to encounter one precise piece of it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-73.png)

The _Jīvakāṇḍa_, the portion concerned with the soul, classifies embodied beings and the vitalities through which embodied life proceeds. In verse 130, Nemichandra identifies ten _prāṇas_.

Five belong to the senses:

-   **touch,**
-   **taste,**
-   **smell,**
-   **sight,** and
-   **hearing.**

Three are powers of **body**, **speech**, and **mind**. **Respiration** and **lifespan** complete the ten.

These are not ten equally distributed tokens that every organism has hiding in the microtubules. The text distinguishes classes of beings according to their embodied vitalities. In verse 133, the classification becomes quite elaborate. Some possess the full set; others possess fewer.

Already, two things matter.

First, a living being need not cease to exist for a vitality to be injured.

-   Sight can be lost while respiration continues.
-   Bodily power can be burdened while sensory activity remains.
-   Speech can be obstructed while other capacities persist.

Second, Jainism's concern with life does not stop at creatures that resemble human beings.

-   Its wider cosmology includes one-sensed life and earth-bodied, water-bodied, fire-bodied, air-bodied, and plant-bodied beings.
-   Whatever we eventually make of that cosmology, it would be a spectacular mistake to describe Jain nonviolence as concern for humans and a few particularly photogenic mammals.

Modal Path Ethics recognizes the first point immediately. A qualified contraction belongs to a bearer in some particular respect. A person can be injured without being annihilated; a living organization can retain some capacities while others are lost.

But we have to keep the Jain source intact. The _prāṇas_ belong to a metaphysics of _jīva_, the soul, and its embodied, karmically conditioned existence. They are not modern physiological measurements wearing Sanskrit names. Nor do they already constitute my own list of structural dimensions.

The more interesting comparison comes immediately before them.

* * *

## Before the Vitality.

-   Chapter IV of _Jīvakāṇḍa_ discusses _prāṇa_.
    -   **Chapter III** discusses _paryāpti_.

In J. L. Jaini's 1927 translation and commentary, _paryāpti_ concerns the attainment or completion of capacities through which a newly embodied being develops its functions. The six concern

-   assimilation,
-   bodily formation,
-   the senses,
-   respiration,
-   speech, and
-   mind.

The text describes their completion within a short interval after embodiment, according to the kinds of capacities attributed to the being.

This is a very specific account. It is not a theory that organisms spend their entire lives acquiring every conceivable new function or an account of the indefinitely open-ended adjacent possible.

Then Jaini's commentary to verse 129 distinguishes the two notions: _paryāpti_ concerns the acquisition of the capacity to develop the relevant faculties; _prāṇa_ concerns their activity.

There it is.

-   **The conditions through which a function becomes available**

and

-   **the operation of that function**

are distinguishable.

We came here looking for weights. We have found an older distinction between developmental capacity and presently operative vitality.

That distinction does not by itself explain everything that Modal Path Ethics calls a **continuation-generating structure**. The Jain account is about the capacities of embodied souls in a particular cosmology. This account must also handle history, external dependencies, ecological reproduction, institutions, and the production of successors that may not yet have a known description.

But it makes the problem much easier to see.

Take the American chestnut.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/84234.jpg)

Blight devastated mature chestnuts across much of their native range. Surviving roots could continue producing shoots. Leaves grew. Living tissue persisted. Yet those shoots often died before reaching reproductive maturity. The species retained activity while its ordinary route toward mature, reproducing trees was profoundly damaged.

If our instrument asks only whether living functions remain, it may congratulate the tree.

The tree is less impressed.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/84235.jpg)

Something similar can happen to an institution. Its offices open. Its reports arrive. Its staff can still execute familiar tasks. Meanwhile records disappear, successor competence decays, appeals become prohibitively expensive, or the ordinary routes through which the institution renews its function are severed.

The output can survive the generator that used to sustain it.

A school can still teach this year's pupils while losing the teachers, records, local trust, and practical access through which next year's pupils would arrive. A community can retain storefronts after the relations that once sustained commerce have deteriorated.

This is not an invitation to declare that a school has Jain _paryāpti_. It is a reason to stop treating present operation as the complete description of continuation.

-   Nemichandra separates acquired developmental capacity from its activity.
-   Modal Path Ethics must investigate the larger causal organization through which present activities can be sustained, regenerated, repaired, or transformed.

The comparison has not yet earned a moral weight.

Now Nemichandra's commentator tries to supply one.

* * *

## The Scale.

The commentary following _Gommaṭasāra_ 133 makes an explicit judgment about degrees of injury. Jaini writes that the degree of sin depends on the number of vitalities injured and their comparative strength.

This is a claim in the 1927 commentary. It should not be silently passed off as a numerical theorem stated by Nemichandra in verse 133 itself.

But it is a real grading proposal. The vitalities affected, their number, and their comparative strength matters.

That is closer to the question we are asking than most of the contemporary machinery I have encountered.

An injury that removes one minor ability and an injury that destroys a whole sustaining organization need not have the same gravity. A serious evaluator has to notice _what_ was damaged before deciding how much the damage matters.

Unfortunately, counting does not get us all the way there.

What does comparative strength mean? Strength for which activity, in which bearer, under which circumstances? Is the scale evaluating the extent of physical injury, the sin of the actor, the soul's karmic condition, or an all-things-considered choice among competing harms?

Those are different questions.

There is also an immediate danger in converting a classification of vitalities into a ranking of persons. Someone whose embodied life depends on assistance or technology is not a lesser moral patient because the analyst can count fewer independent biological functions.

To be fair to Nemichandra, his taxonomy distinguishes classes of embodied existence inside Jain cosmology; it is not a modern proposal to rank disabled people by capacity. Making that translation would be our mistake.

Still a mistake worth guarding against before borrowing anything.

A person's actual continuation can depend on a communication device, a caregiver, a safe route to a building, or a legal standing that the body cannot generate by itself. Remove one of those relations and substantial continuations may close without any change in the number of bodily senses.

Conversely, difference from a supposed normal human body is not evidence that an actual person has just suffered a contraction.

We need the bearer, the relation, and the change. The number alone cannot supply them.

This is where the Jain account becomes more demanding still. The _Tattvārtha Sūtra_ asks what actually makes an injury an act of violence.

* * *

## The Knife of Carelessness.

In the Digambara numbering of the _Tattvārtha Sūtra_, verse 7.13 defines _hiṃsā_ through the severance of vitalities associated with _pramattayoga_: activity marked by carelessness or passionate disposition.

Pūjyapāda's _Sarvārthasiddhi_ commentary refuses to treat the external event as the whole moral description. The state of the acting soul matters. The severance of vitalities, considered alone, does not exhaust the Jain account of culpable violence.

That is not a minor footnote.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-82.png)

Jain nonviolence is a discipline of conduct and spiritual transformation. What the actor cultivates through body, speech, and mind counts alongside the embodied injury inflicted. Its account of karma has a different explanatory job from an external description of damaged continuation.

Modal Path Ethics begins elsewhere.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-83.png)

If a storm destroys a person's sight, the injury occurs without a negligent human actor. If blight damages the chestnut's reproductive process, the generative loss does not wait for a culprit. A violent intention can also remain morally relevant when the intended physical injury fails to occur.

The framework separates the harm, the wider transition, and the questions of responsibility and verdict.

Jainism organizes some of these questions differently. We should not pretend the two definitions are interchangeable simply because both notice injured capacities.

But Jain practice also supplies an extraordinary list of concrete things nonviolence must attend to.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-84.png)

In _Tattvārtha Sūtra_ 7.25, the five transgressions of the householder's lesser vow of noninjury are binding, beating, mutilation, overloading, and withholding food or drink.

Pūjyapāda explains binding through obstruction of movement. Overloading means imposing more weight than a human or animal can reasonably bear. Withholding food and drink denies essential sustenance.

Notice what the list catches.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-85.png)

A being can remain alive while its movement is closed. Its continued labor can be made dangerous or degrading. Its needs can be denied while it remains present and recognizable to its keeper.

Jain ethics has been looking at injury inside continuation all along.

* * *

## The Body Needs a World.

In _Tattvārtha Sūtra_ 5.21, we find:

> **_parasparopagraho jīvānām_**

> Souls render service to one another.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-86.png)

Pūjyapāda's commentary describes reciprocal relations between teacher and pupil, master and servant, and the ways living beings become causes of one another's pleasure, suffering, life, and death.

These examples do not describe a modern egalitarian ideal. Some carry the social hierarchies of their setting. The point for this encounter is narrower and concrete: Jain philosophy already recognizes that embodied life is affected through relations among living beings.

The vitality is never the whole world that sustains its exercise.

A person can possess the bodily power to walk and still be unable to reach food. A child can possess the capacity to learn and still have no reliable access to instruction. A traveler can possess sight, movement, and a functioning vehicle while the institutions around the road make the journey dangerous.

Now we can return to two cases that already sit at the center of the harm paper.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-78-1.png)

The _Negro Motorist Green Book_ was published from 1936 to 1964 to help Black travelers identify lodging, dining, and other services during segregation. Victor H. Green's guide made visible some of the support required for a journey that a road map could not guarantee.

-   The road existed.
-   The destination existed.
-   The traveler's body worked.
-   Safe access was another question.

A list of available roads was a poor substitute for knowing where one could stop without exclusion or danger. The Green Book supplied information about the relations through which movement could actually be sustained.

North Nashville gives us a different case with the same structural wound.

In 1967, community leaders challenged a planned 3.6-mile segment of Interstate 40. The route would cut through North Nashville's Black commercial and educational district. The federal appellate court later affirmed the refusal to issue a preliminary injunction. It also acknowledged substantial damage to the community.

Here a transportation project expanded access through one network by severing relations within another.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-81.png)

The damage reached beyond buildings scheduled for demolition. Shops depend on customers being able to arrive. Educational institutions depend on circulation among people, services, and neighboring organizations. A community's characteristic commercial and social activity depends on relations that cannot be replaced by preserving a few signs and addresses.

A business can remain standing while the route supporting it disappears.

A college can remain open while its surrounding field becomes harder to traverse.

The question of whether North Nashville also constitutes a collective locus at its own grain requires evidence of historically integrated, nonredundant continuation; it is not settled by drawing a line around a neighborhood. The injuries to residents, businesses, and institutions remain real regardless.

The Jain texts contribute two things here:

-   attention to differentiated vitalities,
-   and recognition that living beings sustain and injure one another through practical relations.

Modal Path Ethics follows those relations outward into infrastructure, institutions, records, access, and support. It then has to establish, case by case, what belonged to the actual continuation-generating organization and what the transition damaged.

> **An account of what lives has to be able to see what keeps it alive.**

There is, however, a place where the Jain boundary becomes unavoidable.

* * *

## Before There Was a Soul to Injure.

In 2006, NASA published a Spitzer Space Telescope image from the star-forming cloud IC 1396.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-77.png)

It showed a potential planet-forming disk around a sun-like star being stripped by the intense radiation and stellar winds of a neighboring O-type star. The process, photoevaporation, can remove the gas and dust required for a disk to retain its organization and proceed toward planet formation.

The observation does not prove that a particular planet would otherwise have existed. Modal Path Ethics does not need it to.

The proposed bearer is the presently organized disk. Its material arrangement supports a family of planet-forming continuations. Insofar as the process removes the organization required for those continuations, it damages a real generator before any resulting planet or planetary life appears.

That is the conditional claim defended in _Harm as Contraction of Reachable Continuation_. The **normative bridge**—the proposition that qualifying damage to an actual locus's organized continuability constitutes pro tanto harm—still has to be accepted and defended. The observation alone does not prove it.

Jainism is unusually expansive about forms of life. That makes the comparison worth taking seriously. But nothing in Nemichandra's ten-vitality classification licenses assigning Jain _prāṇas_ to a protoplanetary disk. The disk has not been shown to be a Jain _jīva_. The claim does not require it to be one.

This is the point where the theories part company.

-   Jain injury and nonviolence are situated within a metaphysics of living souls, karma, practice, and liberation.
-   Modal Path Ethics proposes that certain nonliving, presently active causal organizations may also bear contraction of their continuations.

We can borrow the question of damaged capacity without borrowing a universal answer to who can bear it.

We can borrow a distinction between developmental conditions and present activity without treating Jain _paryāpti_ as an account of every form of generation in the cosmos.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-87.png)

We can learn from a theology of nonviolence without appointing its inventory sovereign over the field.

There is a final reason for this caution, and Jainism supplies it itself.

* * *

## The Vitalities Are Not the Goal.

Jain liberation is not an attempt to maximize the number of embodied functions an individual can exercise indefinitely.

Renunciation, disciplined conduct, restraint, and release from karmic bondage belong to its central aims. The activity of an embodied soul and the spiritual good sought through its conduct are not identical questions.

A person can choose a difficult discipline, relinquish an option, or undergo a transformation that changes what they can do without thereby suffering a harmful contraction in every respect.

Protective action can close a destructive course. A harmful institution can lose the means to repeat an injury. A pathogen's successful replication can be disastrous for its hosts. Preservation cannot be the automatic verdict whenever some locally identified organization continues.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-88.png)

The framework has already learned this lesson through the distinction between a harm, a mixed transition profile, and an overall judgment. The presence of one loss does not determine whether the whole action was justified. A justified action does not reach backward in time and erase the loss.

The Jain encounter places another limit on the same. Its ten vitalities help characterize embodied injury. Its account of conduct evaluates the disposition and karmic consequences of acting. Its account of liberation gives the tradition a spiritual horizon that the tally of bodily functions does not itself determine.

> The parts do different jobs.

We should allow ours to do different jobs too.

* * *

## Judgment.

Nemichandra sadly did not give Modal Path Ethics seven new coefficients.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/10/image-76.png)

He gave us an account of injury that differentiates what is damaged within a continuing being, an account of developmental capacities distinct from their activity, a commentarial attempt to grade the seriousness of injury, and, through the wider Jain tradition, a discipline of nonviolence attentive to confinement, deprivation, overburdening, and the relations through which living beings affect one another.

None of that proves a universal severity scale.

The number of vitalities cannot stand in for the worth of a person. The strength of a function cannot be assumed from its presence on an inherited list. The Jain account of sin cannot be silently substituted for an agent-independent harm-token. And the presence of organized matter outside Jain _jīva_ metaphysics does not acquire ten vitalities because one would find the symmetry pleasing.

Those limits are part of what makes the encounter useful. We now have a clearer order of inquiry.

-   First identify the actual bearer and its causally warranted grain.
-   Then investigate the capacities through which its characteristic continuation is sustained or generated, including support that lies beyond its body.
-   Then identify the specific damage: what was lost, what became harder to reach, what can be reopened, who else now carries the burden, and what the injury prevents the bearer from continuing to do.

Only after that does the question of comparative moral weight become properly formed.

The answer may be partial. It may differ across fields. Some claims may take precedence without becoming commensurable in one master score. Others will remain unresolved until the evidence improves.

That is considerably less satisfying than a universal formula. Also less likely to accidentally convert a measurement instrument into a moral sovereign.

-   **Patañjali** asked what undergoes change.
-   **Kauffman** asked what new continuations that organization can generate.
-   **Nemichandra** asks us to examine the living capacities exposed to injury,
    -   and his tradition reminds us that those capacities operate inside relations of dependence and conduct.

Modal Path Ethics can carry all three questions forward without making them one question.

-   The chestnut still sprouts.
-   The traveler can still drive.
-   The neighborhood still has streets.
-   The disk still contains matter.

> **What remains is not yet an answer to what was lost.**

That is where the weighing problem begins.
