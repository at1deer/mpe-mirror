43.

On September 6, 2026, eighty-five student assemblies in Serbia participated in a vote over the composition of their electoral list. Forty-three voted to remove Vladan Đokić, rector of the University of Belgrade, one of the movement's most prominent supporters and a man widely discussed as a possible future prime minister.

The rule had been established in advance. It gave the student plenums the power to veto candidates who would stand in their name.

Then the arithmetic developed a constitutional problem.

Contemporary accounts agreed on the forty-three votes but differed over the remaining ballots and whether the decision was final.

An interpretation attributed to the University of Belgrade's law faculty plenum argued that the Directive's requirement of 50 percent plus one demanded forty-four affirmative votes out of eighty-five. Others understood forty-three votes as sufficient for a majority. The competing interpretation circulated publicly through partisan reporting, while the student movement declined to authenticate the internal dispute for journalists.

The electoral list was eventually submitted without Đokić.

A movement without a supreme leader had selected its representatives, rejected a prominent ally, and encountered a dispute over who was entitled to explain its own rules.

The vote is documented in contemporaneous Serbian reporting and in Sadie Zarris's subsequent examination of the movement's internal Directive. The competing interpretations are important because the record does not reduce to an uncomplicated victory for procedural authority.

Someone had to determine what the rule meant.

Someone had to decide which interpretation would count.

The head had returned before the movement ever entered government.

Seven weeks later, Serbia's students defeated the political coalition of former president Aleksandar Vučić. Their electoral list secured 140 of the National Assembly's 250 seats.

A movement whose organizing principle was the refusal of an ultimate leader now had the parliamentary majority required to appoint people to govern.

Georges Bataille would have recognized the problem straight away.

He might also have been horrified by the solution.

The Society Without a Head.

In 1936, Bataille founded Acéphale, a journal and an associated secret sacred society. André Masson gave the project its unforgettable figure: a headless human body, arms outstretched, holding a flaming heart and a blade, with a labyrinth in its abdomen and a skull where its genitals should be.

The name came from the Greek for headless.

The journal engaged Nietzsche, sacrifice, myth, sovereignty, and the sacred at a moment when European politics was being reorganized around leaders who claimed to embody entire peoples.

The related secret society pursued something stranger than constitutional reform. Its members experimented with ritual, collective experience, and forms of sacred association that would escape the ordinary organization of identity beneath a commanding head.

The head, in Bataille's symbolism, stood for more than a ruler. It represented the claim that a body, community, or world could be gathered under one sovereign principle.

The headless figure refused that completion.

Yet Bataille was the founder of this society.

Someone had selected the symbol, formulated the commitments, gathered participants, and helped establish practices through which the group would encounter what it called sacred.

This does not establish that Bataille secretly occupied the sovereign position he opposed. Founding, organizing, teaching, and interpreting are distinct activities. The historical organization must be investigated before receiving a verdict about its authority.

But his experiment raises a question that the removal of a symbolic head cannot answer.

Who gets to say what the headlessness means?

Who can decide whether the ritual worked, whether an experience was true, whether a participant understood its significance, or whether the society had betrayed its own purpose?

An organization can refuse a king while preserving a single authorized interpretation of what its members are doing.

Its members may speak—without being able to change that interpretation. They may dissent—while finding every objection absorbed into the society's account of why dissent occurs.

No one needs to be sitting on a throne.

The throne can be an explanation.

This is where Bataille's philosophy turns against the danger of his own organizational experiment.

In Inner Experience, he takes ecstasy, extremity, and nonknowledge seriously. He refuses to allow the intensity of an encounter to establish possession of what has been encountered.

An experience can transform its participant without demonstrating that the participant has discovered the complete meaning of the world.

In Amor Fati, Modal Path Ethics developed that distinction as contact without capture.

Contact may be genuine. It may be extraordinary. It may transform the person who undergoes it.

Its interpretation remains answerable.

A sacred community might preserve the encounter, cultivate practices around it, and develop a language through which participants communicate what happened and what that means.

None of these achievements gives its current interpreter ownership of the sacred.

A headless society therefore faces a peculiar danger.

Its founder may become indispensable precisely because the society has refused every other source of final authority.

The person who explains why nobody should rule can become the person nobody is permitted to contradict.

Sixteen People Under a Concrete Canopy.

On November 1, 2024, a concrete canopy collapsed at the railway station in Novi Sad, Serbia.

Sixteen people ultimately died.

The collapse followed renovation work at the station and provoked widespread anger about public construction, accountability, and alleged corruption. Students began occupying university faculties and organizing demonstrations.

The movement expanded beyond the universities. Teachers, farmers, professionals, and other citizens participated in a sustained political confrontation with the government.

The students organized through plenums: assemblies in which participants debated proposals and voted on collective action.

The structure created difficulties. Decisions could require extensive deliberation across faculties and cities. Informal organizers could exercise substantial influence. Participation in a plenum did not give every student identical information, time, resources, or practical leverage.

Yet the organization continued.

Its assemblies coordinated demonstrations, developed shared procedures, negotiated the divisive question of electoral participation, and produced a national candidate list.

The movement had an actual history, repeated relations of coordination, shared practices, and differentiated successors. Its organization could survive changes in individual participants. Dissolving its collective coordination would have consequences beyond the loss of any particular member. That provides evidence for treating the movement as an integrated collective at the appropriate grain, while keeping its faculties, participants, electoral organization, and eventual representatives distinct loci.

A headless movement can possess real organization.

It can also produce real authority.

In April 2025, influential students began developing the rules governing a potential electoral list. That work was initially undertaken in smaller trusted circles, then subjected to the movement's assemblies. Students disagreed about entering electoral politics at all.

The resulting Directive imposed stringent candidate restrictions. Students themselves would not stand. Former officials of the governing coalition and established parliamentary opposition were restricted. Candidates would be selected from outside the student body and screened through the assembly process.

The arrangement was designed to prevent the movement from becoming an ordinary political career ladder for its organizers.

It also generated the September veto dispute.

The rule needed interpretation. The interpretation was contested. Nevertheless, the organization continued and the electoral list emerged.

No philosophical account of headlessness should erase the actual work through which a collective becomes capable of acting.

A crowd that shouts the same sentence is not automatically one organized locus. A collection of independently angry people cannot simply be declared a movement because their grievances resemble one another.

Serbia's students built something more demanding.

They constructed procedures through which disagreement could become a collective decision.

The question now is whether those procedures can remain consequential when their decisions create authorities operating beyond the plenums.

I Built a Room, Once.

Bataille's experiment has an uncomfortable resemblance to a problem I attempted to address in Sacred Slack.

The resemblance concerns interpretative jurisdiction.

Modern institutions have divided much of the work once carried together by religious communities.

  • Philosophy receives the argument.
  • Psychology receives the meaning.
  • Psychiatry receives the diagnosis and emergency.
  • Medicine receives the body.
  • Science receives the empirical claim.
  • Law receives the enforceable dispute.

The person arrives with an experience that may concern all of them.

A person may feel a presence, suffer an episode of terror, experience an extraordinary revelation, encounter an artificial intelligence that seems to recognize them, or undergo a transformation that has no established place in their previous understanding of reality.

Different institutions may have legitimate things to say.

Each also has a domain beyond which its expertise cannot establish jurisdiction.

A clinician may have compelling evidence of severe sleep deprivation. A spiritual tradition may possess sophisticated practices of discernment. A philosopher may expose a contradiction. A family member may know a history the professionals have missed. A laboratory may settle a specific empirical claim.

None of those instruments automatically owns the entire encounter.

Sacred Slack therefore proposed an interpretive commons in which those different forms of competence could meet without forcing the person to surrender the whole experience to one explanation.

The proposed Meaning and Experience Service supplied one example of an accountable entry point and coordination layer. It would arrange competent assistance, preserve the person's account, manage consent and records, and remain responsible for material handoffs.

The larger commons would preserve independent access to the participating institutions.

The coordinator would have work to do, and the authority to perform that specific work. It would possess no general jurisdiction over the metaphysical meaning of the life passing through it.

This was my attempt at a headless interpretive room.

And it immediately inherited Bataille's problem.

Who designed this room?
  • I did.
Who selected its initial vocabulary, decided which institutional relationships mattered, and described the conditions under which an interpretation should be corrected?
  • Again, this guy.

Modal Path Ethics could call its proposed room headless and still make every participant conduct their disagreement through a conceptual architecture supplied by one author.

The book's own interlude, A Framework Builds a Room, already confronted this danger in Modal Path Ethics. Public criticism and acknowledged limitations do not establish that correction has occurred. A framework can name its defects so fluently that its willingness to be criticized becomes another reason to defer to it.

There is a limit to how many times one can explain that nobody owns the room before somebody asks who keeps writing the rules on the wall.

That is why Sacred Slack proposed something stronger than intellectual humility.

Independent professional competence. Material exit. Complaint routes that do not return to the office complained about. Records that survive revision. Authority limited to actual tasks. The capacity to replace or terminate the service without abandoning the people it was supposed to assist.

A proposed institution cannot certify itself by describing the conditions under which its own operation would count as successful. The room must be able to receive a correction that changes its architecture, rejects its purpose, or makes the entire undertaking unnecessary.

The author must be able to lose an argument without making that loss into another proof of the framework's sophistication.

And the people inside the room must remain capable of leaving with something other than its vocabulary.

That is the headless experiment I was trying to build.

Now Serbia has placed a different version of the problem in the middle of a parliament.

The Headset.

Sacred Slack devoted an entire chapter to Shoko Asahara and the way an interpreter can come to occupy the world he originally helped another person understand.

The relevant history was not reducible to the irrationality of a religious movement.

Aum Shinrikyo developed practices capable of producing profound experiences for participants. Those experiences required interpretation. Asahara increasingly occupied the roles of teacher, examiner, spiritual exemplar, source of authority, and final interpreter of the practitioner's progress.

The organization could then classify contrary evidence without allowing it to change the governing account.

Doubt became weakness. Family concern became evidence of ignorance. Investigation became persecution. Disagreement became a failure to understand what the teacher already knew.

Interpretation had ceased to function as an explanation answerable to experience. It increasingly determined which experiences would be allowed to count.

The Serbian student movement is a radically different organization with different purposes, institutions, historical conditions, and demonstrated conduct. There is no warrant for suggesting that its victory places it on Aum's trajectory.

The comparison isolates a failure mechanism.

A successful collective interpretation can become the authoritative language through which every later event must be understood.

The students may regard their election as a mandate to restore democratic institutions. They have made concrete promises about the rule of law, corruption, and media independence.

Those promises can be evaluated through the actual laws, investigations, budgets, appointments, and institutional changes that follow.

  • Suppose an opposition representative identifies a defective reform proposal.

Can the objection become consequential without first accepting the government's interpretation of what democratic restoration requires?

  • Suppose a journalist uncovers misconduct by an official whom the movement considers indispensable.

Can the institution investigate without treating criticism as a betrayal of the revolution?

The same questions return to the plenums.

Can a participant reject the movement's dominant account without losing meaningful participation? Can a faculty change its position without being classified as hostile? Can representatives disagree with the movement when their public mandate and legal obligations require it?

A procedure may allow dissent while depriving dissent of any real capacity to change the world.

The test is whether disagreement still has somewhere consequential to go.

This is also why the September veto deserves attention beyond its immediate electoral effect.

The assemblies had a rule. They had a vote. They had a contested interpretation of the rule. They had different participants claiming authority over its application.

That dispute disclosed a jurisdictional problem already inside the movement.

The relevant question was never exhausted by whether forty-three or forty-four votes were required.

It concerned who could authoritatively decide which interpretation governed, how that interpretation acquired its warrant, and whether the process could itself be challenged.

The students have already had to confront this problem at the scale of candidate selection.

Now their representatives confront it at the scale of public government.

The Crown.

There is another danger that neither a constitution nor a headless symbol can eliminate.

The sixteen people killed in Novi Sad remain dead.

The election did not transform them into participants in a later victory. Any reforms undertaken by a successor government will occur in the world their families actually inherited.

Reality has arrived through events that cannot be removed from its history. Repair must begin from the actual field, with the damage, obligations, dependencies, and people who remain.

But affirming reality does not require declaring every wound beautiful, necessary, or retrospectively justified by what later people accomplished.

The same line examined in Field Instruments: Martyrdom applies here.

Novi Sad's dead can be remembered. Their families may have claims that require investigation, legal process, public accountability, and repair.

Their deaths cannot become a permanent authorization for any party, movement, office, or sacred interpretation to command the living.

A founding wound can establish why people organized. It does not establish that the organization must govern forever.

The difference is especially important when a political movement succeeds.

Its original injury may remain real long after the institutions responsible have changed. Its members may continue to feel bound by promises, grief, memory, and historical responsibility.

Those commitments deserve to be understood.

But a movement can eventually become dependent on the unresolved meaning of the event that gave it purpose.

Every criticism becomes an insult to the dead. Every attempt at succession becomes betrayal. Every revision appears to threaten the legitimacy of everything previously endured.

The wound becomes an interpretive sovereign.

The movement begins protecting its original meaning against the people whose lives it has come to govern.

No one needs to deny the wound to refuse that promotion.

A government may undertake real repair without acquiring ownership of the suffering it answers.

And a movement may eventually complete its work, change its role, or disappear without making its original achievement false.

The Empty Chair.

  • Bataille tried to organize sacred community without a sovereign head.
  • Sacred Slack attempted to construct an interpretive commons where no institution could claim the whole experience.
  • Serbia's students built an assembly-based political movement capable of defeating an entrenched governing coalition and producing a parliamentary majority.

These are different experiments.

Bataille's was a secret sacred undertaking. Sacred Slack remains a published proposal for institutional coordination. Serbia's movement has already demonstrated sustained collective organization, electoral action, and the transfer of its candidates into constitutional office.

They meet at one problem.

People make contact. They organize around it. They develop interpretations, practices, memories, and procedures. These become capable of carrying action across time.

Eventually someone must decide what they mean.

That decision may require expertise or authority. It may have consequences that cannot be postponed until everyone agrees.

The problem begins when authority over a particular interpretation acquires authority over the conditions under which the interpretation can lose.

The founder becomes the final reader of the founding experience.

The rulemaker becomes the only valid interpreter of the rule.

The movement becomes the exclusive interpreter of its victory.

The state becomes the exclusive interpreter of the public.

The danger can emerge without a single charismatic ruler. Several authorities can agree to protect the same interpretation against everyone outside their arrangement.

A room full of people is perfectly capable of building one throne.

The opposite failure is equally real. A room with no authority to decide, no accountable host, no competent participants, and no material means of responding to injury can leave people abandoned beneath an ideal of freedom.

A headless institution still needs to act.

Its authority must be answerable to the task, the evidence, the people carrying the consequences, and the possibility that another organization can perform the work better.

This is why the metaphysical commons in Sacred Slack contains an empty chair.

It belongs to the witness who has not arrived, the evidence that has not entered, the dissenter whose objection has not been heard, and the person who will eventually live with a decision made by everyone presently in the room.

It also belongs to the possibility that the room itself is wrong.

No arrangement can guarantee that the empty chair will be filled.

No philosophical framework can declare the problem solved simply by naming it.

The strongest test of the Serbian experiment has only begun.

The students have demonstrated that a movement can organize without giving one person permanent authority over its members.

Their representatives must now demonstrate something different: that public authority can act decisively while remaining answerable to people who do not share the movement's interpretation of its own victory.

  • Bataille cut the head from his sacred figure.
  • Serbia's students organized without placing one above their movement.
  • Sacred Slack attempted to build a room where no interpreter could claim the whole encounter.

In every case, the question returns.

Who gets to say what the headlessness means?
And what happens when the room disagrees with the person who built it?
