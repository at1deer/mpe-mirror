Applied Case: The Sun is Rising

Published 2026-11-27 · Updated 2026-11-27 · Aidan Lawson
Tags: Applied Case · Geopolitical Wasteland · Inner Apocalypse
Mirror generated 2026-11-27T21:34:50.795Z

On November 25, 2026, Jiji Press reported that Japan intends to declare its ambition of becoming a leading artificial intelligence nation behind the United States and China.

The ambition is to be incorporated into the country's revised national security documents before the end of the year. Japan intends to reduce its technological dependence on other countries, invest intensively in autonomous driving and physical AI, and acquire technological capabilities that make it indispensable to the international community.

Third place.

An interesting declaration from a country that already manufactures a considerable portion of the machinery through which artificial intelligence might eventually act upon the world.

And a very interesting declaration to make five weeks before 2027.

Japan is entering this race with something that many of its competitors are still trying to find:

A very good reason to automate.

The Workers Who Aren't Coming.

According to the OECD's 2026 Economic Survey of Japan, Japan will require about 570,000 additional caregivers by fiscal 2040 compared with fiscal 2022.

Nearly three in ten Japanese residents are already over the age of 65. The employment rate among those aged 15–64 reached 80.2% in the first quarter of 2026, among the highest in the OECD. Recruitment difficulties remain widespread, particularly among smaller businesses and non-manufacturing industries.

There is a limit to how much additional labor can be extracted from a population that is already working.

The children who were never born cannot be recruited into the workforce of 2027.

Immigration, improved working conditions, higher wages, institutional reform and family policy all offer routes through different parts of this problem. Some are already being pursued. Automation introduces another possibility of considerable structural significance: reducing the amount of human labor necessary to maintain a society's existing functions.

Much of the American conversation about artificial intelligence concerns the arrival of machines capable of performing work for which people still need employment.

Japan has plenty of that problem, too. But it also has the inverse problem: work that needs performing for which the people are becoming increasingly difficult to find.

A rural municipality still needs to administer its services. Agricultural land still needs cultivating. The elderly still need care. Bridges, railways, electrical infrastructure and water systems do not adjust their maintenance requirements to accommodate a declining birth rate.

There is an enormous difference between making a worker redundant and making a community's survival less dependent upon finding another worker.

Japan has an opportunity to do a great deal of the latter.

The Body Was Already Here.

FANUC. Yaskawa. Kawasaki. Keyence. Toyota. Sony. Nabtesco. Harmonic Drive Systems. Tokyo Electron.

It is worth looking at what these companies actually make.

Robotic arms, servomotors, precision reduction gears, machine vision systems, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, image sensors, industrial control systems, vehicles and the machinery necessary to manufacture more machinery.

The first great commercial movement of generative AI took place predominantly through software. A new intelligence could be distributed through an API and arrive on millions of screens without requiring millions of new physical objects.

Physical AI is a less convenient proposition.

An intelligent machine needs a body. That body requires actuators, sensors, materials, energy, control systems, manufacturing tolerances and some means of remaining operational after colliding with the part of reality that was insufficiently represented in its training data.

Somebody must also know how to fix it.

Japan has spent generations developing these capacities.

And it has accumulated something else of importance to physical AI: industrial environments in which machines, materials and human operators already interact, generating data about processes that a general-purpose model might otherwise encounter only through representations.

The Japanese government's industrial investment roadmap explicitly identifies these manufacturing foundations and operational data as advantages to be exploited.

On November 12, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi outlined targets of approximately ten million AI-powered robots deployed by 2040 and a Japanese share exceeding 30% of the projected global AI-robot market, worth approximately ¥20 trillion.

The country's broader investment roadmap anticipates more than ¥370 trillion in combined public and private investment across seventeen strategic fields through 2040.

Physical AI alone accounts for ¥10.5 trillion in projected investment.

These are targets. The money has not all been committed, the robots do not all exist, and the market into which Japan intends to sell them has yet to develop at the anticipated scale.

There is also a dragon in the room.

The Dragon Was Building Robots.

The International Federation of Robotics' World Robotics 2026 report contains some unpleasant numbers for Japan.

In 2025:

  • China installed 354,000 industrial robots.
  • Japan installed 36,219.
    • Chinese installations increased by 20%.
    • Japanese installations decreased by 19%.

Chinese manufacturers accounted for 55% of installations within China.

Domestic installations are an imperfect measure of manufacturing competitiveness. They do not capture Japan's enormous export industry, its position within international component supply chains, or the performance of particular manufacturers.

They do, however, illustrate the scale at which China's industrial automation ecosystem is developing.

And China has its own demographic problem.

Japan's manufacturing history grants it a valuable starting position. It does not confer permanent ownership of the future of robotics. Chinese manufacturers are developing the same relationships between industrial deployment, operational data, machine-learning research, component production and manufacturing scale that Japan hopes to exploit.

The machine that Japan could manufacture better ten years ago may be manufactured more cheaply elsewhere five years from now. Its historic advantages must be converted into new capacities while the relevant paths remain available.

Industrial knowledge can decay. Supply chains can migrate. Expertise can disappear through retirement. Markets can reorganize around somebody else's standards.

A civilization can retain the memory of having been technologically indispensable long after the material conditions of its indispensability have contracted.

  • Japan's window is real.
    • It is also moving.

The Fish and the Catfish.

On the very same day that Japan's national AI ambitions were reported, Sakana AI announced Sakana Namazu Small.

The Tokyo-based research company took NVIDIA's pretrained Nemotron 3 Nano model and subjected it to its own continued pretraining and post-training procedures, producing a reasoning model containing thirty billion parameters.

In Sakana's reported evaluation across twenty-five benchmarks, Namazu Small achieved a higher average score than both the corresponding NVIDIA model and the substantially larger Nemotron 3 Super.

The individual results are more interesting than the average.

Namazu Small achieved 73.5 on BrowseComp, against 36.1 for Nemotron 3 Super. It scored 86 on LLM-jp LongBench against the larger model's 60.

On SimpleQA Verified, however, it scored 6.8 against the larger model's 18.1.

Sakana openly acknowledges that the improvements were concentrated in research, information retrieval and document-grounded tasks, with less success in improving knowledge-dependent performance without external information.

There is an identifiable shape to the achievement.

This model has been developed toward a particular family of useful capacities, with planned provision for operation on customers' own infrastructure. Its development does not require Sakana to reproduce the entire computational expenditure behind the original foundation model.

This is one route through Japan's enormous disadvantage in private AI investment.

According to Stanford's 2026 AI Index, the United States attracted around $285.9 billion in private AI investment during 2025. Japan attracted $1.11 billion.

So Japan cannot simply presume that the resources available to American frontier laboratories will become available to Japanese laboratories.

But the computational cost of producing a foundation and the computational cost of developing a useful capacity from that foundation are different engineering problems.

Japan has access to increasingly capable open models, post-training techniques, efficient inference architectures, specialized datasets, industrial research environments and the opportunity to develop systems against concrete operational requirements.

Sakana is investigating ways through that field.

There is an important qualification, of course.

This fish is swimming in American water.

Namazu Small begins with a foundation supplied by NVIDIA. Its existence demonstrates domestic development capacity while simultaneously illustrating Japan's continuing dependence upon externally developed technology.

The relevant question is how much of the capacity can survive the disappearance of the supplier, a change in licensing conditions, a disruption in hardware availability, or a divergence in the technological direction of the original developer.

Technological independence is a material achievement that cannot be established by renaming a model.

The Government Buys a Brain.

Japan's Digital Agency is doing something particularly interesting with its Government AI GENAI initiative.

The initiative's large-scale pilot is designed around a government workforce of approximately 180,000 employees. Five domestic foundation-model developers are participating in trials, with models from NTT DATA, Fujitsu and Preferred Networks undergoing evaluation on SAKURA Cloud, Japan's domestically developed Government Cloud provider.

The government has identified three relationships it hopes to establish:

  • Domestic developers receive a dependable source of demand through public procurement.
  • Government workers receive increasingly useful AI systems.
  • Developers receive operational feedback through which those systems can improve.

This addresses a problem that cannot be solved by research funding alone.

A laboratory can produce an excellent model without possessing a reliable route into commercial deployment. Government procurement can provide that route, helping sustain a domestic ecosystem while producing the operational experience necessary for its further development.

The existence of multiple participating models also creates an opportunity to maintain technological alternatives.

That opportunity needs to survive implementation.

Can the government transfer its accumulated records, workflows, evaluations and operational knowledge from one model to another? Can a smaller domestic developer compete against an incumbent that has accumulated years of integration experience? Can an agency reject a system's recommendations without losing access to the infrastructure necessary to perform its work?

Can the government fire its own brain?

This is one of the material tests of bounded finality.

A government can retain complete legal authority over its technology providers while becoming increasingly incapable of exercising that authority. The accumulation of operational dependence can produce an effectively irreplaceable system without the system ever requesting additional power.

The capacity to procure domestic intelligence must be accompanied by the capacity to replace it.

Otherwise, Japan could achieve greater independence from foreign providers by constructing a domestic dependency from which its own institutions cannot escape.

The Hokkaido Gambit.

In Chitose, Hokkaido, Japan is attempting to manufacture its way back into the leading edge of semiconductor production.

Rapidus, founded in 2022, intends to commence commercial production of two-nanometer-generation logic chips during the second half of fiscal 2027.

The project represents an attempt to establish a new path into one of the most technologically demanding manufacturing processes on Earth.

Pilot infrastructure has been established. Partnerships with IBM and other technology companies have helped develop the manufacturing process. In October, Rapidus announced a network of seventeen design partners intended to help potential customers develop chips for its foundry.

But Reuters reported in October that the company still confronts significant difficulties in securing customers and achieving consistently competitive production yields.

The difference between fabricating a functioning advanced chip and manufacturing commercially useful quantities of advanced chips at an acceptable cost is enormous. Rapidus needs to establish the second capacity.

If it succeeds, Japan will have developed something valuable well beyond its own national ambitions: another advanced semiconductor manufacturing route within a globally concentrated supply chain. The benefit would extend to companies and countries that currently possess few viable alternatives.

This is an important distinction in the pursuit of technological security. Creating an additional capable supplier can enlarge the reachable futures of participants throughout an industrial field.

The success of Rapidus could make Japan more valuable to the world while making the world less vulnerable to the loss of any one manufacturing region.

Of course, Rapidus itself depends on international equipment, intellectual property, materials and expertise. The establishment of domestic advanced manufacturing would not establish semiconductor autarky.

Japan does not need to manufacture the entire periodic table within its own borders, but it needs relationships that remain viable when individual components of the international system fail.

Meanwhile, at Haneda.

A little electric tractor is moving luggage around an airport.

This may be one of the more instructive objects in Japan's entire AI strategy.

On December 15, 2025, Japan Airlines commenced commercial operation of a Level 4 autonomous towing tractor at Haneda Airport. The vehicle operates without a driver under specified conditions, supported by cameras and signaling infrastructure installed by the Japan Civil Aviation Bureau.

On October 30, 2026, Japan Airlines and Toyota Industries announced expanded trials involving more difficult routes, including tunnel sections.

Notice where the engineering work is happening.

  • Some of it happens inside the tractor.
  • Some happens in the operating environment.
  • Some happens through the coordination between the vehicle and the airport's shared infrastructure.

The burden of successful autonomous operation has been distributed across the field.

The tractor does not need to acquire a complete understanding of every road, every city and every possible driving condition before it becomes useful.

It needs to move the luggage.

This is familiar territory for the Chastening of the Controller. The environment can participate in the action. Geometry, infrastructure, signaling, local constraints and other material arrangements can reduce the demands placed upon an individual intelligent system.

  • The result may be less spectacular than a humanoid robot demonstrating its ability to perform a dozen unrelated tasks at a technology exhibition.
  • But the robot has a job.

Japan possesses thousands of environments that could be examined in this way: ports, warehouses, hospitals, factories, rail yards, agricultural facilities and distribution centers.

Each presents different physical constraints. Each offers an opportunity to develop bounded automation against a real operational problem.

There's no need to wait for artificial general intelligence before recovering some of these threatened continuations.

The tractor is already carrying the luggage.

The Sun Requires Electricity.

Japan's energy self-sufficiency rate stood at 16.3% in 2024.

According to the International Energy Agency's October 2026 assessment, fossil fuels still account for more than 80% of the country's total energy supply. Imported liquefied natural gas provides approximately one-third of its electricity generation.

After a decade of declining electricity demand, the IEA anticipates renewed growth driven partly by semiconductor manufacturing and data centers.

Japan's AI strategy is therefore also an energy strategy.

The country cannot independently pursue semiconductor expansion, computational expansion, industrial electrification, energy affordability and decarbonization as though each were operating upon a different island.

Japan is pursuing a broad combination of renewable energy, nuclear generation, efficiency improvements and emerging energy technologies. Its capacity to realize those projects will condition the scale and cost of its AI ambitions.

A domestic data center powered through an increasingly vulnerable supply of imported fuel may reduce one dependency while thickening another.

And an expansion of industrial electricity consumption that increases the cost of maintaining ordinary households or smaller businesses can transfer the burden of technological advancement onto the very loci the advancement was intended to help.

The energy bill belongs inside the audit.

So does the land, the water, the transmission infrastructure, the surrounding communities and the environmental cost of the chosen generation path.

Japan's interest in computational efficiency, smaller models and specialized physical intelligence has particular relevance here.

Intelligence that requires less material expenditure to carry a given function can change which deployments remain economically and ecologically reachable.

The country needs useful intelligence, not necessarily need the largest possible quantity of it.

Who Gets the Robot?

Suppose Japan achieves a substantial portion of its industrial ambitions.

Advanced chips are produced in Hokkaido. Domestic foundation models improve. Robotic manufacturers develop machines capable of performing increasingly sophisticated work. Japanese exports recover technological ground.

What happens to the nursing home?

There are at least two ways a nursing home might introduce robotic assistance.

  • In one, machines reduce the physical burden of lifting patients, carrying equipment, cleaning rooms and performing administrative work.
    • Caregivers can spend more time with residents.
    • Fewer workers suffer injuries.
    • Elderly people retain access to care that would otherwise become increasingly difficult to provide.
  • In the other, the same efficiency gains justify staffing reductions.
    • The remaining caregivers inherit larger workloads.
    • Human contact becomes scarcer.
    • Residents with needs that the machines cannot adequately accommodate become increasingly burdensome to the institution.

Both nursing homes could report impressive automation statistics.

The equipment might even be identical.

The ethical difference lies in the transition undergone by the people within them.

This is why one should resist evaluating Japan's AI strategy exclusively through national economic performance. The national economy can expand while the reachable continuations of particular people, communities and institutions contract.

The country's smaller businesses present a related challenge. A multinational manufacturer can afford a team of engineers to integrate, customize and maintain an advanced robotic system. The owner of a small regional business probably cannot.

If access to useful automation requires capital, expertise and infrastructure that only the largest organizations possess, Japan could develop an enormously successful AI industry while allowing much of its domestic economic fabric to disappear.

A collection of magnificent technological islands would remain.

The society connecting them could continue to contract.

The success of the Japanese strategy therefore depends partly upon the development of technologies that ordinary institutions can actually afford, operate, maintain and replace.

The ten-millionth robot is considerably less important than where the robots went, what they carried, and what remained possible for the people around them.

Indispensable.

There is one word in the November 25 announcement that deserves particular scrutiny.

Japan wishes to become indispensable to other countries.

There are two substantially different ways of accomplishing this.

  • A country can develop extraordinarily valuable capacities that allow other countries to do things they could not previously do.
    • It can contribute to the maintenance of global infrastructure, create alternative supply routes, reduce the cost of useful technologies and develop durable relationships of mutual support.
      • Its importance grows through what it enables.
  • Alternatively, a country can organize the surrounding field so that other countries lose access to viable alternatives.
    • Technological chokepoints, proprietary dependencies and exclusive control over essential resources can make an industrial relationship increasingly difficult to escape.
      • Its importance grows through what others cannot do without it.

Both can generate economic and geopolitical leverage.

Only the first architecture is inherently directed toward expanding the capacities of the surrounding participants, and even that architecture can contain local harms and dependencies.

Japan's pursuit of technological autonomy encounters a choice here. The reduction of its own vulnerability does not need to require the construction of equivalent vulnerabilities elsewhere.

The country could make itself valuable through the proliferation of additional technological paths. It could supply alternatives to existing dependencies, creating a more resilient international industrial field in the process.

The alternative would be to reproduce, under Japanese ownership, the very structure of dependence its strategy seeks to overcome.

2027.

On November 6, Japan's government narrowed its investment priorities, identifying twenty-eight priority products and technologies from its broader industrial strategy.

A more detailed implementation plan is expected before the end of the year. The five years beginning in April 2027 are intended to constitute a period of intensive investment.

So, what should we actually be looking for over the coming year?

Development Material test
Rapidus Commercial customers, competitive production yields and credible progress toward sustained manufacturing.
Domestic AI Demonstrable research and operational capabilities beyond the performance inherited from foreign foundations.
Physical AI Machines performing sustained commercial work outside demonstrations.
Public administration Improved services alongside independent evaluation and provider replaceability.
Smaller businesses Affordable automation that can be deployed and maintained without extraordinary resources.
Care and healthcare Improved access, working conditions and quality of care.
Energy Reliable additional capacity without intolerable costs transferred elsewhere.
Industrial independence Critical functions remaining viable through disruptions to foreign suppliers.

The end of 2027 will be too early to determine the success of a strategy extending through 2040, but it will be a useful point at which to distinguish three emerging futures.

  • The Industrial Renaissance: Japan develops and integrates sufficient computational, manufacturing and energy capacity to relieve the structural pressures of demographic decline while establishing a new generation of internationally competitive industries.
  • The Intelligent Subcontractor: Japan manufactures sophisticated AI-enabled machinery while the decisive intelligence, computational infrastructure and software ecosystems remain controlled elsewhere.
  • The Subsidized Archipelago: Advanced factories, research laboratories and impressive demonstrations accumulate while smaller businesses, regional communities and public services continue losing the capacities necessary for their own continuation.

Japan could exhibit all three developments simultaneously.

The test will be which of them becomes materially dominant, which remains correctable, and which loci are made to carry the cost.

Ruling.

There is a credible industrial path through which Japan could become one of the most consequential AI economies in the world.

Its manufacturing expertise, robotics industry, accumulated operational data, domestic research capacity and existing infrastructure give it a substantial foundation from which to develop physical intelligence.

Its demographic conditions also provide an unusually clear field of application.

The strategy remains vulnerable to Chinese industrial competition, American technological dependence, semiconductor manufacturing difficulties, energy constraints, the uneven distribution of technological access and the possibility that efficiency gains become instruments of further extraction.

Japan's ambitions do not resolve any of these problems by declaration.

But they bring resources and institutional attention toward problems that possess identifiable material paths of intervention.

The ambition of becoming the world's third great AI nation is therefore an interesting one, though Modal Path Ethics is considerably more interested in what Japan might accomplish along the way.

A country could become the third greatest AI power on Earth while its elderly become increasingly isolated, its smaller communities disappear, its workers inherit more precarious lives and its government loses the capacity to function independently of a handful of technological providers.

A country could also fail to achieve third place while recovering enormous regions of threatened continuation.

The rank cannot adjudicate those futures.

Japan enters 2027 with fewer people than it once expected to have, an industrial body built over generations, and a new class of instruments through which that body might become capable of considerably more.

The industrial past cannot be restored through nostalgia.

But the factories are still standing.

The railways are still running.

The engineers are still designing.

And somewhere in Haneda Airport, a little electric tractor is carrying somebody's luggage.
