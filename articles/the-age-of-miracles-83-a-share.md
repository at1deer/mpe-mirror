# The Age of Miracles, $83 a Share

On November 13, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei told JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon that his company follows a simple philosophy: **underpromise and overdeliver.** Anthropic doesn't announce miraculous new models and hope to produce them later, he explained. It builds them, tests them, and releases them when they're ready.

A sensible business practice. Especially for a company whose products occasionally appear capable of things their predecessors could not accomplish a few months earlier.

Six days later, Anthropic went public at $83 a share. Its offering closed November 20, generating approximately $93.5 billion in gross IPO proceeds, $91.6 billion of which went to Anthropic before expenses, alongside a $26 billion private placement.

And in the [founders' letter](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026072949/anthropicpbc-fwpipowebsi.htm?ref=modalpathethics.com) accompanying the offering, Amodei and his six co-founders invited investors to join them in bringing about an **“age of miracles.”**

This is an impressive amount of ambition for a company committed to underpromising.

Also not necessarily a contradiction.

Anthropic is making two different kinds of promises.

-   The first concerns what its products can do today.
-   The second concerns what artificial intelligence might make possible tomorrow.
    -   The first can be tested before release.
    -   The second cannot.

And Anthropic has just raised enough capital to help determine whether its second promise comes true.

* * *

## The Exponential.

Anthropic's optimism is not unsupported speculation.

Its founders include researchers responsible for some of the foundational work on neural scaling laws: empirical relationships showing how aspects of model performance improve as training data, model size, and computational resources increase.

These relationships have proved extremely useful. They helped turn the construction of increasingly capable language models from an uncertain research ambition into something approaching an engineering discipline.

Amodei has spent years making the further argument that sustained improvements in AI could soon produce systems capable of accelerating scientific research, transforming medicine, and substantially expanding humanity's productive capacity.

There is increasingly serious evidence that AI development is already accelerating itself.

On November 5, the independent evaluation organization [METR published an assessment](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/?ref=modalpathethics.com) of Anthropic's internal research operations. It estimated that the company's model capabilities, measured on an internal benchmark index, had been advancing at around 1.9 times their previous rate. In effect, Anthropic had achieved about two years of earlier benchmark progress in one year.

METR attributed much of that acceleration to the use of AI in AI research.

That is a remarkable result. It is also narrower than the conclusion some might draw from it.

The assessment primarily concerned well-specified tasks. METR reported that AI assistance, rather than fully autonomous scientific research, was still responsible for most observed improvements. Significant supporting evidence was redacted, and the evaluators acknowledged considerable uncertainty.

We have evidence of accelerating capability development. We do not yet have proof of an unlimited intelligence explosion.

More importantly, neither proposition is equivalent to a prediction of human flourishing.

A scaling relationship between training resources and model performance does not independently establish how those capabilities will translate into scientific discoveries, economic institutions, employment, or human welfare.

There are a few inferential steps between a model becoming better at solving problems and society becoming better at living with the solutions, as Modal Path Ethics has been studying.

Anthropic's [IPO prospectus](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074944/anthropicpbc-424b4.htm?ref=modalpathethics.com) acknowledges some of this uncertainty. Its risk disclosures warn that the benefits of increased scale may diminish, plateau, or fail to materialize under new conditions.

Yet the same prospectus forecasts the widespread availability, within a few years, of AI systems matching or exceeding top human experts across most professional and scientific disciplines.

These statements can coexist. One describes a commercial and technological expectation; the other admits that the underlying evidence may not support indefinite extrapolation.

But admitting uncertainty is not the same as resolving it.

An **exponential** describes an observed relationship.

It does not, by itself, describe a destiny.

## Hating the Game.

Cal Newport is considerably less impressed.

In his November 19 _New York Times_ essay, [“Don't Play Anthropic's Game,”](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/11/19/opinion/anthropic-ipo-ai-regulation.html?ref=modalpathethics.com) the Georgetown computer scientist describes Anthropic as something resembling a doomsday cult that also makes enterprise software.

His objection is not just that its founders might be wrong about superintelligence. It is that their convictions are becoming inseparable from their commercial enterprise.

Anthropic anticipates a civilization-transforming technology that could bring either extraordinary benefits or catastrophic harm. It believes it must help guide that transformation responsibly.

Meanwhile, developing the technology requires enormous investments, rapid technical progress, and sufficient commercial success to remain competitive.

The danger is that the mission can become its own justification.

If humanity's future depends on getting the technology right, and getting it right depends on your company remaining at the frontier, almost any extraordinary investment can begin to look like a moral necessity.

Newport is right to question this arrangement. He is also right that speculative forecasts should not become authoritative simply because the people making them have impressive scientific credentials and ridiculously large bank accounts.

But dismissing the superintelligence narrative as a kind of technological religion presents its own problem.

Anthropic has real empirical evidence for its expectations. The underlying research deserves serious consideration. And even if Newport is correct that the strongest forecasts will never materialize, his objection does not remove the consequences of pursuing them.

This is the point at which the disagreement becomes more interesting than either side's prediction.

-   Newport asks whether we should believe Anthropic's extraordinary future.
-   Amodei asks whether we are prepared to build it.

There is a third question.

> **What happens to the futures we might otherwise have built?**

> Consider two researchers who independently forecast that a particular technological breakthrough is ten years away.

-   **One** publishes the forecast in an academic journal.
-   **The other** secures tens of billions of dollars in financing,
    -   hires thousands of researchers,
        -   reserves enormous computing capacity,
            -   and reorganizes an industry around achieving it.

These two may hold identical beliefs about the future. They are not performing identical acts.

The second forecast has become an intervention.

Anthropic's founders are unusually explicit about this. Their letter explains that the company intends to make investments, particularly in computing infrastructure, that would seem excessive under ordinary business assumptions. Those commitments make sense, they argue, because the exponential trajectory of AI implies much greater economic value in the near future.

This is not simply a wager on an external event. The investment itself changes the probability, timing, and character of the event being anticipated.

More computing capacity can enable additional experiments. More research funding can accelerate technical progress. More capable models can improve the tools researchers use to construct their successors. The evidence from METR suggests that at least part of this feedback process is already operating.

A forecast that attracts sufficient resources can help produce the conditions for its own confirmation.

That does not make the forecast false. Nor does it prove that the forecast is self-fulfilling. Discoveries still have to be made, and physical, technical, and economic constraints remain.

It does mean that we should be careful when interpreting eventual success as proof that the predicted trajectory was inevitable.

Suppose Anthropic's expectations prove substantially correct. Powerful AI arrives on something like Amodei's proposed timetable. Scientific progress accelerates and enormous new economic possibilities emerge.

We would have good reason to credit the researchers, engineers, and investors who helped make that possible.

We would not thereby establish that the same future was the only available one, or that its particular distribution of benefits and costs was necessary.

The path taken to an outcome matters independently of the outcome itself.

And if Anthropic's predictions prove wrong, the consequences do not simply disappear.

The infrastructure will have been built. Capital and skilled labor will have been committed. Institutions will have reorganized around particular expectations. Other research programs and economic priorities may have received less attention because the promise of an AI exponential attracted resources elsewhere.

An inaccurate prediction backed by sufficient capital can still permanently change the world.

The IPO does not expose investors to the risk that Anthropic's vision will fail. It finances an attempt to make that vision real.

The market offered an almost immediate reminder that the future remains pretty difficult to price.

By its second trading day, Anthropic had surrendered most of its initial gains, briefly falling to just five cents above its $83 offering price before closing at $88.91. The _Wall Street Journal_ reported a burgeoning blame game over share allocations, employee selling, and underwriting decisions.

None of these disputes tells us whether Amodei's technological predictions are correct. They do, however, illustrate the distance between anticipating an extraordinary future and successfully financing its arrival.

## Outside the Prospectus.

Modal Path Ethics begins from a concern that conventional evaluations of decisions often neglect: the structure of reachable futures available to those affected by an intervention.

The question is what a transition makes reachable, what it makes difficult to reach, and what it renders unavailable for each locus in the affected field.

An investment in AI infrastructure can open many valuable paths: new treatments, cheaper scientific experimentation, more accessible expertise, forms of productive activity not previously feasible.

It can also impose commitments and dependencies that make alternative paths harder to pursue. These effects do not need to be symmetrical.

And importantly, the possibilities that disappear cannot always be reconstructed afterward.

A research program abandoned today may not restart automatically when its competitor fails. Institutional capacity, specialized knowledge, and the people who might have developed them are not resources that remain perfectly preserved while everyone waits to see whether the winning forecast comes true.

This is not an argument against technological ambition. Any significant technological development alters the possibilities available to society. Roads, antibiotics, electricity, and the internet all reorganized the futures that followed them.

Nor is it an argument that every foreclosed possibility should be preserved indefinitely. Some paths are harmful, some are redundant, and some are worth sacrificing to enable something better.

The ethical problem arises when confidence in one anticipated future is treated as sufficient justification for reorganizing the possibilities of everyone else.

Anthropic's IPO makes the problem unusually visible because the company's extraordinary expectations are themselves part of the rationale for its extraordinary investment.

The founders are candid about their motivations. They believe the upside will be immense, that the risks require serious precautions, and that their unusual corporate mission is necessary to navigate both.

These beliefs may be sincere. They may even be correct.

But sincerity does not establish that the choices made in pursuit of a forecast are justified. And accuracy does not retroactively settle the question.

That requires examining the paths the pursuit actually opens and closes, the burdens it distributes, and the opportunities for revision that remain as its commitments grow.

## Underpromise, Overdeliver.

There is a charitable interpretation of Amodei's philosophy worth preserving.

Anthropic has reason to avoid exaggerated product announcements. Its founders acknowledge uncertainty in the technology, disclose material investment risks, and explicitly warn shareholders that the company may sacrifice profits in pursuit of its public-benefit commitments.

That is considerably more responsible than pretending the future is guaranteed.

It is also important not to confuse an ambitious forecast with a contractual promise. The company has not guaranteed investors that a miraculous future will arrive.

But the central tension holds.

Anthropic can refrain from promising a model until it has built and tested that model. It cannot apply the same method to its civilization-wide ambitions. Those require decisions in the present precisely because their eventual results cannot yet be tested. And those decisions affect people who are not shareholders, who have not accepted Anthropic's forecasts, and who may prefer other technological futures.

-   Newport is right to resist the demand that we accept Anthropic's vision on its own terms.
-   Amodei is right that the evidence of AI progress is too substantial to dismiss because its implications sound fantastical.

Neither position resolves the central ethical difficulty.

We are not watching to see which forecast wins. We are already living through the consequences of choosing to pursue particular forecasts over others.

If the _age of miracles_ arrives, we will still need to ask how it was made, what alternatives were sacrificed, and who was permitted to participate in choosing the path.

If it does not, those same questions will remain.

The price of Anthropic's future was $83 a share. The cost of pursuing it cannot be measured in quite the same way.

It includes possibilities that may never receive a prospectus of their own.

## The Ruling.

There is a problem with the argument about foreclosed futures.

It can become much too generous toward the futures we imagine losing, and much too forgiving toward the one we were already inhabiting.

In August, I wrote [_The Past Is Not in the Option Set_](https://modalpathethics.com/the-past-is-not-in-the-option-set/) against the idea that civilization could escape its technological predicament by retreating into an earlier one. The dependencies and damage remain. Eight billion people do not become nineteenth-century villagers because we have decided that industrial society was a terrible mistake.

The past is not an available alternative.

And if we're being honest, the futures we were already pursuing were not coming along particularly well, were they?

Climate change was accelerating. Nuclear competition was intensifying. Ecological damage was accumulating. Governments were struggling to coordinate across borders while institutions became increasingly proficient at pursuing objectives that made their shared circumstances worse.

Artificial intelligence did not invent those problems. It arrived inside them.

In October, [_The Trees Are Still Standing_](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-the-trees-are-still-standing/) examined exactly this predicament. Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark had declared that the singularity had begun, while the physical infrastructure supporting AI's acceleration was already transforming forests and landscapes.

The important question was never whether technological progress should defeat nature, or nature should defeat technological progress.

It was whether the next thing to be built had to destroy something valuable that was already there.

In Finland, a forest had been cleared for a proposed datacenter. In West Virginia, mature American chestnuts remained alive at a nursery that a separate developer intended to acquire. The state proposed relocating the trees, despite the absence of any documented successful mature American chestnut transplants known to the American Chestnut Foundation.

One loss had already occurred. The other intervention remained open to examination.

The important distinction was not between an _artificial future_ and a _natural past_. It was between a path that had already closed and one that could still be preserved.

The preservation of possibility is still not the preservation of every imaginable possibility.

Newport is correct that Anthropic's founders have no special entitlement to govern humanity because they anticipate its technological future. Their prediction, however plausible, does not confer jurisdiction.

But rejecting their prediction does not restore a better world. Nor does declining the technological wager relieve us of the problems that made new capabilities so attractive in the first place.

We still need more effective medicine, scientific discoveries, functioning institutions, climate adaptation, and ways of coordinating systems that have become unbelievably capable of damaging one another.

If AI can materially expand our ability to address those problems, refusing to develop it carries ethical consequences of its own.

The alternative is not automatically safer because it requires fewer decisions.

A future needs a reachable path, not just an attractive description. The continuation of an existing trajectory is not morally privileged because it requires no visible intervention.

Nor does the possibility of future progress justify destroying functioning systems that could have been preserved.

We must compare what is actually available.

Anthropic's investment may enable discoveries that would otherwise remain inaccessible. Its accelerating capabilities may help civilization escape some of its inherited constraints. Those are substantial reasons to continue the work. But a promised future does not excuse a demonstrably unnecessary loss in the present.

-   The evidence for accelerating AI progress is sufficiently serious to warrant continued development.
-   The evidence for inevitable superintelligence, universal prosperity, or Anthropic's unique fitness to deliver either remains insufficient to authorize a surrender of judgment.

So the ruling is not to preserve the existing order at all costs. That order has already demonstrated its capacity to contract the futures available to the people and environments depending on it.

Nor to hand the future to Anthropic because it might be right about scaling laws.

And not to demand certainty before undertaking a transition that is already underway.

-   Amodei may be right about the scale of the coming transformation.
-   Newport may be right about the dangers of granting extraordinary authority to those who predict it.

Neither answer determines which path we should take next.

That remains a question about the world as it exists, with its accumulated damage, functioning dependencies, unresolved obligations, and remaining possibilities.

The IPO has not purchased the future. But it has financed one attempt to change it.

-   Its success will not establish that every sacrifice was necessary.
-   Its failure will not establish that the attempt should never have been made.

And we should resist the comforting fiction that history offers us an undamaged alternative waiting patiently to be selected.

Monday morning is still on the way.
