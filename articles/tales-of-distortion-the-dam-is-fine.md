# Tales of Distortion: The Dam Is Fine

On the morning of October 10, 1963, the Vajont Dam was still standing.

And the center of Longarone was gone.

The dam was a double-curved concrete arch, about 262 meters high, built across a narrow gorge in the mountains of northeastern Italy. The pressure of the reservoir was carried through the curved wall into the rock on either side.

The gorge provided the abutments; the concrete provided the arch. Together these two formed one of the most remarkable hydroelectric structures of its time.

The morning after the disaster, the arch was still in the gorge.

The road along its crest had been torn away. Nearby control structures and equipment had been destroyed. The reservoir above it no longer occupied the same valley in quite the same way. Much of one mountainside now lay where the water used to go.

But the wall had not burst. There had been no ordinary failure in which water pressure broke through a weak section of concrete and emptied the reservoir through a hole.

The water had gone **over** the dam.

This is an incredibly inconvenient distinction, especially if the existence of that dam had encouraged anyone to think that the places below it were protected from the water held above it.

To understand what happened, we need to look very closely at three objects.

-   A **mountain**.
-   A **lake**.
-   And a **wall**.

* * *

-   The **mountain** was **Monte Toc**, rising along the southern side of the Vajont valley.
-   The **lake** was artificial, held within that valley as part of a larger hydroelectric system.
-   The **wall** closed the narrow gorge through which the Vajont stream had once descended toward the Piave River.

Below the gorge was Longarone.

**Longarone** had streets, a railway station, a brewery, a timber depot, shops, a parish church, and squares with names. People used those to work, travel, worship, buy food, keep appointments, and return home.

Photographs preserved by survivors show the town before October 9, with its roofs and public buildings arranged along the Piave valley.

Beyond Longarone were **Pirago**, **Villanova**, **Rivalta**, **Faè**, and other settlements along the river.

Above the dam, the communities of **Erto** and **Casso** occupied a different part of the same landscape. Some houses and hamlets stood low enough to meet the reservoir's water while others sat higher on the slopes.

These distinctions in elevation would shortly become distinctions between survival and devastation.

At **10:39 p.m. on October 9**, part of Monte Toc began moving with catastrophic speed.

Some 260 million cubic meters of rock and earth slid into the reservoir.

This was not a scattering of stones that happened to fall from a cliff. A vast mass of the mountainside moved together. Ground that had supported trees, tracks, and cultivated land became one moving body of rock.

The reservoir had been deliberately lowered as the mountain's movement grew more and more alarming.

-   At the time of the slide, its surface was near **700.42 meters above sea level**.
-   The dam's crest stood around **725.5 meters**.

There were roughly twenty-five meters between the still water and the top of the wall.

The descending mountain drove into the basin and displaced water on a scale that the ordinary relationship between a reservoir level and a dam crest could no longer describe. The water had to move away from the entering rock.

It moved violently in more than one direction.

One surge ran up and along the reservoir toward its **eastern reaches**, striking low hamlets along the shore. The higher center of Erto escaped the worst of this. Lower settlements did not. Water and debris also climbed toward Casso, damaging places beyond the immediate lake margin.

Another surge went west, toward the concrete arch.

It rose **above** the crest, tore away the roadway and nearby installations, and passed over into the narrow gorge beyond. From there the rushing mass of water, air, and debris raced out toward the Piave valley.

The dam was fine. It held its shape. It could not stop water that rose above it.

**Longarone** stood at the end of that descending path.

The flood reached the town with overwhelming force. A burst of wind and spray preceded and accompanied the water. Buildings, roads, and the ordinary connected arrangement of the town were demolished. The wave struck the wider Piave valley and the neighboring settlements; the destruction was concentrated along particular routes and elevations.

About 1,900 people died.

The photographs afterward are difficult to reconcile with the photographs before. The street network is interrupted or absent. Buildings that once identified the town are no longer buildings at all. At the site of Longarone's parish church, later images show little of the structure remaining. A bell was recovered from across the valley.

The dam, meanwhile, could still be photographed.

The dam was still fine.

* * *

There is a temptation to give this event the familiar name of a _dam failure_.

That is definitely useful for some purposes. **Vajont** certainly belongs in the history of catastrophic reservoir engineering and the discussion of dam safety.

But the concrete arch did not fail in the sense that phrase usually suggests.

This disaster occurred through a relation among objects that could each be studied separately:

-   rock,
-   slope,
-   water,
-   reservoir,
-   gorge,
-   dam,
-   power system,
-   settlements,
    -   and the people making decisions about them.

There were instruments, reports, experiments, and decisions before October 9.

This mountain had been examined _extensively_.

Its movement had been measured. Water levels had been changed in response.

Engineers had constructed a special tunnel to preserve the reservoir's functioning if a landslide divided it.

Others had built a physical model of the basin to study the waves a landslide might generate.

These are not the arrangements of a project in which nobody had noticed a mountain was moving.

A photograph of the surviving dam can tell us something important about the strength of its concrete arch. It cannot tell us why the reservoir was placed against that mountain, what they had prepared for, what the scale model could actually reproduce, or which communities the operating decisions were supposed to protect.

For those answers, we have to leave the town standing for a while.

We have to find out why the lake was even there.

* * *

## **The Electricity.**

Before there was ever a lake in the Vajont valley, northern Italy had a problem with **snow**.

This requires some explanation, because snow is not usually considered an obstacle to the production of electricity.

-   **Snow** is water.
-   **Water** can turn turbines.
    -   **The Alps** contain an extraordinary amount of both.

The difficulty was timing.

In the mountains, water accumulated as snow during winter and returned to the rivers when it melted. But the electrical grid had a different schedule. Workshops, factories, trains, mines, lights, and households needed power when they needed it. A steel furnace could not arrange its production calendar around the spring thaw.

Italy relied heavily on hydroelectric power. In the industrial north, winter reduced the water available for generation when industry still needed electricity. The shortage was serious enough to interrupt production.

A [1948 report by the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/581381468049199618/pdf/multi0page.pdf?ref=modalpathethics.com) described northern industrial power supplies falling around **35 percent** below requirements between November and March. Some factories were limited to four working days per week. Aluminium, electrochemical, and other power-hungry industries could be curtailed or shut down for part of the season.

This was a real problem.

There was also a real solution.

> You could save the water.

* * *

A **hydroelectric turbine** does not ask water to be very intelligent. It just needs water to arrive with enough pressure and flow to do mechanical work.

-   A reservoir stores water above the turbines.
    -   Gates and conduits admit it when power is required.
        -   The water passes through the machines,
        -   turning shafts attached to electrical generators.
            -   The electricity leaves by wire.
            -   The water continues downhill.

The useful thing is that the water can be held until the electricity is wanted.

The _less_ useful thing is that a natural river does not provide storage on demand. It just runs through the landscape it has. So if you want millions of cubic meters to remain in a valley until winter, you must close part of the valley.

This can be done with a dam.

The northeastern Italian Alps were well suited to this kind of project in several obvious ways. They had steep descents, large catchments, tributary streams, existing industrial connections, and narrow gorges in which a comparatively short wall could impound a much longer body of water.

They also had towns, roads, farms, pastures, forests, slopes, and people whose lives depended upon the same ground.

We will return to those.

First, the water.

* * *

The **Società Adriatica di Elettricità**, or **SADE**, was a private electrical company based in Venice. It had spent decades acquiring rights and developing hydroelectric works in the region. What eventually became the **Piave–Boite–Maè–Vajont system** did not begin as one titanic dam. It grew through proposals for different rivers, different reservoirs, and different generating stations.

-   The **Piave** was the principal river.
    -   The **Boite**,
    -   **Maè**,
    -   and **Vajont**
        -   entered the larger system through tributary valleys.
    -   **Val Gallina** supplied another regulating basin.

These waterways did not naturally meet in the order most useful to a power company.

So the engineers proposed to connect them.

-   A reservoir on the **Boite** could release water through a tunnel toward a reservoir on the **Piave**.
    -   The **Piave** water could be taken through another long tunnel into the **Vajont** basin.
        -   From there, water could pass through the connected system toward the great generating station at **Soverzene**, where its drop would be converted into electrical power.
            -   After passing through the turbines, some of the water could continue toward **Lake Santa Croce** and be used again farther downstream.

A river that had once followed its own valley could be led down a tunnel into another.

This was the central ingenuity of the undertaking.

The result was an interconnected hydraulic machine distributed across the Alps.

* * *

At **Soverzene**, engineers Carlo Semenza and Mario Mainardis designed a powerhouse whose main machinery would sit inside the mountain. Construction began during the Second World War, stopped in 1943, and resumed after it. The plant entered operation in 1951, years before the great Vajont dam was finished.

A passage roughly half a kilometer long led from the outside entrance into the generating hall. Inside were four large Francis turbines, coupled to generators. This station was designed to act as a _pilot plant_ for the wider system. Its machinery could respond to changing demand while the connected reservoirs supplied the water.

The underground hall was deliberately lit. Its ceiling carried paintings celebrating electricity and the power of water. Careful architectural treatment made a room excavated inside solid rock feel less like a room wrapped in stone.

But the windows and painted sky were not responsible for operating the turbines.

The water was.

By 1951, Soverzene could already turn falling water into electricity. What the growing network needed was a larger, more dependable supply of water to send through it.

The **Vajont valley** offered storage.

That is why it was so important.

Not because its stream alone promised some astonishing new output. The reservoir could hold water brought from elsewhere in the connected network. It could act as a large regulating chamber for a much bigger system.

The lake was being built to serve a machine that extended far beyond it.

* * *

-   In **1940**, SADE filed an application to combine earlier plans for the Piave, Boite, and Vajont rivers.
    -   The proposed Vajont barrier was roughly **200 meters high**, with a reservoir of around **50 million cubic meters**.
-   By **1948**, a more detailed version envisaged a **202-meter dam** and **58.2 million cubic meters** of useful storage at Vajont.

The complete Piave–Boite–Vajont development was expected to provide about **820 million kilowatt-hours a year**, counting the additional generation made possible at downstream stations.

The World Bank's appraisal set out dams, storage volumes, tunnel diameters, generating capacities, construction costs, anticipated electrical output, and the foreign equipment and materials the undertaking would require.

For the planned network, the bank concluded that it was **“well engineered and technically sound.”**

This is worth keeping in its proper place.

Here, the bank was assessing a hydroelectric development in 1948, including the economic and technical case for relieving a shortage of power.

It had no opportunity to approve the much taller Vajont dam that would be proposed years later.

* * *

In **January 1957**, SADE asked to enlarge the Vajont project again.

-   The proposed dam rose from **202 meters** to **266 meters**.
-   The planned maximum water level rose from **677** to **722.5 meters above sea level**.
-   Useful reservoir capacity grew from **58 million** to roughly **150 million cubic meters**.

These numbers describe more than an unusually tall wall.

A **dam** closes a valley at one place, but the water it holds extends backward across a much larger landscape. Raise the allowable water level, and the lake does not simply become deeper against the concrete.

It reaches farther along the valley floor and up its sides.

It touches more land.

More trees.

More slopes.

More of the world that was there before anybody drew the new shoreline.

The revised design promised a much larger store of water, greater usable hydraulic head, and additional generation. This was part of an expanding national effort to supply electricity to an economy that increasingly depended on it. The company sought public authorization, and the state had its own interest in the planned output.

The dams and turbines were real engineering works. The power shortage was real. The storage requirement was real.

And so was the additional valley the reservoir would occupy.

In the network diagrams, **Vajont** was a basin connected to other basins by lines representing tunnels and controlled flows. The water could be counted in cubic meters, scheduled for release, and translated into anticipated kilowatt-hours.

That diagram was very useful for understanding how electricity would be produced.

It did not yet tell us everything about the ground required to hold the lake.

Before we meet the mountain, we need to meet the people who were already living on it.

* * *

## **The Valley Has People.**

A **lake** can separate a family from its fields without taking away the family’s house.

This is one of the less obvious things about making a new lake.

The water does not have to make it to the kitchen. It can just cover the path to the fields, the bridge used by the cattle, or the way a child gets to school.

The house can remain exactly where it was. Everything around it has become farther away.

In 1949, the mayor of Erto and Casso attempted to explain this to the Italian government.

Then, the **Vajont** was still a stream. It ran along the bottom of a narrow valley, with settlements and cultivated ground on both sides. **Erto** and **Casso** stood on the northern slopes. Across the water, on the side of Monte Toc, were hamlets and farm plots belonging to the same municipal world: **Pineda**, **Spianada**, **Ceva**, **Prada**, **Liron**, and smaller clusters of houses.

The two sides were connected.

Four principal mule tracks and three bridges crossed the stream, according to the mayor's petition. People used them to move themselves, their animals, and their produce. A household could live on one side and work land on the other. A village could have a school, a church, and neighbors who were reached by crossing the valley instead of traveling the length of it.

Then SADE proposed to put a reservoir between them.

The bridge was going to need some work.

* * *

The valley was not empty land waiting to become useful.

Its farms were small. Families grew potatoes and other crops on ground that was valuable because level, workable earth was scarce in a steep mountain valley. There were meadows, grazing areas, woods, barns, and buildings used seasonally as well as houses occupied year-round.

People also left the valley to earn money and came back. Men worked on construction sites elsewhere in Italy. Women traveled as itinerant traders. One woman later interviewed by journalist Tina Merlin carried wooden spoons down into the Venetian plain to sell. Those journeys formed part of how households sustained themselves in Erto and Casso.

The families were not living outside the modern economy. They were moving through it by routes that did not appear on a hydroelectric flow diagram.

-   The valley was already a working system.
-   SADE's electrical network would enter it as another one.

Both had roads, but they did not require the same ones.

* * *

On **August 1, 1949**, Mayor Barzan [submitted a formal objection](https://www.visitlongarone.it/vajont/storia-del-vajont/?ref=modalpathethics.com) to the proposed Vajont development.

His municipality counted around 2,270 inhabitants. He described the stream as dividing the municipal territory lengthwise for more than seven kilometers. Some of its most productive agricultural land lay along the two slopes and the lower valley. The new reservoir, he argued, would take homes, fields, and the crossings on which the settlements depended.

He feared that Pineda would become isolated.

He demanded compensation, assistance for those displaced, reconstruction of communications, and a greater share of the benefits derived from the water leaving the area as electrical power.

The petition gave large estimates: **170 rural houses** and roughly **3,000 hectares** of productive land were described as threatened by the proposed works.

A later geographical survey by **Giorgio Valussi**, published in 1963 and reproduced in subsequent historical research, recorded approximately **306 hectares** expropriated from **386 holdings**, and **74 buildings**, including **44 dwellings**.

These are two different counts made for different purposes and moments in the project's history. The smaller completed figures still describe a substantial reorganization of a small agricultural community.

Some displaced households built new houses in Erto. Some bought land in the lowlands and left. Farm buildings tied to land that no longer existed could not always be reproduced by building another house.

An agricultural holding is not maintained by keeping its owners' names in the land register.

* * *

**Longarone**, below the gorge, had a different objection.

On **July 30, 1949**, its mayor, **Giuseppe Celso,** worried that altering the Vajont's contribution to the Piave might change the river's course and erode land near Dogna and Provagna. He asked for protective works along the river.

This was a real hydraulic concern, although it was not a prediction of the catastrophe that would occur fourteen years later.

The two municipalities were looking at different parts of the same undertaking.

-   **Erto and Casso** faced a new lake cutting across their daily movements.
-   **Longarone** faced changes to a river flowing past its settlements.
-   **SADE** faced storage volumes, tunnels, release levels, power production, and the geometry of a dam.

There were going to be consequences outside every one of those descriptions.

* * *

In **1952**, the formal concession terms provided for a replacement crossing.

SADE set out to build a bridge at the site of an existing pedestrian crossing, capable of carrying vehicles weighing 10 to 15 tons. The new structure was meant to reconnect routes that the water would interrupt.

People used the crossing. The lake would remove the crossing. So the project would supply another crossing.

Then the dam got taller.

The enlarged project of 1957 raised the intended maximum reservoir level to **722.5 meters above sea level**. The water would reach higher up the slopes and farther across the valley.

The replacement bridge would now need to span about **306 meters**.

SADE objected to the cost and scale of such a structure. It proposed replacing the crossing with a road running around the lake.

A **road** is a great invention.

A _road around a lake_, however, does not go across the lake.

The municipality and the farmers' association objected. They told the Public Works Council that at least 55 families needed to cross this part of the valley daily for agricultural work and access to schools.

The Public Works Council considered the bridge disproportionate to the service it would provide. Its reasoning was that the longer journey around the reservoir could be offset by faster transportation.

The revised arrangement called for a perimeter road and a one-time contribution toward a bus service on a circuit of 15 kilometers.

On **July 22, 1958**, the council approved the substitution. The terms adopted that October replaced the earlier crossing commitment with road works and a bus contribution whose amount was not specified in the recorded provision.

This was now the plan. In the plan, access had been preserved.

The people using the path were less convinced.

* * *

On **August 19, 1959**, the Committee for the Defense of the Municipality of Erto wrote directly to SADE, copying the prefecture, civil-engineering offices, the Ministry of Public Works, the municipality, and parliamentary representatives.

The committee named the hamlets whose access was at risk: **Pineda**, **Prada**, **Ceva**, **Seveda**, and **Liron**. It said the remaining fertile land lay across the valley and warned that the rising reservoir would soon sever the existing paths.

The proposed road would be long. During winter, the committee warned, portions would be exposed to landslides and avalanches beneath the slopes of Monte Certen.

And then the letter began asking very ordinary questions.

-   Who would send children to school on a day of bad weather?
-   How would a seriously ill person be transported quickly?
-   How would older residents get to the village, attend church, or visit a public place?
-   How would families work their land?

It requested the promised crossing and asked SADE to send someone to inspect the problem in person, with the residents and municipal authorities.

The dispute had already gone beyond the municipal council.

On **May 3, 1959**, residents organized a civil consortium to defend property and communal interests against the reservoir works. **Tina Merlin** reported on it two days later in _L'Unità_, describing roughly 130 heads of household, women and men, joining the effort.

She also described a family with six children displaced during blasting operations and temporarily lodged in a stable. In subsequent court proceedings, a resident named **Carrara** testified that his family had remained in this accommodation for nine months.

Merlin also reported a man from Pineda saying that his house had been burned by German forces during the war, his sons had since left to work abroad, and the project was now taking his field.

She noted fears that the reservoir itself could destabilize the surrounding slopes. Those fears were present in the community, but did not amount to the technical prediction of the exact 1963 landslide, its speed, and the wave that would drown Longarone.

The residents already had plenty to complain about before any final catastrophe was predictable.

Merlin was prosecuted over her reporting.

In **November 1960**, a Milan court acquitted her and her editor. The court found that the public anxiety she had described existed independently of the article, and that her account of the displacement and local fears was supported by testimony.

* * *

By the time the reservoir began filling, the valley had already changed.

Buildings had been removed. Fields had been acquired. New houses stood where families had relocated. Work opportunities arrived with construction, while other ways of earning a living became harder to maintain. A road had been planned where residents had asked for a crossing.

-   SADE had gained a basin capable of holding and transferring water through the Piave hydroelectric network.
-   The residents had gained some compensation, some new roads and houses, and a much longer argument about what counted as an adequate replacement for what they had lost.

Their argument was about a very specific physical question.

> Could people still get from here to there?

A map of the finished reservoir could answer where the water would be.

It could not, on its own, answer that.

And across the proposed lake, above the road that was supposed to replace the bridge, stood **Monte Toc.**

We need to stop and look at the ground.

* * *

## **The Mountain.**

A **mountain** is a very large collection of rocks that have agreed, for the time being, to remain in roughly the same place.

This agreement is much less formal than it appears.

**Mount Toc** rose above the southern side of the Vajont valley. Across the stream were **Casso, Erto,** and the roads and fields we have already met. On the Toc side were the pastures, houses, and tracks that the new reservoir would interrupt. Above them stood a steep mountain. Below them was limestone.

A dam engineer could look at the narrow gorge beneath the mountain and see exactly what he wanted to see: two enormous walls of stone between which to build an arch.

That was true enough.

* * *

The mountains here were made from layers of sediment deposited over immense periods of time, when the region that would become the Alps occupied a very different part of the world. Limestone accumulated. Other layers included marl, chert, and thinner clay-rich material. Later, movements of the Earth's crust folded, tilted, fractured, and raised the rock.

A cross-section through Mount Toc would therefore not show a single enormous limestone block resting on a convenient flat floor.

You would see beds of different material, bent and broken, with surfaces along which water could enter and forces could act.

The individual limestone beds could be quite strong.

A rock sample could withstand considerable pressure. The rock at the gorge where the dam would be anchored could perform exactly as an engineer had hoped.

But a mountain slope contains many other relations.

-   A layer may dip toward the valley.
-   A fracture may connect the surface to rock much deeper down.
-   Water may pass through one part of the mountain and collect along another.

A thin, weaker seam may matter more to movement than a thick bed of strong stone.

Whether an actual landslide can occur depends on how these parts meet, what is supporting what, and how the conditions change.

The material has a history. The slope has its shape.

And the reservoir was about to introduce a new body of water along its foot.

* * *

**Giorgio Dal Piaz** had been examining the Vajont valley since the first plans for a reservoir.

In **1928**, he assessed the general geological conditions and judged them comparable to those of other mountain basins in the region. Further reports followed in **1937**, **1940**, and **1948**.

For the **202-meter** dam proposed at the time, his investigations were favorable. The rock near the chosen dam site appeared competent to carry the structure.

This was an important assessment. The project required an exceptionally large concrete arch to transfer its load into the sides of a narrow gorge. Bad abutments would have been an immediate problem.

Dal Piaz's reports carried substantial professional authority. He was a distinguished geologist with a long working relationship with the project.

And his judgments also addressed the wider valley. In a 1948 report devoted to possible slope movements resulting from the proposed reservoir and changes in its level, Dal Piaz acknowledged areas with uncertain debris and the possibility of smaller slides.

Dal Piaz did not identify the enormous moving mass that would later enter the lake.

There is a difference between recognizing that some material may fall and identifying a part of the mountainside capable of moving as one catastrophic entity.

That smaller possibility could be accommodated within a reservoir project.

The larger one required a whole different conversation.

* * *

Then SADE enlarged the project.

The proposed dam rose from **202** to **266 meters**, with the planned reservoir reaching substantially higher and farther along the valley. In 1957 the company sought approval for the revised work, relying initially on Dal Piaz's earlier geological assessments and additions to them.

An interesting piece of correspondence survives from that February.

On **February 6, 1957**, Dal Piaz wrote to chief engineer **Carlo Semenza** about an additional geological statement for the enlarged Vajont scheme.

He was unhappy with his own draft and asked Semenza to send the wording the engineer had previously described to him. He also asked whether the statement should carry the current date or an earlier one.

The following day, Semenza sent proposed text and suggested the date **January 31**. Dal Piaz was to complete and sign the statement.

These letters do not establish by themselves that the geology was fabricated or that every opinion in the signed report came from the engineer.

They do establish that the document's wording and dating were being discussed between the person designing the dam and the geologist whose judgment would support it.

The report was to be written by the geologist. The engineer had been invited to help write it.

Meanwhile, the mountain retained its original arrangement.

* * *

In **June 1957**, the Superior Council of Public Works gave a favorable opinion of the enlarged project, accompanied by a requirement for further geological investigation to protect people and public works near the reservoir's maximum level.

The approval and the request for additional safety studies existed in the same process.

An engineering decision could move forward while the examination of the surrounding slopes remained unfinished.

The dam had a site. The reservoir had an authorized design under consideration.

The rest of the valley still had questions.

One of the people asking them was **Leopold Müller**, an Austrian specialist in rock mechanics consulted by SADE. In a report dated **August 6, 1957**, he identified heavily fractured, deteriorated rock along the future reservoir's left bank, including ground beneath pastures and farmhouses. He considered some parts susceptible to sliding.

The _left bank_ here means the southern side when facing downstream: the Mount Toc side. It is the same side on which the residents had been promised that perimeter road.

Müller was not reporting that 260 million cubic meters would move in a matter of seconds in October 1963. His early concerns were more limited.

But the competent stone of the dam gorge and the fractured stone of the reservoir slope had now both entered the engineering record.

As different observations of different parts of the project.

* * *

The planned road gave the geologists another reason to inspect the mountain.

In **October 1958**, Dal Piaz studied the ground along the proposed perimeter route, which was being offered up as a replacement for the crossing that the reservoir would drown.

He described fractured rock and deep cracks around **Pian della Pozza**, a bench or hollow on the north-facing slope of Mount Toc.

Some fractures ran roughly parallel to the valley.

Yet he did not interpret the visible forms as evidence of a great ancient landslide.

He expected localized movements rather than the collapse of a vast section of slope.

The same ground was carrying two kinds of information.

-   It had cracks, which could be seen and mapped.
-   It also had an unknown history, which had to be inferred from what those cracks, folds, and rock masses meant together.

A **crack** in a mountain can be a serious sign. It can also be an old fracture in rock that is no longer moving.

A **depression** can be a feature left by dissolving limestone. It can also be part of a folded or displaced rock mass.

The photograph does not label the difference for you. Neither does the mountain.

In the 1958 assessment, the evidence had not been assembled into an old, large slide that might move again.

A road could still be planned along the slope.

* * *

In the summer of **1959**, the inquiry became more demanding.

A landslide at another SADE reservoir had prompted closer attention to what the banks of Vajont might do. Carlo Semenza brought Müller into a more systematic program and commissioned geological fieldwork from his son, **Edoardo Semenza**. **Franco Giudici** joined the detailed survey later in the year.

Edoardo was a geologist. Carlo was the dam's engineer. They had different jobs, however inconveniently similar their names may be.

Edoardo went onto the mountain.

He examined the ground around Pian del Toc and Pian della Pozza, the small and larger benches visible across the valley from Casso. He walked the fractures, inspected the rock, and followed changes in the orientation of the beds.

One depression at Pian della Pozza had been understood as a _doline,_ a kind of hollow of that can form when water dissolves limestone underground.

Edoardo considered a different explanation.

The rock layers might be folded down into the shape of a basin: a _syncline_.

Such a fold could belong to the deformation of a mass that had moved.

Then there was **Colle Isolato**, an isolated mass of rock on the opposite side of the gorge. Its beds were disturbed, with some turned steeply or even overturned. Broken material and deposits beneath it suggested that the rock's position might have a different history from the cliff behind it.

> _Why would a mass of disturbed rock be sitting across the valley from Mount Toc?_

Edoardo developed a possible answer.

A section of the mountain might already have slid in prehistoric times, obstructing the Vajont stream. The stream could then have cut a new channel through or around this old deposit, leaving parts of the displaced rock isolated on the other side.

The rock would have moved long before there was a dam on Earth.

The stream would have kept working.

And the valley would have taken the appearance that the engineers now encountered.

On **August 26, 1959**, Edoardo sketched this possible sequence in his field notebook. His observations were communicated to SADE technicians by the end of the month; a formal geological study, incorporating further work with Giudici, would follow in June 1960.

This was a hypothesis about the mountain's possible history. Later geological researchers have disagreed over the precise existence, shape, and mechanical role of the proposed prehistoric slide.

The pertinent fact in 1959 is that identifiable features of the ground had acquired another interpretation.

And this one required the possibility of an enormous unstable mass to be investigated.

-   A hollow could be a collapsed limestone cavity.
-   Or it could be part of a displaced mountainside.

That difference would mean a great deal to the people below.

* * *

By the end of that summer, this project had several geological accounts.

-   The rock holding the dam appeared strong enough for the arch.
-   Portions of the reservoir's southern slope were visibly fractured.
    -   One expert had judged the likely slides limited.
    -   Another had warned about susceptible rock.
-   A younger geologist had assembled a hypothesis in which much of this landscape was the remains of a much older movement.

These reports had different objects, different depths, and different uncertainties.

There was no single observation that could settle the mountain.

Further surveys, boreholes, and measurements would be needed. Even then, the question would remain whether the proposed reservoir could safely occupy this valley, under the water levels and operating conditions actually intended.

Before we follow those investigations, we have to return to the event that helped set them in motion.

On **March 22, 1959**, at another reservoir owned by the same company, a mountainside entered the water.

A wave followed.

A man died.

* * *

## **Pontesei.**

In March 1959, a road above a reservoir in northern Italy began coming apart.

This is pretty much always a serious development for a road.

A **road** can be narrow, badly surfaced, difficult to maintain, and inconveniently located. It can climb a mountain by a sequence of turns that seem designed mainly to remind the driver that the mountain was here first.

But a **road** is supposed to stay where it was built.

At Pontesei, the road was attached to a hillside that had begun moving.

The cracks were not a defect confined to the asphalt.

They were the surface of a much larger problem.

And below that road was a lake.

* * *

The **Pontesei reservoir** lay in the **Val di Zoldo**, on the **Maè**, another tributary of the **Piave**. It was a working part of SADE's hydroelectric system, built during the same period as the great dam at Vajont. Its dam, completed in **1957**, stood about **93 meters** high. The planned storage was on the order of nine million cubic meters.

Compared with Vajont, this was a small reservoir.

Compared with most things, this was an enormous fucking body of water.

The water was held against steep mountain slopes. On the left bank, near **Fagarè**, part of the hillside had a history of instability. There were old masses of displaced material, fractured rock, and signs of deeper deformation.

A road crossed the affected ground. Other roads and work tracks ran around the basin and toward the power works.

The different roads occupied different sides of the water.

-   If the hillside moved, the road on it might be destroyed.
-   If the hillside moved _into the lake_, something could happen to a road on the other side as well.

* * *

The slope had issued warnings before.

In **August 1957**, a fissure appeared on the mountainside. Its movement was observed and recorded. During one period of rapid lowering of the reservoir, the measured displacement reached roughly **35 centimeters**.

That did not establish a simple law under which lowering water would always accelerate this particular slope, but it showed that the ground was responding under changing conditions.

Then the movement slowed. From March into October **1958**, the monitored displacement appeared to stop. Observation was suspended during the quieter period.

This, too, was a real observation.

-   The mountain had moved.
    -   Then, at the points being watched, it appeared to have stopped.

There is a very large difference between those two readings and this third bullet:

-   **The mountain has finished moving.**

At the beginning of March **1959**, melting snow and new fractures brought the problem back into view. Cracks appeared along the road.

On **March 19**, as the reservoir was being drawn down, the ground's movement became more conspicuous. The developing fracture, subsidence, and displacement were no longer easy to regard as a road-maintenance problem.

-   The reservoir was lowered.
-   The slope was watched.
-   People inspected the damage.

There was action before the disaster.

There was also uncertainty about how much mountain was involved and how quickly it could move.

Measurements did not supply the speed and shape of what would occur on Sunday morning.

* * *

On **March 22, 1959**, shortly before seven in the morning, an engineer named **Camillo Linari** and a surveyor named **Marinello** were making their way along a service road on the other side of the reservoir.

They had been alerted to the worsening cracks. The **SS 251**, the state road affected by the unstable slope, had already been closed because its pavement was badly damaged.

The men on the service track were looking across water at a hillside whose movements had become alarming.

A third man approached on a bicycle.

His name was **Arcangelo Tiziani**. He was 55, a carpenter employed by the firm Cargnel, working on construction associated with the hydroelectric installation. He was headed toward the worksite buildings.

The three men were now on a route that did not cross the unstable slope.

This distinction was about to become insufficient.

Across the reservoir, the hillside began to fail.

The visible ground accelerated. Rock and loose material moved downward.

Witnesses later disagreed about how to divide the time of the event: the most violent collapse was described in seconds, while movement from its rapid onset to the mass coming to rest was estimated at roughly two to three minutes.

The slide displaced several million cubic meters of material into the lake.

There is no single settled volume in the accounts. Contemporary reporting put it at no less than two million cubic meters. Later histories often use three million.

A modern geological survey gives approximately **4.5–5 million.**

For the people on the opposite shore, the uncertainty was not about whether enough cubic meters of material had entered the water.

It had.

And the lake rose toward them.

* * *

A **reservoir** does not have to rush through a broken dam to become dangerous.

Put a large moving body into the water and some of the water must move out of its way. _How_ it moves depends on the entering mass, its speed, the depth of the lake, and the shape of the basin.

-   Some of the displaced water may run along the shore.
-   Some may climb the opposite bank.
-   Some may travel toward the dam.

Pontesei supplied all three possibilities on very short notice.

The reservoir was already around thirteen meters below its maximum permitted water level.

There was empty space above the still water.

The wave generated by the slide did not behave like a slow and orderly rise of the whole lake. The reported maximum run-up was on the order of **twenty meters**, though that was not the water depth at every point around the shore.

The water that reached the service road where the men stood was described in later local reconstructions as **six or seven meters** high.

Linari and Marinello scrambled uphill, away from the road and toward the rising ground.

They survived.

Tiziani did not reach the slope in time.

The water swept him away.

His body was never recovered.

The warning had reached a road. The road had been closed. Two men had been sent to observe the mountain. They had seen it move and survived the wave.

None of those facts pulled Tiziani from the reservoir.

He had been traveling to work on a road across the lake from a landslide that did not need to reach him.

* * *

The wave continued through the basin.

Water passed over the dam's crest and into the works and roads below. A bridge over the Rio Bosconero was destroyed. The force of the water was sufficient to remove a substantial concrete crossing from the route it had been built to carry.

There was also a bus.

Its driver had approached the dangerous section of the state road and decided not to proceed. The way ahead was already judged unsafe. He turned back toward Forno di Zoldo.

A police account, reproduced during the later Vajont proceedings, describes passengers leaving the bus shortly before the wave reached it.

They found shelter near a sharp bend in the road. The bus itself was struck from behind and thrown against the rock wall. The passengers survived.

The cracked road had warned the driver away from the ground that was moving. The wave came from the reservoir beside it.

Pontesei was no longer a question of whether the hillside might damage a particular section of roadway.

The entire basin had joined in.

* * *

The photographs taken after the slide show an enormous exposed scar on the bank, uprooted trees, wrecked road sections, disturbed earth, and the altered surface of the reservoir.

They also show the dam.

The dam was fine.

Pontesei had demonstrated a danger whose most damaging route could pass through the _water held behind_ a dam without requiring the dam itself to collapse.

It had done so at a facility operated by the same company, within the same developing hydroelectric network, four years before Vajont.

The event did not establish that Mount Toc would fail in the same way. The two slopes were geologically different. Their potential sliding masses were not equal. Their basins were not the same shape.

Any prediction of the 1963 disaster would have required additional work.

But the general relation had become very difficult to deny.

A moving slope could displace reservoir water fast enough to kill a person on the opposite bank and destroy structures downstream.

The lake was part of the landslide.

* * *

There is an awkward part of the record here, and it begins the day after Tiziani died.

On **March 23**, geophysicist **Pietro Caloi** wrote to SADE engineer **Dino Tonini**. He asked Tonini to reread a report he had sent in July 1958.

Caloi believed the Pontesei event had confirmed his earlier warning with remarkable precision.

On **March 27**, he wrote again, this time to **Rossi-Leidi**, offering assurance of his discretion and urging the company to draw the appropriate conclusions.

The letters are documented in the later historical chronology and were discussed during the criminal proceedings.

They do not, by themselves, prove that his 1958 report predicted the exact location, volume, speed, and wave of March 22. Even in the later courtroom arguments, the geographical applicability of Caloi's earlier report was disputed.

It would be a mistake to correct one excessively confident account of the mountain by adopting another without examining the underlying study.

Still.

The slide happened on Sunday.

On Monday, a geophysicist was already telling the engineers to look again at what they had been told.

-   The ground had moved.
    -   The water had moved.
        -   Now the evidence was moving through letters.

It would have to arrive somewhere capable of changing what came next.

* * *

The Pontesei disaster did produce changes. Its reservoir was operated with reduced storage. Its slopes were examined more closely. At Vajont, Carlo Semenza and other engineers had a concrete reason to reconsider the stability of their own reservoir banks. The intensified investigations at Vajont followed in the months after Pontesei.

In his argument during the Vajont proceedings, lawyer Odorico Ascari returned repeatedly to the duration of the Pontesei slide.

He reproduced testimony in which Linari distinguished the final violent movement, lasting about **half a minute**, from the roughly **two-to-three-minute** rapid episode as a whole.

Ascari also pointed to a later hydraulic-model committee account that referred to the Pontesei slide as having taken something closer to **ten minutes**.

He argued that the longer figure had softened the warning as it passed into the experimental record.

A million cubic meters entering a lake slowly and the same amount entering quickly have no agreement to produce the same wave.

The clock belongs to the physics.

So if the recorded duration changes, the modeled danger can change with it.

Pontesei had left the engineers a slope scar, a damaged road, a destroyed bridge, a nearly lost bus, measurement records, correspondence, and the absence of Arcangelo Tiziani.

It had also left a question that would become more important with every additional meter of reservoir filled at Vajont:

> **How fast can a mountain move once it stops moving slowly?**

On Mount Toc, the cracks were still being investigated.

They would not remain small.

* * *

## **The Mountain Starts Moving.**

In May 1960, engineers began putting small markers on Mount Toc.

A **survey marker** is a really good way to ask a mountain where it is.

You install one at a known point. You measure its position from other known points. Later, you come back and measure it again.

If the mountain has not moved, the readings should agree.

If they do **not** agree, there are two immediate possibilities.

1.  The measurement is wrong, or
2.  the mountain has gone somewhere.

At Vajont, that second possibility was becoming increasingly difficult to dismiss.

* * *

The reservoir was beginning its first experimental filling.

SADE had begun admitting water on **February 2, 1960**.

The formal authorization for an initial level of **595 meters above sea level** followed in February. The eventual operating design contemplated a maximum water level of **722.5 meters**.

These elevations are heights above sea level, not depths measured from the bottom of the lake. A lake surface at **595 meters** tells us where the water stands against the valley. It does not tell us that the lake contains **595 meters of water**. The rock beneath it has its own elevation, and the banks rise unevenly around it.

The shore was not a fixed line. As the reservoir filled, the line between wet and dry ground traveled up the slopes.

Water entered fractures, covered rock that had been exposed to air, reached old deposits, and altered the pressures acting on the base of the mountain.

Rain was also entering the ground from above.

The mountain had two sources of water, a complicated interior, and no obligation to tell the engineers which source was doing what.

In March, a small fall of rock occurred near the mouth of the Massalezza stream.

Elsewhere, along the northern edge of Pian della Pozza, part of the slope was already slipping. Photographs taken in April show a block that had descended roughly ten meters, although its exact movement history was uncertain.

Edoardo Semenza later cautioned against simply blaming the new reservoir for that particular movement. The lake had not yet risen to the lower limit of the small slide's failure surface.

Mountains can move without reservoirs.

This mountain had one anyway.

* * *

There was another way to examine the slope before following its movement.

You could send a vibration through the rock and measure how it traveled.

During the winter of 1959–1960, geophysicist **Pietro Caloi** conducted seismic investigations on the left bank of the Vajont reservoir. Small explosive charges supplied the pulses. Instruments recorded their travel through the mountain. From the velocities, Caloi inferred characteristics of the material underground.

His conclusions were reassuring.

The readings suggested strong, elastic rock, which he interpreted as material formed in place beneath a comparatively thin surface covering. His report of **February 4, 1960**, was provided to the public control authorities.

Seismic waves pass differently through different materials. Their speed can help distinguish layers and reveal structures that cannot be seen directly from the surface.

There is a limit.

The speed of a pulse through rock does not, by itself, establish that a vast body of rock has no surface along which it can move.

A mountain can contain strong limestone in both the moving part and the ground beneath it. The important difference may lie in how those rocks are connected, in their fractures, or in a layer that a particular measurement does not resolve.

Caloi had measured an actual property of the mountain.

Edoardo Semenza and Franco Giudici were still trying to determine what _body_ that property belonged to.

In their interpretation, much of the material on the Toc slope might be the remains of an ancient landslide, with a possible surface of weakness beneath it.

That interpretation had not yet been demonstrated. Later geological research would contest important parts of it.

But it supplied a different question from the seismic one.

> What if the rock being measured was strong, **and the rock mass was still capable of moving?**

* * *

So SADE began drilling into the slope.

From **May to July 1960**, three boreholes were sunk around Pian della Pozza.

They were numbered **51, 52,** and **53**.

-   The first went down **172 meters**.
-   The second reached **71 meters**.
-   The third reached **105 meters**.

The men doing this work were trying to find the suspected sliding surface: the boundary at which the material above might move relative to the rock beneath it.

A **borehole** is quite good at discovering what lies along a narrow vertical path.

It can recover cores, encounter water, and pass through beds of different stone. It can show you broken rock where a solid mass was expected.

A **borehole** is less good at discovering something that lies just beyond its bottom.

The Vajont borings encountered heavily fractured limestone and other rock. Water entered and escaped through openings. The walls of the holes repeatedly collapsed, making deeper drilling difficult.

The investigators did not find the surface they were looking for.

The rock was broken. The water was moving through it. The drill could not reach farther. And the suspected boundary remained somewhere else, if it existed at all.

Here the record becomes unexpectedly important.

In his [later account of the investigation](https://www.vajont.info/H%26Patton2App.pdf?ref=modalpathethics.com), **Edoardo Semenza** said the unsuccessful borings led him toward a **more optimistic** interpretation.

If the moving material rested on a surface that had not been intercepted at these depths, perhaps the surface passed farther inside the mountain and included a broad, nearly horizontal portion beneath the mass.

Such a shape might provide greater resistance to sliding.

That was a hypothesis about geometry.

The boreholes had not revealed that broad floor. They had failed to reach the contact that might have established or contradicted it.

A missing interface had become a reason to consider a more favorable one farther down.

Not an unreasonable possibility to investigate.

A very dangerous possibility to treat as settled.

* * *

The geological study completed by **Semenza and Giudici in June 1960** did not say that everything was safe.

It considered the possibility that a large mass had moved in the past and might be reactivated, especially if its contact surface inclined toward the reservoir.

Water could change the conditions of movement.

The eventual consequences, the geologists warned, depended on a sliding surface whose shape and properties had not been established.

There was now a substantial qualification inside the company's geological work.

Before the study was formally delivered to SADE, Carlo Semenza asked his son to show it to the older geologist Dal Piaz.

In a letter dated **May 24**, Carlo suggested that if some of Edoardo's claims needed softening, this would not be _the end of the world_.

The documentary chronology records that the study was not sent to the public control authorities. The exact distribution of drafts, summaries, and subsequent findings needs to be reconstructed from the archival correspondence; we should not assume that every official knew nothing of the broader concerns.

But we can establish the more limited point.

-   The state had received Caloi's reassuring geophysical interpretation.
-   The geological investigation inside SADE was developing a much less settled picture.

Those were two different instruments, addressing different aspects of the same ground.

Meanwhile, the reservoir continued to rise.

After the initial trial, SADE sought permission to increase the water level to **660 meters**. Permission was granted in June.

Measurements of movement began in May, using roughly ten fixed points on the slope and repeated surveying from outside it.

The points were there to establish whether the surface was changing position.

By late summer, the answer was **yes.**

* * *

The geologists kept climbing the mountain.

During July, they followed the branches of the Massalezza stream higher up the slope, where crushed and fractured rock was exposed along the channels. These outcrops appeared to mark part of the upper boundary of the mass Semenza believed had once moved.

There was still an enormous difference between drawing a possible boundary from old geological forms and watching a new fracture open along it.

Then, in the autumn of **1960**, the new fracture appeared. It traveled across the upper slope in two great bends, rising and falling along the terrain.

Seen on a map, its outline resembled the letter **M**.

The crack extended for **more than** **two kilometers**.

This was not a small split beside one failed section of road. It crossed a large part of the mountain above the reservoir. In places the **M** opened to tens of centimeters, and some later photographs show sections half a meter or more across.

Along one segment, the rock on the downhill side had moved enough to create a low step that did not appear in photographs made during the summer. Trees along other sections were tilted in different directions.

A **tree** is a useful witness in this kind of situation. The thing grows out of the ground.

So if the ground moves while the tree continues trying to stand upright, the tree may finish at an angle it did not select.

There were now many such witnesses around.

The grass was split. The soil had separated. The rock had shifted.

The survey markers also recorded accelerating movement. By the end of October, some rates exceeded **three centimeters a day**.

**Three centimeters** is a very small distance when you are running on a treadmill.

It is an alarming daily distance when the thing moving is a mountainside, especially when you have not established how much of that mountain is involved or what holds it up.

* * *

The reservoir was rising during this period, reaching approximately **650 meters above sea level** by early November. The autumn was also unusually wet.

The two observations should stay together.

The water behind the dam had risen. Rain had entered the mountain from above. Fractures were opening and the movement of survey points was changing.

These facts do not, by themselves, isolate one hydraulic cause for every movement recorded in 1960.

Some later analyses emphasize reservoir-induced pressures. Others have challenged the usual geological reconstruction and emphasized rainfall and deeper groundwater pathways. Even the measured movement rate appears to have slowed during the first days of November while the lake was still rising.

The fissure, however, required no theory to be seen.

The entire outline was right there on the ground.

And on **November 4**, the lower slope let go.

* * *

About **700,000 cubic meters** of rock detached from the northern edge of Pian della Pozza, along a front roughly **300 to 350 meters wide**.

The material entered the reservoir.

The water responded.

The landslide produced a **two meter** wave in the lake.

As that wave traveled and met the dam face, the water climbed higher; around **ten meters above the prevailing lake level**, according to the later photographic reconstruction.

The dam was wetted up to approximately **660 meters above sea level**.

The crest was far above it.

There was no catastrophic overtopping. The arch was fine. The accounts examined report no deaths or major property damage from this particular wave.

This was a good outcome for the people living around the basin.

It was also an incredible result for the project.

A lake that had only just been created had already received hundreds of thousands of cubic meters of its own mountainside.

The displaced water had crossed the basin and climbed the dam.

The event had happened in the same valley, against the same engineered wall, under the same operating system that would have to decide what to do next.

* * *

The November slide did **not** mean that every rock inside the M-shaped fracture had fallen.

Most of that area remained on the mountain.

It also did not establish that the remaining mass would necessarily move as one body, or that a single hidden sliding surface had been located beneath it.

-   The November 4 scar was comparatively small.
-   The fracture above it described something much larger.

That difference now occupied the engineers.

On **November 8 and 9**, **Edoardo Semenza** inspected the newly opened perimeter crack with engineer **Ruol** and met **Leopold Müller**, who had been called back to Vajont. They examined the fractured ground around Pian della Pozza.

During further meetings on **November 15 and 16**, Müller and the SADE engineers considered the mountain's movement on both sides of the Massalezza stream. The mass was being understood in sections, with different possible forms of motion. Semenza later recalled estimating the unstable volume at about **250 million cubic meters**; Müller's estimates were of the same enormous order, around **200 million**.

The difference was almost absurd.

The small slide had entered the water. The rest of the mountain had remained above it.

But a crack more than two kilometers long had traced a boundary across the slope, and the engineers were now discussing **hundreds of millions of cubic meters** of material on the other side of it.

Even then, there was no settled account of the depth and geometry of the moving zone.

A public-works expert, Francesco Penta, would shortly acknowledge the possibility of a deep fracture separating a huge mass while questioning whether the measured surface motion proved such a deep slide. A thinner moving layer remained, in his assessment, a serious alternative.

-   A shallow cover of debris moving slowly is one problem.
-   A mountain moving above a deep failure surface is another.
    -   Neither is defined by the length of a line alone.

That line had acquired enormous evidentiary weight because the mountain had drawn the warning itself.

It still had to be interpreted.

And the next practical decision could not await the disappearance of that uncertainty.

The reservoir was being lowered.

The engineers were planning a passage through the rock on the opposite bank, so water could continue through the valley even if a landslide divided the lake.

A mountain was moving into the reservoir.

The next construction project would be a tunnel designed for the possibility that it decided to finish the job.

* * *

## **The Tunnel.**

A **lake** can be cut in two without losing any water.

This is a problem if the lake is supposed to move water from one place to another.

A **reservoir** is not just water sitting behind a wall.

At Vajont, it was part of a much larger machine. Water could arrive through tunnels from the Piave system, be stored in the valley, and leave through the arrangements near the dam when the electrical network needed it.

The water had a path.

Mount Toc was threatening to put a mountain across it.

* * *

The November 4 slide had left around 700,000 cubic meters in the reservoir. Above it, the mass outlined by the **M** remained in place. By mid-November, the geologists were discussing possible movement involving **hundreds of millions of cubic meters**, without agreeing on the depth of the moving zone or the timing of a larger failure.

But suppose a sufficiently large part of the mountain came down.

The rock could fill the narrow valley from the southern shore toward the northern bank. It could form an embankment across the reservoir, with water on both sides and no open channel between them.

This would create two lakes.

-   The **upstream lake** would remain toward Erto, where the Vajont and other streams continued feeding water into the valley.
-   The **downstream lake** would remain beside the dam and its hydraulic works.

The new embankment would be made of fallen mountain.

It would not come with a spillway, inspection gallery, operating rules, or a gate.

Water arriving upstream could accumulate behind it. If it could no longer reach the normal outlets, its level might rise toward the settlements above the blockage.

Meanwhile, the lower part of the reservoir could be cut off from the water the power system expected to receive.

The mountain could therefore damage the same project in two very different ways.

-   It could interrupt the movement of water needed for electricity.
-   And it could create a new, uncontrolled reservoir threatening people who had already been living beside the first one.

So the company needed another route.

* * *

On **November 15 and 16**, SADE's engineers and advisers met at Vajont. **Leopold Müller** was there, along with **Carlo Semenza**, **Mario Pancini**, **Alberico Biadene**, **Camillo Linari**, and **Ruol.**

They agreed on two substantial interventions.

-   **First,** lower the lake.
-   **Second,** dig a tunnel around the part of the valley the mountain might occupy.

Lowering began on **November 17**. By the end of December, the water surface was near **600 meters above sea level**, down from 650 meters when the November slide occurred.

The lowering was undertaken under Müller's advice as a way of controlling movement. The survey markers subsequently showed a sharp reduction in displacement. By January 1961, movement had become very small at the points being observed.

But there was also colder weather and less precipitation.

The reservoir level, groundwater conditions, and rainfall had all changed over the same period. The measurements did not independently establish how much of the slowing belonged to each cause.

-   The water was down.
-   The mountain was quieter.

The problem of what would happen when the water went back **up** remained.

Meanwhile, the tunnel could be built.

* * *

This tunnel was called the **galleria di sorpasso frana**: _a passage to bypass the landslide._

It would run through the rock on the northern, right-hand bank of the reservoir, opposite Mount Toc. The proposed entrance stood toward the upstream part of the lake, near Mulini delle Spesse. From there, the passage would travel west through the mountain beneath the Erto–Casso side of the valley and emerge near the dam.

If a landslide blocked the open valley, water could still pass through the rock on the other side.

This is a very good use of a tunnel.

A **road** goes around an obstacle on the surface.

A **tunnel** goes through something that can remain in place when the obstacle appears beside it.

The project required about **1,800 meters of excavation**, with a passage 4.5 meters in diameter. Access openings were also made near Ponte di Casso and Colombèr, so workers and equipment could reach intermediate parts of the underground route.

This was expensive. The estimate discussed after the November meetings was on the order of **one billion lire**.

This also meant work.

The reservoir had to stay low while the tunnel was cut. Roads and access points had to be prepared. Rock had to be drilled and removed. The passage had to meet its planned line beneath a mountainside on which people were already living.

Two contractors, **Monti** and **Zadra**, worked on the excavation to shorten the schedule.

Photographs from **February 1961** show construction at the service access near the former Colombèr bridge. Another, taken in **April**, shows the lowered valley and the new tunnel works against the rock and the scar left by the November landslide.

They were extracting nearly two kilometers of stone so that the system could keep functioning after part of another mountain entered the reservoir.

* * *

There was another investigation proceeding in tandem.

On **January 31, 1961**, SADE commissioned the **Hydraulic Models Centre at Nove**, near Vittorio Veneto, to reproduce the reservoir and investigate the waves a landslide might produce.

The **tunnel** and the **model** addressed different physical questions.

-   The **tunnel** would help water get around a mass of rock _after_ that mass had blocked the valley,
    -   provided the passage and the hydraulic works it depended upon remained usable.
-   The **model** was supposed to investigate what happened _while_ rock entered the water.

A **bypass** could carry a continuing flow.

A **landslide-generated wave** was a rapidly changing displacement of water through the basin.

The tunnel could solve the first without solving the second.

This difference was especially important in a valley that had already sent a wave up the face of its dam.

Pontesei had done worse than that.

It killed a man across the water from a landslide.

There was no lack of physical demonstrations.

* * *

The experts also disagreed about how much mountain the new works would have to accommodate.

In a memorandum dated **December 1, 1960**, **Francesco Penta**, advising the public testing commission, acknowledged that the long fracture could be the surface trace of a deep rupture separating an enormous mass from Mount Toc.

He was reluctant to adopt that explanation as established. The measurements were taken at the surface. He considered a thinner, slower-moving layer a serious alternative.

On **February 3, 1961**, **Müller** presented a much more alarming geological and geomechanical interpretation.

He estimated the unstable mass at around **200 million cubic meters**. His analysis distinguished movement on either side of the Massalezza stream and examined the effects of the reservoir and groundwater.

He did not think that a mass on this scale could reliably be stopped by an artificial retaining measure. Even abandoning the reservoir would not necessarily make the already moving slope return immediately to rest.

That did not make the bypass useless. A tunnel could still address the prospect of water accumulating behind a landslide deposit.

But it left a very large unresolved problem outside the tunnel's diameter.

The engineers could excavate a new water route.

They could not excavate an agreed prediction of the mountain's next movement.

* * *

The danger was also moving through public offices.

On **February 2**, representatives on the Belluno Provincial Council called for action to protect Erto, Longarone, and the surrounding villages. A proposal to obtain another geological assessment was accepted.

Belluno's provincial president, **Alessandro Dal Borso**, sought cooperation from the neighboring province of Udine, because Erto belonged to Udine's jurisdiction.

The documentary chronology preserves the reply that Dal Borso later reported:

> **The matter was of no concern to Udine.**

The water would occupy ground on only one side of the provincial boundary.

But the reservoir did not recognize that division.

A large slide might affect settlements in both territories, as well as the town downstream of the dam.

-   The two provincial administrations had different jurisdictions.
-   The mountain had one big slope.

It is important, however, not to mistake that exchange for the entire response of the state. Public-works officials, testing commissioners, government engineers, and geologists were actively inspecting Vajont and requiring reports. The state had not simply disappeared due to a lack of concern. Its different offices were obtaining different pieces of the problem.

Some of those pieces were arriving with substantial uncertainties attached.

* * *

In **April 1961**, the water was still being kept low and the tunnel was under construction.

**Penta** inspected the site on **April 15**. At the prevailing reservoir level, he considered the immediate situation comparatively quiet. The slope's surface movement had slowed to almost nothing.

At the prevailing level.

Five days later, **Carlo Semenza** wrote privately to engineer **Vincenzo Ferniani**.

He could see the improvement after the drawdown. He also had a question about the next filling.

> What would happen when the water rose again?

The letter is unusually revealing because the writer is the man whose hydroelectric project and bypass tunnel we have been following. He was not disputing the usefulness of the new passage.

He was confronting a different limit.

This mountain's movement, he acknowledged, had become more than he knew how to contain. Dal Piaz and Penta were relatively hopeful that the sliding material would settle into a stable arrangement. Semenza hoped so too.

A **hope** is a real part of a decision made under uncertainty.

Hope is not an observation of a mountain coming permanently to rest.

The tunnel could be finished. The lake could be held low. The survey markers could continue recording almost no movement.

And none of those facts had yet told Semenza what the mountain would do during the next filling.

* * *

By the autumn of **1961**, the landslide-bypass gallery was completed.

In a letter dated **October 5**, Semenza told the Dams Service that it was finished and asked permission to resume experimental filling toward **680 meters above sea level**, in stages and under continued observation.

The tunnel had supplied a new physical route through the mountain. It also changed the conditions under which the project could contemplate using the lake again.

A future landslide might divide the reservoir. The gallery was designed so that the water would not necessarily have to remain divided with it.

That was a real engineering achievement. One that depended on a future obstruction leaving the tunnel's entrance, exit, and connecting hydraulic system available.

The tunnel could not test that assumption by existing.

And the project still needed to know what would happen to the water during the slide itself.

Carlo Semenza died on **October 30, 1961**.

**Alberico Biadene** took over the project's technical direction.

The dam was fine.

The tunnel was finished.

The mountain was quiet enough, for the moment, to begin asking the next question.

At **Nove**, engineers were preparing to answer it with a smaller mountain, a smaller lake, and water that could be measured as the model came down.

* * *

## **The Model.**

There are some difficulties involved in asking a mountain to fall into a lake for experimental purposes.

The big one is that the mountain might actually do it.

At Vajont, this would have been a terrible way to learn whether the reservoir could survive a landslide.

The great **M** on Mount Toc was already open.

Engineers estimated that an enormous volume of rock could move toward the water. They did not know when it might happen, how the movement would develop, or how quickly the mass could descend once it started.

They needed to see the wave without producing it.

So they built a smaller lake.

And a smaller mountain.

* * *

The experiments took place at **Nove**, near Vittorio Veneto, at a hydraulic-model center associated with SADE's electrical works. The work was directed by **Augusto Ghetti,** a professor of hydraulic engineering at the University of Padua, with the participation of other engineers and university specialists, including **Francesco Marzolo**.

The reservoir was reproduced at **1:200 scale**. The contours of the shore, the narrow approach to the dam, and the ground facing Mount Toc could be assembled into an object small enough for people to walk around.

The water could be supplied and drained through pipes. Its level could be raised to represent different operating elevations. Measuring points could record the rise of waves as they passed through the model.

There was even a miniature dam, too.

At one end of the apparatus, a measuring basin could collect water that passed over the crest. Water could then be counted instead of _imagined._

Photographs and plans show gravel piled along one side of the model, a shaped trough representing the reservoir, nets and cables, and a small tracked tractor attached to the machinery that would move the artificial slide.

The mountain remained several valleys away.

* * *

The model had a defined job.

SADE wanted to know what a major landslide would do to the water already held behind the dam.

> Would the wave damage the dam?

> How high might water run along the shores?

> Could it reach settlements around the reservoir?

> How much might spill over the crest into the gorge leading to Longarone?

Those were good questions. Pontesei had already demonstrated why they needed answers. There, the hillside had entered the reservoir without destroying the dam, and the wave had killed Arcangelo Tiziani on the opposite bank.

The engineers did not need another man to be killed in order to establish that displaced water could be dangerous. The model was meant to estimate how dangerous.

A separate set of studies at the experimental institute in Bergamo examined dynamic forces on the dam itself. The Nove apparatus concentrated on water moving through the reservoir. What an escaping flood wave would do down the narrow Vajont gorge and into the Piave valley would require an extension of the hydraulic investigation.

These were different physical problems.

-   The water rising against the wall could be measured in one apparatus.
-   The ability of the arch to withstand pressure belonged to another analysis.
    -   The fate of the towns below required following the water beyond the wall.

You could perform the first operation without completing the others.

You could also obtain a _very_ convincing-looking number from it.

* * *

The first tests began in **August 1961**.

The experimental mountainside was a sloping board, covered with metal sheet. The gravel sat above it, restrained by metal netting. When the restraints were released, gravity took over.

At first the sliding surface was inclined at **30 degrees**. Other trials used **42 degrees**. Different inclinations gave the gravel different opportunities to accelerate.

There was a small problem with gravel.

**Gravel** is made of many small stones. These stones can move past one another. A pile of them can spread, break apart, and descend in a way that is pretty easy to observe and comparatively easy to reproduce.

**Mount Toc**, conversely, was made of fractured limestone, layered rock, and weaker material whose relations at depth were still being argued over. A moving section of that mountain might deform and break up. It might also retain enough internal coherence to push a broad front of rock toward the water very rapidly.

A wave responds to _how_ the space occupied by water is taken away.

One million cubic meters entering a lake slowly is not the same hydraulic event as the same volume entering it in a sudden rush. A broad advancing front does not necessarily displace water like a heap of loose pieces spreading across a floor.

**Edoardo Semenza,** who observed an early test, questioned whether the surface and the gravel adequately represented the motion of Mount Toc.

The experimental team revised the apparatus in response.

This is worth being exact about.

They changed the model.

* * *

During the second series, from **January through April 1962**, the simple incline was replaced with a shaped sliding surface, built to reflect a geological reconstruction of the slope. **Semenza** had supplied profiles for it.

The gravel remained. But rigid sections were embedded within the material, and cables connected the moving arrangement to a tractor.

This made it possible to impose different speeds on a slide that would not otherwise move along the curved surface in the desired way.

-   The tractor would pull.
    -   The gravel would descend.
        -   The water would rise.
            -   The instruments would measure it.

And after the test, the people at **Nove** could set the mountain back up again.

The researchers could try different starting water levels and different slide timings, including conditions they regarded as severe.

They could not, however, make the apparatus answer a question nobody had arranged for it to ask.

-   The shape of the sliding surface was a hypothesis.
-   The mechanical behavior of the artificial rock was another hypothesis.
-   The timing of the motion was an input, even where the experimenter deliberately made it faster than engineers expected.

The model would produce water behavior conditional on all of them.

It was extremely good at letting people see what happened _given the event they had just built._

* * *

**Mount Toc** had already been divided on paper.

The **Massalezza** stream crossed the unstable slope. In the engineers' reconstruction, one region of the mountain lay **upstream** of it, and another lay **downstream**, closer to the dam.

Some contemporary geological interpretations suggested that one region might slide before the other. That possibility became the organization of the tests.

The artificial landslides descended in separate operations.

-   Sometimes the team let the effect of the first subside before releasing the second.
-   Sometimes they timed the second descent to meet a returning wave from the first, producing a more severe combined surge.

That was not the same as making the entire mountain enter the water together.

No test reproduced a sudden, simultaneous collapse of the full mass enclosed by Mount Toc's great fracture.

The researchers ran five initial trials and seventeen later controlled trials. Across them, they varied water level, sliding surface, the portion of the slope released, and the speed of the artificial movement.

They knew the actual unstable mountain could contain far more rock than the material made to enter the miniature lake.

That fact alone does not settle whether their chosen slide volume was adequate. Not all the rock in a mountain enters the water. SADE's engineers reasoned that the hydraulically relevant volume could be substantially smaller than the total mass, on the order of **tens of millions of cubic meters.**

A big number describing a mountain is not automatically the number describing how much lake it can displace.

But an estimate of displaced volume does not settle how violently the displacement occurs.

The laboratory had to supply a motion to go with the volume.

That motion had a clock.

* * *

In one of the most demanding tests at **700 meters** above sea level, **Trial XIX**, the artificial slide represented about **20.5 million cubic meters** of material at full scale.

-   The upstream portion moved first.
    -   Its equivalent full-scale descent lasted **180 seconds.**
-   After an interval of several minutes, the downstream portion was released.
    -   That one descended in **60 seconds.**

One minute had been chosen as an exceptionally rapid movement compared with the several-minute slides contemplated in the project specifications. At **1:200** scale, reproducing that gravity-controlled time meant a little over four seconds in the model itself.

The tractor pulled the simulated mountain through its prescribed journey.

The water rose.

Near the dam, the modeled wave reached roughly **27 meters** above the starting lake level. At a measuring point about **430 meters upstream of the dam**, the rise was **31 meters**.

Some water went over the crest.

Around **21,000 cubic meters** at full scale passed over in that test.

This was, under the assumptions of the test, a limited spill compared with the volumes the reservoir contained.

The experimental team could measure the wave, estimate how much water passed over the wall, and compare the result with experiments beginning at different lake elevations.

At **670 meters**, the wave stayed well below the crest.

At the maximum intended reservoir elevation of **722.5 meters**, very rapid slides could produce much more serious overtopping.

-   The water level mattered.
-   The speed mattered even more.

And in the laboratory, the fastest dangerous event still required a full minute of descent.

* * *

On **July 3, 1962**, Ghetti delivered the final report to SADE.

This report did not certify the mountain’s geological stability. It acknowledged substantial uncertainty, including uncertainty about how a landslide would unfold. It described the separate slide regions, the modeled times, the waves, and the water passing over the dam.

The report concluded that if the reservoir began at a level of **700 meters**, even the most catastrophic foreseeable landslide event would remain within what it called

> **“absolute safety.”**

This was a consequential phrase.

-   Those experiments had established water heights for the particular slides that had been made to occur at Nove.
-   That phrase now described **safety at a real reservoir level.**

Between those propositions lay the mountain, still moving slowly in its own valley, with a sliding surface that had never been directly located and a future speed that no instrument had measured.

There was another limit in the report itself. Ghetti recommended further tests on a model extended beyond the dam, so that waves passing down the Vajont gorge and into the Piave could be examined more closely.

The existing reservoir model had not completed that downstream investigation.

-   It could tell its operators how much water had passed over its little dam during a given test.
-   It could not tell the people of Longarone what would happen to them without following that water down to Longarone.

This additional study was not completed as proposed.

Nor did the documented campaign reproduce the earlier Pontesei slide and its wave as a check against a known event.

**Pontesei** was a different reservoir with a different slope. A successful comparison would not have certified Vajont, and a failed one would not have proved what Mount Toc was going to do.

It could have at least tested whether the apparatus at Nove reproduced a landslide-generated wave whose real consequences were already known.

They had a measured wave, water going over the wall, a conclusion about **absolute safety**, and an unexamined route from the water to the town down below.

* * *

That experiment was real.

Researchers constructed a detailed apparatus, performed repeated trials, revised the sliding surface, and deliberately tested a one-minute descent faster than their initial assumptions. They measured waves produced by the movements imposed on the model.

Nothing in this record justifies calling the experiment fraudulent.

The much harder question is what those events represented.

The actual mountain in October 1963 would move far faster than one minute and substantially together. Later numerical studies, using different representations of the moving rock, found that the choice of material behavior could be even more consequential to the predicted wave than dividing the slide into separate masses.

Those studies were done after the disaster and cannot be smuggled into the heads of the experimenters in 1962.

They can, however, tell us where the experimental result was sensitive.

> **The model had not reproduced the event that happened.**

It could move a tractor, release gravel, measure water, and print a convincing figure for the dam's crest.

It could not prove that the actual mountain would accept the motion assigned to it.

The researchers knew the geology was uncertain.

The report said so.

And then it gave the reservoir a number.

> **Seven hundred meters.**

That number could now leave Nove and get to work.

We have to follow it.

* * *

## **Seven Hundred Meters.**

**Seven hundred meters** is a height above sea level.

It does not mean that a lake is seven hundred meters deep.

It does not describe the volume of water behind a dam.

It does not tell us whether the mountain beside the lake will move.

At Vajont, however, this number was beginning to do several jobs.

-   It was a level the reservoir could be filled to.
-   It was a level an administrative authority could permit.
-   And, after the experiments at Nove, it was a level associated with the phrase **absolute safety**.

Those were not the same finding.

The lake was about to test the difference.

* * *

When the bypass tunnel was completed in 1961, the reservoir could be filled again.

The gallery through the northern bank offered water an alternative route if a future landslide ever blocked the main valley. It could not prevent the landslide, and it could not catch a wave.

Still, the project could proceed with the next experiment now.

-   Water would be admitted slowly.
-   Surveyors would measure points on Mount Toc.
-   Instruments would record the water inside the rock.
-   The dam itself would be watched for deformation and leakage.
    -   The results would be reported to government officials, who could decide whether to permit a higher level.

This was both an observation program and a procedure for continuing to use the reservoir while the underlying geological question remained unresolved.

The company wanted permission to reach **680 meters**.

The public authorities were less enthusiastic.

On **November 16, 1961**, the relevant public-works authority permitted filling only to **640 meters**, with rises of no more than one meter a day. Reports on the dam, the banks, survey markers, and groundwater were to be sent every fifteen days.

The company had already begun raising the water on October 19, before that authorization was issued. The later parliamentary investigation records both dates.

The resulting permission therefore put conditions around a filling already underway.

-   The first permitted level was **640**.
-   In December the authorities allowed **655**.
-   In February 1962 they allowed **675**,
    -   with a slower maximum daily rise of half a meter.

A sequence of numbers was now moving through letters and instruments.

At each stage, the water had to get there physically.

The permission arrived on paper first.

The mountain's response came later.

* * *

The reports were not simply statements that _everything looked fine._

They contained measurements of the concrete arch, movements at surveyed points, reservoir elevation, groundwater levels, rainfall, and observations along the shores. The public testing commission and state engineers had inspected the site.

Their accounts distinguished

-   the behavior of the dam

from

-   the behavior of the mountainside.

The distinction is easy to lose because both are contained in the word _stability_.

-   The concrete arch can be _stable_
    -   while a slope beside its reservoir is moving.
-   The reservoir can retain its water
    -   while the land forming one of its banks loses its place.
-   A dam can perform exactly as designed
    -   and still stand in the wrong relationship to a falling mountain.

There was more than one object to keep track of here.

Meanwhile, the residents of **Erto** and **Casso** were providing observations of their own.

During **April and May 1962**, people reported noises and tremors in the area.

The mayor raised the issue with the public engineers and asked whether the shocks were related to the reservoir. One inspection found no new landslides comparable to that of November 1960, but the physical cause of the reported disturbance remained a separate question.

**Pietro Caloi** examined the seismic evidence. In his May correspondence he attributed much of the renewed activity to wider geological forces in the region rather than directly to filling the lake.

In a later report he allowed that reservoir water might contribute to shallow movements in an already active area.

* * *

On **May 3, 1962**, SADE asked to raise the experimental level to **700 meters**.

The [authorization was granted on **June 8**](https://patrimonio.archivio.senato.it/inventario/scheda/vajont-iv-leg/IT-SEN-123-000215/autorizzazione-del-genio-civile-belluno-rilasciata-alla-sade-aumento-invaso-fino-quota-700?ref=modalpathethics.com) and formally communicated by the Belluno civil-engineering office on **June 18**. This is why different historical records give different June dates for the same step.

The state document cited the dam's satisfactory behavior, the apparently limited movement of Mount Toc, groundwater measurements, favorable technical opinions, and Caloi's assessment of the seismic activity.

It authorized another experiment, given under the conditions then known.

Ghetti's final model report did not yet exist. He delivered it on **July 3**.

That report's conclusion at **700 meters** could therefore not have been the original basis of the June permission to fill to that height.

It would be easy to write that the laboratory declared **700** safe and the authorities consequently allowed the lake to rise to **700**.

But the order was the other way around.

-   The permission came first.
-   The reassuring experimental conclusion arrived afterward.

And the lake still had to reach the permitted level.

* * *

There was another number in heavy circulation that summer.

> **Seven hundred and thirty.**

On **June 22, 1962**, the mayor of Erto and Casso prohibited access to land around the basin below an elevation of **730 meters**, along with boating on the reservoir.

The order was a local protective restriction, requested as filling tests continued. It addressed people entering areas around the lake where the evolving slope and water conditions could expose them to danger.

The laboratory's **700 meters** was something different: the wave produced by the simulated landslides under specified conditions.

The municipal **730** marked an area where people were not supposed to go.

* * *

On **July 8**, government engineer **Mario Bertolissi** reported additional fractures around Mount Toc, some superficial and others apparently deeper. He wanted a geologist to examine what they meant.

In August, the head of the Belluno civil-engineering office forwarded that concern to the national Dams Service and supported a prompt geological inspection.

The Fondazione Vajont documentary chronology records no reply from the Dams Service to that particular request.

And the experiment continued.

By the middle of November **1962**, the reservoir reached **700 meters**. The chronology records **November 17** as the day it attained that level, remaining there until early December.

The survey markers responded.

At first, through much of the filling, the measured movement had remained very small. But around the time the water reached its new high level, displacement accelerated. Some monitored points approached **one centimeter a day** in December.

-   That was slower than the worst measured movement in 1960.
-   That was also a real acceleration, observed while the lake occupied a higher level than it had before.

So the operators began lowering the water.

The measured movement slowed.

By March and early April **1963**, the lake had been brought back to roughly **650 meters**, and displacement at the watched points had diminished toward zero.

This was an encouraging observation.

This seemed to suggest that water level could be used to influence the motion.

> Raise the water, watch the markers.

> If the mountain begins moving faster, lower the water until it slows.

That is a useful operating hypothesis.

Not a mechanical guarantee that a mountain can always be stopped by reversing the last change in water level.

* * *

The government had noticed the acceleration.

Bertolissi prepared another report, dated **January 10, 1963**, accompanying the survey and groundwater records. He described the increased movement and warned that the readings were approaching a critical condition.

By the time the report was dated, the drawdown had already begun and the measured movement was decreasing. The later parliamentary inquiry makes that timing explicit.

This is one of the key difficulties of administering a moving mountain through periodic reports.

An **observation** has a date. A **chart** covers an interval. A **warning** refers to whatever those things may mean.

And the object being watched continues moving while the report travels.

There was now a record of an acceleration after filling and a deceleration after drawdown.

The project had found another reason to believe it could operate the reservoir through careful adjustment of its level.

It had also been given another warning that the mountain could be moved by conditions the project itself was changing.

Both conclusions arose from the same measurements.

* * *

Then the owner changed.

In December **1962**, Italy established **ENEL**, the national electricity authority. The country was reorganizing its electrical industry and transferring the operating businesses of private power companies into a public system.

The Vajont plant was among the assets affected.

On **March 14, 1963**, a presidential decree transferred SADE's electricity business to ENEL. The transfer took legal effect when the decree appeared in the official gazette on **March 16**. This included the Vajont concession, its installations, and the personnel attached to the electrical undertaking.

Formal delivery of the enterprise was documented later, on **July 27**.

Those are different dates with different jobs.

-   The decree changed the legal owner.
-   The later handover recorded the transfer of administration and documentation.

The mountain did not change owners in any sense that affected it.

Much of the technical organization continued with the same people. **Alberico Biadene** remained a central figure in the hydraulic works. An ENEL-appointed provisional administrator, **Feliciano Benvenuti**, assumed management functions during the transition.

It would be incorrect to describe the operation throughout 1963 as the activity of an unchanged SADE. It would also be incorrect to imagine that the technical history was reset when the company became part of ENEL.

A new title to the enterprise did not install a new sliding surface beneath Mount Toc.

* * *

On **March 20, 1963**, during that transfer, the operating organization requested permission for a third experimental filling.

This time the requested level was **715 meters**.

The request was fifteen meters higher than the level Ghetti's report had described as safe under its modeled worst-case scenarios.

This did not mean that every elevation **above 700** was known to be fatal.

The **Nove** tests had examined more than one water level. Their findings described changing degrees of overtopping as water level and simulated slide conditions changed.

But **715 was outside the report's stated 700-meter assurance.**

And the proposed downstream extension of the hydraulic model had not been completed.

The authorization arrived on **May 4, 1963**, through the Belluno civil-engineering office, permitting a rise of no more than **half a meter per day**.

Its stated reasons are striking.

The dam had behaved normally during the previous experimental filling. Water losses had been small. The movement at Mount Toc was considered continuously monitored and without abnormal new facts. The testing commission's favorable opinion was cited.

There were at least two objects hiding inside those assurances.

-   One was the **concrete dam**, which had indeed continued to behave well.
-   The other was the **mountainside**, which had accelerated during the 700-meter filling and slowed again as the water was lowered.

It was possible to regard that history as evidence that the landslide could be regulated.

It was also possible to regard it as evidence that the reservoir was affecting a dangerous slope whose ultimate response remained unknown.

The permit did not resolve that dispute.

It allowed the next experiment to begin.

* * *

The third filling began in **April 1963**.

At first the reservoir climbed through levels already visited. Its operators watched the instruments. The survey markers did not immediately repeat the strongest earlier acceleration. This was consistent with the working view that, once a higher portion of the slope had been wetted and the lake lowered again, returning to the same elevation might not reproduce the earlier movement in the same way.

That observation did not establish what would happen when the water entered levels it had _not_ reached before.

The lake passed **700 meters**.

It was now above the number attached to the model's assurance.

By summer the surface was around **705**.

By early September it was near **710 meters above sea level**.

It did not reach 715, the maximum permitted test level.

This was therefore legally permitted.

This was also higher than the experimental reference that had previously been described as **absolutely safe**.

The request and authorization survive in the parliamentary archive. In their own terms they show a system progressively increasing the water held against a mountain whose behavior it was still learning to predict.

And then, as September began, the measurements changed again.

**The water was near 710.**

**The survey markers were moving faster.**

On the slopes above the lake, roads and trees were starting to provide observations difficult to put inside an orderly fortnightly report.

The next decision would involve lowering the reservoir once more.

The mountain would not follow the schedule.

* * *

## **The Road Moves.**

**The road across Mount Toc** had been offered as atonement for a bridge.

The reservoir drowned the old crossings over the Vajont stream.

The public authorities accepted a perimeter road as their replacement, despite objections from families whose journeys became much longer.

**The road across Mount Toc** was built to preserve those paths.

By September 1963, it had taken another function.

**The road across Mount Toc** was telling the engineers that the mountain was moving.

* * *

**Roads** are laid upon the ground.

Their surfaces can crack from frost, poor drainage, overloaded vehicles, or bad construction.

**Mountain roads** are particularly good at requiring maintenance, because **mountains** do not generally supply flat places for **roads.**

But the Vajont perimeter road had a more serious problem. It crossed ground inside the great **M** that had appeared on Mount Toc in 1960.

Much of the slope beneath it had since been moving slowly toward the reservoir. When one part of that ground moved differently from another, the road had to accommodate the difference.

Asphalt bent a little.

A retaining wall cracked.

A road surface sank where the ground beneath it was leaving.

These changes were the visible consequences of movement already being recorded by the survey markers above and around the lake.

The markers supplied numbers.

The **road** gave you something to trip over.

* * *

In July, the reservoir was still rising through its third experimental filling.

By then, much of the system had passed into ENEL ownership, while the former SADE technical organization continued operating it.

The people of Erto and Casso were living beside the experiment.

An access order from June 1962 had restricted entry near the reservoir below the elevation of **730 meters**.

On **July 1, 1963**, the municipality lifted that restriction.

The operating organization objected, warning of danger near the Vajont lake and challenging the municipality's decision.

The restriction was restored.

On **July 22**, the mayor sent a telegram to the Udine prefecture and ENEL describing muddy water in the lake, rumbling noises, and tremors felt in the municipal territory.

He requested urgent measures.

Fondazione Vajont's documentary chronology records no answer to that particular telegram.

* * *

In **August**, the survey markers on Mount Toc began accelerating again.

At the beginning of the month, the movement at monitored points was still relatively slow. A later geological reconstruction places some readings around **half a centimeter per day.**

As the water rose from approximately **705 meters** toward **710**, movement increased.

There were also periods of heavy rain. The 1985 U.S. Army Corps of Engineers study records roughly 200 millimeters of rain from August 10 to 20, followed by another unusually wet period.

A mountain full of fractures is very interested in rain.

So is the water standing against its lower slope.

Both can change conditions underground. The fact that they occurred together makes it difficult to use this interval to show the exact contribution of either one.

By **September 1**, the lake surface stood at **709.4 meters above sea level**.

On **September 4**, it reached **710**.

That was its highest level during the final filling.

The authorities had permitted a test up to 715. The lake didn't test them.

It stayed close to **710** through most of September.

And the mountain kept moving.

* * *

On **September 2**, a strong tremor shook the area around Erto. The parliamentary inquiry later recorded an estimated intensity between V and VI on the Mercalli scale, with damage to some isolated houses.

The seismographs did not, by themselves, explain its source.

The mayor of **Erto** and **Casso** wrote to ENEL that same day. His letter recalled the unanswered July telegram, described residents living in persistent anxiety, questioned the stability of the reservoir banks, and demanded measures that would restore safety before the basin returned to ordinary operation. Copies were directed to the Ministry of Public Works, the civil-engineering authorities, and the prefecture.

The letter also asked for the damaged buildings in Erto and Casso to be examined. The authorities had a concrete question to answer about walls that had cracked during the tremors and nearby blasting.

In **Longarone**, on the other side of the gorge and below the dam, the deputy mayor also sought an explanation of the tremor through the Belluno civil-engineering office.

He was told it had a seismic origin.

* * *

The survey markers supplied another kind of letter.

In the series reproduced by the Vajont memorial foundation, the daily rate stood at around **6.5 millimeters on September 2**.

By **September 15**, it was about **12 millimeters per day**.

By **September 26**, it had reached **22 millimeters per day**.

A later technical reconstruction records higher maxima at some western measuring points, reaching several centimeters per day during September. Different points and averaging intervals do not necessarily give the same number.

The important shared finding is that **movement was increasing.**

These markers had been installed so the operators would know whether the ground was changing position.

They were doing their job very well.

The problem was what to do with the answer they gave.

* * *

On **September 12**, **Alberico Biadene** answered the mayor of Erto and Casso.

He called some of the municipality's claims **“rather rash assertions.”**

His reply drew upon the work of **Pietro Caloi**, who interpreted the September 2 tremor as tectonic rather than as an event caused by the moving slope. It also cited the older geological judgments of **Giorgio Dal Piaz** and subsequent studies concerning the stability of Erto, whose inhabited center stood well above the maximum reservoir level.

He described regular inspections of the reservoir banks and the fortnightly reports sent to the public engineers, the Dams Service, and the testing commission. He also criticized the municipality's earlier decision to lift the prohibition on approaching the lake shore.

These statements all addressed real things.

Caloi had examined seismic records. The village of Erto did stand higher than the lake. The banks were being watched. Reports really were being produced. Restricting entry near a dangerous reservoir was a sensible precaution.

But none of those facts established that the entire moving slope could not generate a destructive wave.

Nor did the answer erase the other observations entering the record at the same time.

The mountain's rate of movement was increasing.

The great **M** had not closed.

The people living beside the reservoir were continuing to report disturbances.

The same organization was reassuring the municipality about the village while preparing to consider whether the reservoir needed to be lowered.

Its answer and its subsequent decision were concerned with different parts of the danger.

That distinction comes back on September 18.

* * *

By the middle of the month, the mountain was supplying evidence that required no special instrument to notice.

A new crack appeared near the **Punta del Toc**, in the vicinity of the smaller landslide of 1960, about **400 meters upstream of the dam**. The long perimeter fracture widened. Trees tilted downhill. Sections of the perimeter road developed depressions and cracks. The change of level was evidence of a much larger movement below the pavement. Repairing the surface would not repair the ground below.

A tree leaning on a moving slope presents a similar problem. The trunk may remain perfectly sound. The roots may stay attached to their surrounding soil. The entire piece of land supporting them can still be changing position.

There is no defect in the tree.

For the people who used the road to reach farms and hamlets, these were immediate practical dangers. For the engineers, the deformations joined the measurements that were already arriving from the survey points.

The **road** had become an unusually large indicator attached to the moving mass.

* * *

On **September 18**, the operating engineers gathered at Vajont.

**Biadene** attended with **Mario Pancini** and other technical staff. The consultants included **Pietro Caloi**, the geophysicist, and **Arturo Oberti**, a specialist concerned with the dam's structural behavior.

Two questions were under discussion.

1.  Was the dam stable?
2.  And what did the accelerating movement on Mount Toc mean for continued reservoir operation?

The later parliamentary inquiry reports no particular concern over the concrete arch. Its behavior under the tests remained reassuring. The dam seemed fine.

The mountain was another story.

Biadene expressed concern about the acceleration. He said he no longer intended to raise the reservoir to the permitted **715 meters** and would order a drawdown if the monitored movement continued to worsen.

We should be careful about the apparent precision of that meeting.

The Senate's document inventory records that ENEL later stated there was **no surviving formal report or set of minutes** for the September 18 visit. It was reconstructed afterward from other correspondence and testimony. We can recover the decisions and the general subjects discussed without inventing a transcript of who said every sentence.

One conclusion nevertheless stands out.

-   The dam continued to satisfy its structural tests.
-   The operating plan for the lake was being reconsidered because of the mountain.

The engineers had divided those questions correctly enough to worry about one while remaining confident in the other.

What they still lacked was a reliable description of the event that would occur if a very large part of the slope moved very suddenly.

* * *

The reservoir was held near **710 meters** for another several days.

Previous experience suggested that drawing water down could slow the moving mass. But the speed of the mountain's response to changes in water level remained uncertain, and the system also had to deal with groundwater already inside the slope.

A surface level can be lowered by opening an outlet. Water in the fractures of a mountain may not leave at the same rate.

How the changing pressures act on a sliding mass depends on connections underground that the operators had never fully mapped.

Lowering was a real intervention, with evidence in its favor from previous fillings.

It was not an emergency stop button attached directly to Mount Toc.

On **September 26**, **Biadene** ordered the drawdown.

It began on **September 27**.

The lake was now moving in one direction.

The mountain was still moving in another.

The surface of the reservoir began falling. During the following days the reduction proceeded at roughly the order of a meter per day, although accounts differ on how to characterize the exact initial rate and schedule.

The earlier experiments had seemed to show that lowering water could quiet the slope.

The operators were attempting to recover that result.

The markers had not yet provided it.

* * *

On **September 30, Pancini went to Rome.**

He was the site director at Vajont and was about to leave on planned travel. Before going, he personally informed **Baroncini**, ENEL's central director responsible for hydraulic construction, about the increasing movements and the drawdown.

He also asked Baroncini to obtain another urgent inspection by **Francesco Penta**, the geologist associated with the public testing and oversight of the dam.

Pancini did not leave without communicating that the mountain was moving. The warning had reached another level of the organization.

It would now have to reach a geologist, a decision, and—if that judgment required it—the people whose safety depended upon that decision arriving in time.

* * *

## **The Warning Arrives.**

The mountain continued moving.

**Beniamino Caruso,** an engineer responsible for works on the middle Piave, took over Pancini's duties at Vajont.

-   The memorial foundation's chronology says there was no direct handover from Pancini.
-   The parliamentary inquiry records that Caruso assumed the functions and was sent to examine the site under Alberico Biadene's instructions.

Both accounts leave the same working problem.

The man now responsible for the site had to inspect a mountain whose behavior was becoming less and less like anything the previous inspections had successfully predicted.

The surveyors were still there. Rittmeyer, one of the surveyors assigned to the site, had been due to transfer to Venice. His transfer was canceled, and he was ordered to remain.

So were the markers on the slope, the piezometers, the reservoir, the bypass tunnel, and for now, the road around the lake.

* * *

On **October 2**, **Caruso** went to Vajont and examined the new movements and fractures. He and assistant **Dal Pra** agreed that the civil-engineering authorities in Belluno should be informed.

That same day, **Biadene** went to Rome to see **Baroncini,** the ENEL official **Pancini** had spoken to two days earlier. He brought graphs showing the mountain's acceleration and again urged Baroncini to arrange a visit by **Francesco Penta.**

Penta was ill.

On October 3 the request was renewed by telephone. The parliamentary inquiry records that Baroncini first considered whether phoning Penta might amount to commissioning professional work, and consulted ENEL's director general before making contact. An informal consultation, a formal assignment, and an inspection performed on behalf of a public testing body were different kinds of acts.

The mountain did not pause while this was clarified.

Penta agreed that his assistant, **Professor Esu,** could visit. The inspection was arranged for **Friday, October 11**, with Baroncini also planning to attend.

There was now a date at which another geologist might examine the ground.

And Mount Toc was moving every day.

* * *

On **October 4**, **Caruso** went to the civil-engineering office in Belluno.

He spoke to engineer **Beghelli.** The official responsible for the particular site-surveillance assignment had changed; Caruso learned that surveyor **Lazzari** now held it.

He did not meet Lazzari, who was on strike that day.

This detail does not establish that a labor dispute caused the catastrophe. Government officials and engineers continued working, and Caruso would meet the head of the office himself four days later.

It establishes something smaller and less reassuring.

The information about the mountain still had to move among particular people in particular offices. Its delivery depended on specific encounters, reports, and the occasions on which those specific people became available.

-   The measurements were being made in the valley.
-   The people responsible for responding to them were finely distributed everywhere else.

On **October 6**, the perimeter road was becoming almost impassable.

Its pavement was broken by widening cracks, depressions, and sections that no longer met where the builders had put them. The ground was moving underneath the route.

We have now followed this road since the 1950s.

The people living on the southern bank were supposed to use it to get around the water. Now the road itself was being carried toward the water.

A **road** can be closed because the road is dangerous.

Here, closing it would also acknowledge that the land had become dangerous.

* * *

On **October 7**, workers inspecting Mount Toc found new fractures in a wooded part of the slope.

Some openings were roughly **a meter wide** and ran for about **ten meters**. Further cracks appeared during the day. Stones rolled down the hillside. People heard deep cracking noises. The long fracture already crossing the mountain was widening, and the monitored movements were accelerating.

At this point, the people working beside the lake were not confronting an obscure anomaly hidden in a laboratory reading.

The ground was coming apart where everyone could see it.

**Caruso** returned to the site and reported the deterioration to **Biadene.** The local staff, including **Dal Pra,** urged measures to remove people from the endangered part of Mount Toc and restrict access along the reservoir.

People began leaving.

The first evacuation effort concerned houses and seasonal shelters on the moving mountainside. Historical chronologies place the beginning of these measures on the evening of October 7, initially excluding **Pineda, Liron, and Prada** from the area to be cleared.

Workers and local authorities were not waiting for a complete explanation of the mountain before trying to move people out of its way.

But the line around the evacuated area followed the danger as then understood: **the ground expected to slide and the banks immediately beside the reservoir.**

This was not a warning to everyone living along the Piave below the dam.

The original problem was now showing itself in two forms.

-   A mountain could fall onto people living on the mountain.
    -   It could also fall into a lake.

* * *

On **October 8**, **Biadene and Caruso** went to Vajont and examined the worsening situation.

Caruso then met **Almo Violin,** the head of the Belluno civil-engineering office, with charts of the slope movement. He described the request for local evacuation. Violin demanded a written report and sent the government's assistant engineer to inspect the site.

The assistant engineer was **Mario Bertolissi**.

By midday, Biadene had also instructed the ENEL office in Venice to send an urgent telegram to the mayor of Erto and Casso. Its message requested immediate precautionary evacuation of people in the Mount Toc area, prohibited access around the reservoir below **730 meters above sea level**, and restricted travel on the left-bank road between Pineda and the dam.

The telegram also went to the prefect of Udine.

It did not ask for Longarone to be evacuated.

The houses on the unstable slope could be reached by falling rock, torn ground, or water rising around the basin.

**Longarone** occupied the valley below the dam, beyond the narrow gorge.

* * *

In the afternoon, **Bertolissi** went to the dam.

He examined the instruments and the movement records. His [report, dated **October 8**](https://www.senato.it/documenti/repository/relazioni/archiviostorico/vajont/4_ci_vajont_doc_n_76_bis_ocr.pdf?ref=modalpathethics.com), described measured movement accelerating from about **half a centimeter a day** in August to **five centimeters a day** toward the end of September, with readings of **ten centimeters a day** during his inspection period.

These were readings of observed points, not one universal velocity assigned to the whole mountainside.

These were nevertheless very large readings for ground expected to remain in place.

The water had been lowered from its September maximum of **710 meters** to around **702.5 meters**. The operators intended to continue drawing down at about one meter a day, hoping to avoid abrupt changes in underground pressure while giving the reservoir more space above its surface.

Bertolissi also recorded the protective actions already taken: evacuation, closures, warning signs, and continuous observation of the landslide.

According to the report, the slide was expected to move in successive blocks. The precedent was the smaller movement of November 1960, and the visible fractures were interpreted as divisions within the larger mass.

The report also repeated a wave estimate of about **25 meters** from the physical-model work and described the dam's elastic behavior as satisfactory.

-   On that basis, it judged that the available clearance, together with the protective measures, should be sufficient for the safety of people.
-   Then it stated that the seriousness of the situation remained and requested instructions from the Dams Service.

Both judgments occupied the same document.

-   A very large mountain was moving faster and faster.
-   The authorities needed to act.
    -   But the expected event was still one that the existing clearance and precautions appeared able to contain.

The instrument had supplied the shape of a manageable catastrophe.

The mountain had not agreed to match it.

* * *

**Bertolissi's** report was not sent to Rome as an instantaneous alarm.

Although dated October 8, it was delivered to **Violin's** office in Belluno on the morning of **October 9**. The memorial foundation's reconstruction states that it was then forwarded toward Rome by ordinary post that afternoon.

This report was not the only warning in circulation. **Biadene** had telephoned Rome. ENEL had sent urgent telegrams to the prefecture and municipality. Local officials were carrying out evacuations and restrictions. Policemen and engineers were visiting the roads.

It would be false to make the entire disaster depend on one envelope.

But the **envelope** had its own limitations.

The report inside described conditions on **October 8.**

It left Belluno on **October 9.**

The conditions were already changing again.

The next scheduled expert visit remained **October 11.**

* * *

On the morning of **October 9**, the Carabinieri reported that 35 families from the threatened Mount Toc area had left. The parliamentary inquiry records confirmation at 9:45 a.m. that they had found temporary accommodation in Casso.

The record also shows the limits of describing an evacuation as a single completed act. Some residents returned by day to gather potatoes and belongings. Other places around the reservoir were outside the first evacuation boundary. Later accounts describe continuing efforts to collect people and animals while the road became increasingly difficult to use.

At Vajont, the ordinary agricultural life of the valley had not stopped simply because a telegram had been sent.

In Venice, **Biadene** wrote to the absent **Pancini,** asking him to return early. He described the accelerating displacement, the road depressions, the tilting trees, and survey points toward Pineda that had begun moving after previously remaining quiet.

The observations, he wrote, made him fear the worst.

He also described the intention to lower the reservoir further, toward **695 meters**, to provide more clearance against waves.

The lake was then near **700 meters**, roughly twenty-five meters below the crest of the dam.

Back in the laboratory at Nove, **700 meters** had been given the status of an exceptionally reassuring limit under the simulated slide scenarios.

But on Mount Toc, the sliding mass was now supplying new observations that no scale model had reproduced.

The people watching it did not know exactly when it would fall.

But they were no longer waiting to find out whether it would.

* * *

Around midday, workers on the crest of the dam could see the mountainside change position without surveying instruments.

There was no need to take a reading, return to an earlier chart, and calculate a difference anymore.

A crack near the temporary buildings on the southern bank opened to about half a meter. A few hours later, witnesses found that it had widened substantially. Elsewhere, trees tipped and fell while carrying large clods of soil with their roots. The road shifted and split as the ground beneath it moved.

The survey markers were still useful. They were no longer the loudest voice in the room.

The visible movement had reached a scale at which a person could watch it during a break from work.

This still did not supply a reliable countdown. A moving slope can accelerate, pause, and move again under complex conditions. Even a visibly moving mountain does not confess the exact moment of its final collapse.

Every passing hour was changing the physical conditions under which a warning, a truck journey, or an evacuation might still be carried out.

The available time was becoming difficult to estimate.

* * *

On the afternoon of **October 9,** the engineers and authorities were still communicating.

**Biadene** tried to reach officials in Rome. A conversation with **Penta** took place late in the afternoon. Accounts of that call differ in emphasis, but the surviving later record establishes that the **Nove** model results and the **700-meter** assurance were discussed while the mountain was nearing collapse.

There were also instructions for further restrictions.

At about **five o'clock**, **Caruso** received directions to involve the Carabinieri in blocking dangerous roads. By evening, trucks could no longer use parts of the left-bank road. The company closed the route toward Mount Toc.

Later, Caruso contacted a Carabinieri officer about stopping traffic on the Alemagna state road north and south of Longarone. The instruction was relayed toward Longarone.

A **road closure** can keep new travelers from entering a dangerous stretch. It can prevent a vehicle from being trapped where the ground is collapsing.

It does not, by itself, evacuate a town beside that road.

The late traffic instructions should not be mistaken for a completed warning to the inhabitants of **Longarone, Pirago, Faè,** and the other settlements in the Piave valley.

Nor can we confidently reconstruct from these records how much of the roadblock was in place before the wave arrived.

The people responsible for transport were receiving orders.

The people in their homes below the dam were not receiving an equivalent, effective order to leave.

* * *

After dark, the instruments were still being watched.

The workmen continued observing the slope, using lights to inspect it. The damaged road had become so difficult to cross that the route used to reach the mountain was failing while efforts to protect the remaining occupants continued.

Shortly before the catastrophe, the site surveyor **Rittmeyer** spoke by telephone with **Biadene** in Venice.

Later accounts disagree about exactly what was said in the final calls.

One version has a telephone operator asking whether Longarone was in danger.

Another places a similar concern in a different call involving workers at an industrial facility below the dam.

A 1969 account of Biadene's testimony describes the surveyor telling him shortly after nine that there was no new sign of immediate danger and that he would call again if conditions changed.

The additional inspection was still scheduled for Friday.

In Longarone, below the dam, the ordinary evening continued.

**At 10:39 p.m. on Wednesday, October 9, 1963, the mountain moved.**

* * *

## **The Wave.**

A **lake** needs somewhere for its water to be.

At 10:39 p.m. on October 9, 1963, Mount Toc took that place away.

The long fracture on the northern face of the mountain had been opening for three years now. The road across it had cracked. Trees had tilted with their roots still in the soil. Survey markers had traveled centimeters a day, then faster. On the last afternoon, workers could watch the ground moving without instruments.

Now the movement ceased to be slow.

**Around 260 to 270 million cubic meters of rock began sliding toward the Vajont reservoir.** This was a tremendous part of the mountain, extending across nearly two kilometers of slope and in places hundreds of meters thick.

It carried the land that had been on it.

The tracks, the soil, the trees, the road, and the farms did not fall individually. Much of the underlying rock mass moved with enough coherence for parts of its former surface to remain recognizable afterward. Other parts broke, rotated, climbed over one another, or disappeared beneath the displaced material.

The mountain began to pour.

* * *

The slide did not fall vertically into the reservoir like a stone dropped into a well.

It moved down and across the slope, toward the opposite wall of the Vajont valley.

Later reconstructions estimate a horizontal movement of roughly **350 to 400 meters**, with speeds on the order of **20 to 30 meters per second** at the fastest stage.

That is roughly the speed of a car traveling along a highway.

A **car** is not usually several hundred million tons of limestone.

The principal runout took **tens of seconds**. Researchers have reconstructed the fast movement as around twenty to twenty-five seconds; other accounts allow as much as about forty-five seconds for the wider collapse.

None of the defensible estimates approaches the several-minute movements used in much of the earlier hydraulic testing at **Nove.**

A mass that large, crossing a valley that quickly, does not enter the water gently.

* * *

-   The reservoir surface stood near **700.4 meters above sea level**.
-   The crest of the dam stood at about **725.5 meters**.

There were roughly twenty-five meters between the lake and the top of the wall.

Engineers had been lowering the water for nearly two weeks, trying to slow Mount Toc and increase the space available for a wave.

**Now the mountain was entering a basin that contained well over a hundred million cubic meters of water.**

The moving rock’s total volume was roughly twice that amount.

Not every cubic meter of rock displaced a cubic meter of water: part of the sliding mass remained above the old water level, and the new topography had room for water in some places and none in others.

But the lake was being forced to reorganize around an object much larger than itself.

**Water** cannot ordinarily be compressed enough to make the missing space disappear.

**Water** has to move out of the way.

And the walls of the valley did not give it many directions in which to go.

* * *

The first great movement of water was toward the **northern wall** of the valley, opposite Mount Toc.

The displaced water climbed the slope below **Casso** with extraordinary force. It carried mud, broken trees, and stones, stripping vegetation and soil from places far above the ordinary shoreline. Post-event reconstructions place the highest marks in this part of the valley near **930 to 935 meters above sea level**—more than two hundred meters above the surface the lake had occupied just moments earlier.

These are elevations and run-up marks, not a claim that one flat wall of water stood more than two hundred meters tall everywhere in the reservoir. The ground was steep. A surge running up a steep mountain face can reach a very high point and still leave places only a short distance away untouched.

**Casso** was high on that northern wall.

Much of the village escaped direct inundation. Some of its lower buildings were damaged, and water spray, moving air, and rocks reached houses above the ordinary lake.

**Anna De Lorenzi** was watching the illuminated mountain from a neighbor's terrace shortly after half past ten. In testimony preserved by the Dolomiti Friulane park, she remembered the pine and larch trees moving together with the soil beneath them.

The light went out. Stones fell. Water reached the women where they sheltered beside the wall.

When she tried to return to the children she had put to bed, the way across the street was flooded.

That street had not been a lake a moment earlier.

Casso's position had still spared most of the settlement from the surge.

* * *

Water that climbs a slope must descend again unless it finds a place to remain.

As the surge fell back from the northern side, it divided across the new landscape.

-   Some water rushed back over the fallen rock.
-   Some traveled **east**, upstream through the remaining reservoir.
-   Some ran **west**, toward the dam.

There were additional waves along the shores and reflections from the narrowing valley. The water was not divided into two tidy, separately labeled objects, one for the lake and one for **Longarone.**

Modern hydraulic reconstructions find several interacting fronts during the first minute, including a surge traveling along the northern bank toward **Erto** and renewed flow toward the dam after water descended from the opposite slope.

The hillsides determined what these waves could reach.

The center of Erto, on higher ground, escaped the worst of the inundation. The water reached lower parts of its surroundings, and the settlements closer to the shore were exposed to a very different event.

**Frasègn, Le Spesse, Il Cristo, Pineda, Ceva, Prada, Marzana, and San Martino** were among the hamlets and groups of houses destroyed or severely struck around the reservoir.

Those names were the places joined by those bridges, fields, and roads we met when the reservoir was first being planned.

Some families had objected because the new lake would make a journey longer. Some had been relocated or compensated.

Others had continued living and working around the shore.

* * *

The landslide itself was building another barrier.

The rock that had crossed the valley came to rest against the opposite slope, raising the floor of the reservoir by a tremendous amount. For a long stretch of the valley, land took the place of open water. The deposit obstructed the old connection between the upstream and downstream parts of the lake.

This was the event for which that **bypass tunnel** had been constructed.

The tunnel through the northern bank could provide a route for water around a landslide that divided the reservoir. It addressed what would happen _after_ rock occupied the valley and the streams continued supplying water upstream.

It could not prevent the displacement taking place above it right now. An opening roughly four and a half meters in diameter was not a place into which a vast, rapidly moving wave could obediently drain and disappear.

The **tunnel** and the **wave** belonged to two different timescales.

The deposit also made new depressions. Where portions of the fallen mass rotated backward, water collected. The photographs taken on October 10 show a new little lake in the Massalezza area, enclosed by the wreckage of the mountain. Farther upstream, another body of water remained behind the fallen rock.

The original reservoir had become several different things.

-   **Rock** where there had been water.
-   **Water** where there had been rock.
    -   **And water still moving toward the dam.**

* * *

The **dam** was a double-curved arch, designed to take water pressure on one face and carry that force into its rocky abutments.

The **wave** came from above that face.

Water surged west through the narrow end of the basin, rose above the crest, and poured over and around the top of the structure. Later engineering accounts describe the surge reaching **more than a hundred meters above the crest** in the dam area, although the exact height depends on the location and method of reconstruction.

The concrete arch did not need to crack for this to happen.

Its top was just now below the moving water.

The roadway over the crest was torn away. So were the control buildings, the nearby working installations, and parts of the equipment attached to the dam.

But the main wall and its abutments remained substantially intact.

The engineers had built a dam capable of remaining a dam under extraordinarily violent loading.

And there was water on the wrong side of it.

The quantity sent beyond the wall is commonly estimated at **about twenty-five million cubic meters**. This is a reconstruction, not to be confused with the often-cited **fifty million cubic meters** describing the larger mass of water set in violent motion in the reservoir.

Water crossed a broad and irregular crest and passed around the nearby high ground. A flow for which the operating outlets had no adequate control had found a much larger exit.

* * *

Below the dam, the **Vajont gorge** narrowed sharply before opening into the **Piave valley.**

The overtopping water crashed into it carrying air, stone, wood, and whatever else it had picked up. The gorge gave the flood steep walls and very little room or reason to spread sideways. Air was driven ahead of and around the rushing water; a burst of wind and spray accompanied the arrival of the flood.

Some structures were damaged by that wave of pressure and airborne debris. Others were struck by the water itself. The mechanisms cannot always be separated in the rubble afterward.

An early engineering reconstruction put the flood at around **seventy meters high** where the narrow Vajont gorge met the wider Piave valley.

**Longarone** stood opposite that opening.

The town occupied a working valley along the Piave, with its own river, bridges, factories, streets, homes, and routes to other settlements.

The flood emerged from the side gorge into that valley and spread along the paths the terrain allowed.

It struck **Longarone** and the nearby settlements.

The current tore through buildings and foundations, carried debris, damaged roads and railway works, and continued along the river valley. When the initial surge lost its forward force, water rushed back across parts of the devastated ground, cutting and carrying material in the opposite direction.

**Pirago, Villanova, Rivalta, and Faè** suffered catastrophic destruction. **Codissago** and other places along the Piave were damaged as the flood traveled both up and down the wider valley. At Pirago, the church bell tower survived where much of the village around it did not.

When daylight returned, the surviving people had to enter the world left behind by the water.

* * *

## **The Morning After.**

When the lights went out at the cinema in Belluno, **Paolo Bolzan** did not know that Longarone had been destroyed.

He knew something had happened to the electricity.

Bolzan was the deputy commander of the provincial fire brigade. He was attending a film at the municipal theatre when the room went dark. Outside, he discovered that the rest of Belluno had gone dark too.

That was strange. The city's lighting was divided into sectors. A failure in one part was not supposed to extinguish all of them.

Bolzan started toward the fire station.

He heard the siren before he reached it.

* * *

At around **10:55 p.m. on October 9**, a caller from **Ponte nelle Alpi** had reported deep booms, damaged electrical lines, and a Piave River visibly rising. The fire brigade suspected that a dam upstream had broken.

This was wrong about the dam.

The dam was fine.

It was still a reasonable first explanation for the evidence arriving in Belluno.

The lines had failed. Water was coming down the river. Something enormous had happened upstream, and the people who might have explained it from Longarone were among the people it had struck.

Bolzan sent a radio-equipped vehicle toward the river with instructions to warn communities farther downstream. More units followed. Officers attempted to telephone the Carabinieri at Longarone, but the connection was broken.

A **telephone** works pretty badly when the place you are calling has lost the lines leading into it.

There were now two problems.

-   The rescuers needed to know where the catastrophe was.
    -   And they needed a way to get there.

* * *

The first useful message to reach the firefighters north of Longarone took a different route.

At **11:15 p.m.**, somebody arrived in **Pieve di Cadore** in a private vehicle with a Savona registration plate. The traveler told **De Polo**, who ran the Hotel Cadore, that there had been a disaster at Longarone.

De Polo called **Antonio Bergamo**, commander of the local volunteer fire detachment.

Bergamo dispatched an ambulance and a small field vehicle. He warned the Carabinieri, called for firefighters from neighboring Cadore towns, and arranged for medical assistance.

At **11:40**, the ambulance reached the first houses still standing on the northern edge of Longarone. It began taking injured people to a clinic in Pieve di Cadore. Bergamo arrived minutes later, picked up an injured woman, and drove back to organize more assistance.

The trip had already supplied him with enough information to know this was no ordinary flood response.

He tried calling his superiors. Some telephone lines were unavailable; others were occupied by people reporting the same catastrophe.

The rescue system was now assembling itself from a car arriving at a hotel, a hotelier's telephone, an ambulance, neighboring volunteer detachments, and whatever roads remained passable.

There had been no completed map of the damage waiting for them at the firehouse.

They were drawing that map by trying to drive through it.

* * *

The first rescuers were also the people who had survived.

Neighbors came out of the parts of Longarone that remained standing and entered the devastated ground while it was still dark. They listened for voices and moved toward them. They found injured people among timber, broken stone, mud, and possessions that no longer lay anywhere near the rooms from which they came.

In the testimony later assembled by historians, one survivor, **Micaela,** remembered lying in bed as a violent force carried her away. She was found roughly **400 meters** from her home. Another survivor, **Giuseppe Sacchet,** was discovered still on his mattress after it had traveled far from the place where he had been sleeping.

The **bed** was still a bed.

It was just no longer in the bedroom.

The people doing the searching faced another difficulty: the familiar spatial clues by which someone would ordinarily find their neighbor had been removed.

There might be no front door anymore. No house number. No shop at the corner. No street with its previous edges.

A person could know Longarone intimately and still be unable to find the place where their friend had lived the evening before.

* * *

The firefighters approaching from north and south met in the early hours of **October 10**.

**Stefano Gabotto,** the senior fire-service commander directing the response, reached the devastated area in the morning. By then teams were arriving from Belluno, Cadore, and elsewhere. The Italian Army sent Alpini and other units. Carabinieri, police, physicians, volunteers, and civilians were already working in different parts of the valley.

The river had divided the response as effectively as it had divided the survivors.

Gabotto organized the firefighters into three principal sectors:

-   a **northern** sector around Castellavazzo,
-   a **southern** sector near Villanova, and
-   an **eastern** sector toward Erto and Casso, above the dam.

Their immediate tasks were not identical.

-   The **north** needed to reach people cut off by ruined crossings.
-   The **east** needed roads cleared and boats to reach isolated shores of the damaged reservoir.
-   The **south** needed access through the destroyed settlements and along the Piave.

By that morning the fire-service report records around 500 firefighters operating under this arrangement, alongside much larger military and civilian efforts. More units, heavy machinery, helicopters, and boats continued arriving through the day.

The organization was working.

Its equipment still had to meet the valley as the flood had left it.

* * *

At **Codissago**, a bridge over the Piave had disappeared.

A **bridge** is the sort of thing people notice when it is missing. Normally it just joins two banks without requiring anyone to think too seriously about the river between.

-   The people at Codissago were now on one bank.
-   Assistance, supplies, and many of the arriving rescuers were on the other.

During the first night, when the water fell enough for an attempt, firefighters crossed through the river to reach injured people. An early account describes five volunteers wading through water up to their armpits to rescue five people endangered by the current.

The next job was to make the crossing ordinary enough that it could be used repeatedly.

On **October 10**, firefighters established a ferry using a small plastic river boat between Codissago and **Castellavazzo.**

On October 11, the service carried 700 people by four in the afternoon, along with drinking water. The next day it carried another 800, besides food, medical supplies, and other necessities.

But Codissago also needed clean water without having to transport every container across the river.

So firefighters and military engineers laid a **temporary water pipe across the Piave.** The same operational record mentions supplying water to a bakery at Castellavazzo.

A **bakery** requires water to make bread. A community trying to continue after a disaster requires bread, along with many other things.

* * *

On the **Erto** side, the route from the Passo di Sant'Osvaldo toward San Martino was blocked by landslide material and debris. Fire teams had reached the area by early morning but could not take their vehicles through.

They first searched damaged hamlets and moved the injured. Once the obstruction was cleared sufficiently, they launched an outboard boat to cross the remaining water and reach people isolated on the other shore.

Later operations included evacuation by boat around **Pineda.** Helicopters supplied another way across broken roads and difficult terrain. American Army aviators participated alongside Italian responders; one survivor, **Giovanni De Lorenzi,** later remembered U.S. helicopters helping evacuate hundreds from his community.

The lake that had once separated fields from houses now separated rescuers from people who had survived its wave.

Same body of water.

Its work had changed again.

* * *

Some of the flood's material continued downstream after the first night.

The Piave had carried timber, concrete, machinery, and human beings through the valley. Piles of wood were gathered against bridges and hydraulic works farther south, blocking water that needed to keep flowing.

The firefighters cleared those obstructions, partly to reopen the river and partly because people were still missing beneath them.

Other objects carried a danger of their own.

Industrial stores had been swept out of damaged premises, including drums containing **cyanide compounds.**

The fire-service records describe recovering many of these containers from the river corridor and sending divers to examine the basin near Busche for others.

The flood was over in minutes.

Some of its cargo took weeks to find.

* * *

Finding people was the most difficult part.

The water had carried bodies away from the streets where they lived. Search parties worked along the riverbed, in trees and piles of timber, beneath mud and collapsed structures, and at downstream barriers where the current had deposited debris.

The [original fire-service account](https://vajont.info/vigilidelFuoco/corpoVV.FF.opereVajont.html?ref=modalpathethics.com) credits its personnel, over the whole emergency operation, with **73 people saved** and **1,243 bodies recovered**.

Other organizations and local people were searching too.

The number of injured survivors was tragically small beside the dead, particularly in the places that received the strongest part of the wave. The first work of rescue gave way, in many areas, to recovery.

The death toll is often given today as **1,917** by Italy's Civil Protection Department. Other historical and memorial institutions use slightly different counts. The Civil Protection account states that around **400 people were never found**.

The early newspapers could only estimate. They gave figures far above and below the numbers established later, because nobody yet knew exactly who had been in the destroyed places that night.

The town had a register of residents. It did not have a record of every visitor, traveler, temporary worker, and person sleeping somewhere other than home.

And the number of bodies recovered could not tell investigators how many bodies were still unrecovered.

A list of missing people had to be made from survivors, relatives, records, and absences.

There were families for whom no one remained to answer.

* * *

On **October 10**, officials began making arrangements for a dedicated burial ground.

The next day, they selected a site near **Fortogna,** south of Longarone, in a field that had been used to grow maize. It was outside the area reached by the fatal wave.

On **October 12**, the ground was blessed. Burials began on **October 13**.

Coffins arrived from temporary collection places and cemeteries along the Piave. The rescue record describes transporting remains to these places and making photographs and written observations wherever possible to assist later identification.

The maize field became a cemetery because the town's old burial arrangements could not meet the new demand.

Some graves received names. Others could not.

A person might have survived the destruction of a home and still never know where a husband, child, parent, or neighbor had been buried. Some families never recovered any remains.

Fortogna became a permanent place in the lives of the survivors.

The cemetery was built quickly.

The work of identifying and mourning the dead could not be completed on its schedule.

* * *

A municipal government had to continue operating as well.

Longarone's mayor, **Guglielmo Celso,** had been killed.

**Terenzio Arduini,** the deputy mayor who assumed responsibility, had lost his son. He had seen the young man that evening at the railway-station bar operated by the family.

Other municipal employees were among the dead. Some survived because they lived in the higher part of Longarone that escaped the flood.

The town hall at **Palazzo Mazzolà** remained among the buildings in the surviving area, but municipal offices could not simply resume ordinary work the next morning.

There _was_ no ordinary work to resume.

People needed food vouchers and clothing. Injured residents needed help. Children whose parents had been killed needed care. Relatives arrived from elsewhere in Italy and abroad asking who had survived. Donations began reaching the town, and somebody had to record them, safeguard them, and decide how to distribute them.

The municipal secretary, **Mario Laveder,** helped rescuers during the first hours. **Ezio Zuliani,** another municipal official, later remembered him throwing mattresses from windows so the injured could be placed on them for transport.

When the municipal offices were unusable, Laveder kept emergency accounts at his own home.

The employees responsible for the population register began the work of discovering who still lived in the municipality. In later council records, the historian Ferruccio Vendramini found crosses placed beside the names of absent council members, including the mayor.

* * *

The human scale of the loss is difficult to hold inside one casualty total.

Longarone's local historical account records **1,452 victims associated with 508 families** in the municipality, including **305 families it describes as completely lost**.

An entire family can disappear from a town's records in a way that a destroyed building cannot explain.

There is nobody to apply for a replacement house.

Nobody to collect their belongings.

No one asks the school what became of a child's books.

Other families survived in fragments. Children were placed with relatives or other carers. Adults who had been living and working abroad returned to find that the place they intended to come home to had changed beyond recognition.

* * *

On **October 18**, nine days after the catastrophe, the surviving administration sent an appeal to the government's extraordinary commissioner, **Giacomo Sedati.**

It was signed by **Arduini** and **Laveder.** **Zuliani** had helped prepare the typescript through the night. Copies went to the president of the Republic, the prime minister, ministers, parliamentarians, military authorities, and other offices that might have the power to make the demands consequential.

The document had a title.

> **Ricostruiamo Longarone.**

> _Let us rebuild Longarone._

The survivors were not asking for the town to exist again only on a commemorative plaque. They wanted it rebuilt where it had stood.

That demand contained practical questions which the mountain, the dam, and the water had not answered.

-   Was the valley safe enough to inhabit?
-   Who would pay for housing and public works?
-   Could industry return?
-   What happened to the schools, the hospital connections, the shops, the roads, and the services on which neighboring settlements depended?

They were not finished with Longarone.

Meanwhile, the concrete arch remained in the gorge.

The town below it was beginning the work of returning.

And the files explaining how the hell a functioning hydroelectric project had just destroyed it were becoming the next contested objects in the valley.

* * *

## **The Second Record.**

The mountain destroyed Longarone in a few minutes.

It left most of the paperwork intact.

The geological reports were still there. So were the reservoir-level charts, the drawings of the dam, the calculations from Nove, the authorizations to fill the lake, the telegrams, the inspections, and correspondence between the engineers who had been trying to understand Mount Toc while it was moving.

This produced a second and considerably slower operation.

The mountain had to be reconstructed from documents.

So did the decisions made about it.

* * *

In the first days after **October 9,** investigators returned to a site whose basic geography had changed.

The old shoreline could no longer be walked. The landslide occupied much of the reservoir. Rock formerly visible on **Mount Toc** now lay against the opposite slope. The small road that had been built to replace the villagers' bridge was part of the wreckage.

The dam was fine.

An investigator could inspect its concrete, examine the stripped crest, and establish that the arch had survived the wave. But the survival of the dam did not explain why the wave had been thought manageable.

For _that_, the investigator had to go back.

-   What did the first geologists see?
-   What was known after the **November 1960** slide?
-   What did the boreholes fail to find?
-   Which conclusions came from the model at **Nove**?
    -   Who received them?
-   What was permitted when the lake reached **700 meters?**
    -   What changed when the permission rose to **715?**
-   Who had the last measurements from **October 9?**

Those questions belonged to more than one institution.

The state had licensed the project and supervised its construction.

**SADE** had built the dam and commissioned much of the technical research.

**ENEL** had inherited the works and continued the final filling.

And the people of **Erto, Casso, Longarone,** and the surrounding settlements had been living with the consequences of those decisions for years.

All of them had records.

They did not have the same records.

* * *

The Minister of Public Works appointed an administrative commission chaired by **Carlo Bozzi,** president of the Council of State. It began its work within days of the catastrophe and delivered its report in **January 1964**.

The commission had to examine a familiar engineering problem under very unfamiliar conditions.

It could study the cracks, inspect the deposits, reconstruct the reservoir, and compare the physical outcome with the earlier calculations.

It could also examine the correspondence through which the project had received its geological warnings.

The administrative inquiry was critical of two parts of that history.

-   It found that SADE's communication of the developing danger and the studies undertaken after the 1960 slide had been too partial, especially in its dealings with the public testing authorities.
-   It also criticized the failure of local government and administrative offices to coordinate adequately around protection of the population in the final period.

These were findings of the Bozzi inquiry, not criminal convictions. The people named and criticized would contest them, and a later parliamentary inquiry would give parts of the same record a different interpretation.

SADE submitted objections to the ministry's conclusions in May 1964. Former prefects submitted their own memoranda defending their conduct.

The criminal investigation followed another route.

The prosecutor in **Belluno** assembled technical experts, obtained the engineering papers, and examined whether the disaster had been caused or aggravated by negligent decisions. The work passed to investigating judge **Mario Fabbri.**

A **courtroom** needs a different kind of answer from a geologist.

-   A geologist may explain how a rock mass failed.
-   A criminal court must identify conduct attributable to particular defendants, the duties attaching to that conduct, the relevant knowledge available to them, and a legally sufficient relation between what they did or failed to do and the deaths that followed.

Neither can perform the other's work by changing the heading on its report.

And while those investigations continued, the survivors still needed houses.

That got a third body of paperwork.

On **November 4, 1963**, less than a month after the disaster, Italy enacted a relief law authorizing an initial **ten billion lire.** The money was divided among immediate assistance, restoration of public works, urban planning and settlement needs, and rebuilding privately owned structures.

Trouble was that **allocations** were not identical to completed buildings or reopened streets.

A family could be eligible for reconstruction support without having a house to sleep in tonight. A town could have a plan without having a school or a factory. A government could approve a road that did not yet exist.

The survivors of Longarone had already sent their own demand to rebuild where the town stood.

Now they had to turn that into a sequence of public decisions, money, surveys, plans, contracts, and foundations.

-   There were investigations into why the old town had been lost.
-   There were also plans for where a new town should go.

And the latter could not safely wait for the former to finish.

* * *

The [parliamentary inquiry](https://patrimonio.archivio.senato.it/inventario/archivi-commissioni-parlamentari-inchiesta/vajont-iv-leg?ref=modalpathethics.com) came next.

Three bills proposing an investigation had been introduced on **October 16, 1963**, one week after the catastrophe. The law establishing the inquiry was not enacted until **May 22, 1964**.

Its commission assembled in July under **Senator Leopoldo Rubinacci.** Thirty deputies and senators would investigate the causes of the disaster, public and private responsibilities, the adequacy of the governing rules, and the assistance being given to the survivors.

They divided the work.

-   One group examined the technical studies.
-   Another investigated the laws and offices responsible for dams.
-   A third rebuilt the chronology.
-   A fourth examined relief and reconstruction.

The record was enormous. It ran from an early proposal to dam the valley through decades of permits, drawings, modifications, surveys, water-level changes, laboratory experiments, and the final calls made on October 9. There was enough material to fill an archive.

Some of it was already in the custody of the criminal investigators, who had their own obligations concerning evidence and witnesses. The parliamentary commission had to negotiate access without taking over the prosecution.

The court and Parliament were examining the same event for different purposes.

The documents traveled between them under conditions that neither could simply ignore.

This was already much more complicated than ordering the surviving engineers to explain themselves and writing down what they said.

* * *

The parliamentary commissioners reached agreement on one part of their work.

On **May 11, 1965**, they submitted a first report concerning the people still living after the disaster. It covered emergency assistance, public works, the restoration of safe conditions, and reconstruction. This part had been approved unanimously.

It also recorded the problem created by the effort to plan the whole region's redevelopment while destroyed towns were still awaiting houses.

Longarone had a general rebuilding plan. The larger regional plans were intended to organize roads, industry, settlements, and future economic life across a much broader territory. Those larger plans might be useful, but they were slower to complete.

The commission supported allowing the immediate rebuilding of Longarone and Castellavazzo to proceed separately, rather than making every house wait for the entire region to finish deciding its future. A December 1964 law had already given the approved local plan independent effect for that purpose.

A regional plan might improve the eventual reconstruction. It did not have to hold every urgent reconstruction decision hostage while the rest of its map was drawn.

There was also disagreement about where the people of Erto and Casso should live. Some wished to remain in their original communities; others needed or preferred relocation. The new settlement eventually built near Maniago would acquire the name **Vajont** and become a municipality in **1971**.

* * *

Agreement about rebuilding did not produce agreement about the history of the dam.

The commission's final report was adopted in **July 1965**, by nineteen votes to eight. Two minority reports were attached: one associated with **Franco Busetto** and other Communist parliamentarians, the other with Socialist commissioners **Ercole Bonacina** and **Luigi Ferroni.**

There were now three substantial political accounts of the same project.

The majority gave considerable weight to the exceptional characteristics of the October 9 slide. It observed that the catastrophe had occurred in a form not predicted by the experts. The known mountain had been studied extensively, and the investigations had involved distinguished geologists, geophysicists, engineers, and state officials.

It also argued that the state had not simply surrendered its powers to SADE. The administrative proceedings had been extensive, with prescriptions, inspections, tests, and controls. The inquiry found no adequate basis to conclude that unlawful company pressure had determined the public authorities' decisions.

This did not mean the majority believed the system had worked perfectly. It identified deficiencies in the organization of technical supervision, the state's ability to employ independent specialists, and the division of responsibilities among offices. It called for more continuous oversight and a broader assessment of public safety.

There is an important restraint in the majority's report as well. It distinguished the observation that **nobody had predicted the event in its actual form** from the criminal question of whether the failure to foresee it involved culpable negligence. That judgment, it said, belonged to the courts.

A disaster can be _unprecedented_ in its precise mechanics while the decision to expose people to a poorly understood danger remains open to criticism.

The majority supplied a defensible question about the limits of knowledge.

It also supplied an interpretation of the whole institutional history that many of its colleagues refused to accept.

* * *

The Communist minority examined the same warnings and asked a different question.

After the fractures and the first large movements on Mount Toc, experts had continued disagreeing about the depth and speed of the possible slide. Francesco Penta had left serious alternatives unresolved. One of those alternatives carried a far greater danger than the operating assumptions eventually represented.

> Why, the minority asked, had an experiment involving a populated valley been allowed to continue while that alternative remained alive?

The engineers could not be required to know the exact future motion of every rock.

The government, however, had authority over whether the lake could be filled at all.

For these commissioners, that made a decision to suspend filling more important than a successful prediction of the final minute.

Their report also challenged the relationship between a powerful private concessionaire and the state departments charged with reviewing it.

They saw the accumulation of technical studies, project decisions, and operational knowledge inside the company as a structural weakness in public control.

The Socialists' minority report took a related, though not identical, view. It described SADE as an enterprise able to select and modify proposals, control much of the technical information, and obtain the authorizations on which its undertaking depended. The state's separate powers, it argued, had too often failed to amount to independent public direction.

Its authors reached for a memorable image:

> The state was **everywhere and nowhere**.

An administrative system that could be present at every formal stage without taking possession of the entire safety problem.

The majority disputed that characterization.

There was one further disagreement about the record itself.

The majority rejected a proposal to publish the commission's hearing minutes alongside the three reports. The minutes and supporting documents still entered the parliamentary archive, but the final printed volume did not supply every reader with the full testimony in the same package.

-   The investigation had collected the evidence.
-   The conclusions were public.
    -   The route from the evidence to those conclusions still required bonus work.

* * *

Then a judge had to assign responsibility to particular people.

On **February 20, 1968**, **Judge Fabbri** issued the order bringing the criminal case to trial. The investigation had taken years. Its case involved company managers, engineers, public regulators, and experts whose work we have followed since the first chapters of this Tale.

Some of the original figures were already dead. **Carlo Semenza,** who designed the great dam, had died in **1961**, before the catastrophe. **Giorgio Dal Piaz** had died in **1962**. **Penta** had died after the disaster, before trial.

**Mario Pancini,** who had supervised works at Vajont and left on vacation shortly before the final collapse, died by suicide on **November 28, 1968,** on the eve of the trial.

The proceedings moved to **L'Aquila**, away from the devastated region, amid concerns about conducting the trial impartially in the area most directly affected.

The hearing began at the end of November 1968.

There were photographs of the missing town and measurements of the fallen mountain.

The court had to determine what defendants had been obliged and able to know before those photographs existed.

And it had to decide which failure was theirs.

* * *

On **December 17, 1969**, the court delivered its first judgment.

It convicted **Alberico Biadene, Curzio Batini, and Almo Violin** of multiple negligent homicides, sentencing each to six years, of which two were covered by an amnesty.

It held them responsible for failing to give warning and set evacuation in motion adequately.

But it did not uphold the prosecution's wider account that the landslide and flood had been criminally foreseeable as they occurred. On those charges, the defendants were acquitted. Others, including hydraulic modeler **Augusto Ghetti,** were acquitted of the charges against them.

A failure to evacuate could be legally established without treating the whole engineering operation that produced the fatal wave as a foreseeably disastrous undertaking.

In the judgment's account, the criminal responsibility lay principally at the last opportunity to protect people.

That was **not** the account the prosecutors and civil parties had sought.

The case continued.

* * *

The appeal court returned to the earlier sequence.

On **October 3, 1970**, it convicted **Biadene and Francesco Sensidoni,** a senior public-works engineer who had been involved in the dam's supervisory arrangements. It recognized responsibility for the landslide, inundation, and deaths, with the foreseeability of the disastrous event treated differently from the first judgment.

Biadene received six years. Sensidoni received four and a half. Three years of each sentence were covered by amnesty. Other defendants were acquitted; Batini's proceedings had been separated because of serious illness.

The appeal changed the object of legal attention.

The earlier decisions to operate a reservoir beside a moving mountain above a populated town had reentered the criminal account of the disaster.

The appeal court did not settle every disputed question about the mountain's ancient history, or establish that an engineer in 1960 could have calculated the exact velocity of the October 1963 slide. Criminal foreseeability and exact geomechanical prediction remained different demands.

The final review arrived in Rome.

On **March 25, 1971**, the Court of Cassation left convictions standing against Biadene and Sensidoni. It treated the slide and inundation within one negligent-disaster offense, while retaining the legally significant finding of foreseeability.

-   Biadene's sentence became **five years**, with three covered by amnesty.
-   Sensidoni's became **three years and eight months**, also with three covered.

The other acquittals stood.

The final ruling came only days before the relevant criminal limitation period would have expired.

The dam was still fine.

* * *

The civil cases did not end with the criminal ones.

Liability for damaged public property, lost municipal resources, compensation to affected communities, and the financial obligations of ENEL and SADE's successor **Montedison** continued through later courts.

A 1982 decision changed the allocation of civil liability. Further judgments and proceedings followed into the 1990s, and a settlement among the state and the corporate successors arrived decades after the night of the disaster.

This was not a continuation of the original criminal trial. It concerned different claims, different parties, and the material cost of what had been destroyed.

The surviving people still had decisions of their own to make, and the reconstruction was not uniform.

Longarone was rebuilt on and around the ground of the former town, with altered roads, new buildings, and a different arrangement of public space. Some families from Erto and Casso moved to the new settlement near Maniago. Others remained attached to their original villages and land.

* * *

The criminal judgment became final. The civil claims continued.

The question the investigations had not resolved in one voice was how all the earlier observations had come to support continued operation of a reservoir beside a moving mountain with a town full of people underneath it.

The record remained open to that question, even after the last criminal sentence.

* * *

## **The Distortion.**

**The Vajont Dam** was very good at being a dam.

Its concrete arch carried enormous pressure into the rock of the gorge. It survived the wave that crossed it in October 1963.

Northern Italy needed electricity through the winter, not just during the spring melt. **Soverzene** was already converting moving water into power. The **Piave–Boite–Maè–Vajont network** could store water, direct it through tunnels, and release it where electrical demand required. The operators did not invent that need, and the machinery did not fail to produce useful energy because the enterprise ended in disaster.

The rock had its own history. Its fracture surfaces were not necessarily the surfaces the engineers had found. Rain entered from above. Reservoir water entered from below. The mountain moved in ways that changed as these conditions changed.

The survey markers said that the slope was moving. The boreholes found fractured, water-permeable limestone, although they failed to intercept the suspected deep sliding surface. Geophysical tests produced measurements compatible with strong rock, while geological fieldwork developed a very different question about whether a coherent mass of that rock could move. Drawdowns were followed by quieter readings at the watched points. The bypass tunnel was engineered to carry water around a possible new obstruction.

All of these observations deserved to be taken seriously.

**The distortion came when their separate achievements were permitted to stand in for safety of the connected field.**

Strong rock at the gorge could support an arch without making the reservoir slopes stable. Missing the deep sliding surface with a borehole did not show that the surface was absent. A previous slowing during drawdown could not guarantee that the mountain would obey the same control on the next cycle. A tunnel capable of carrying a continuing flow around a fallen mass could not absorb a wave formed while that mass entered the reservoir.

And the model at Nove could measure the waves made by the mountain the researchers had constructed.

They built a detailed model. They changed it in response to criticism. They imposed deliberately fast movements and observed the resulting water. In one demanding experiment, some water passed over the crest. Those were all real measurements, not a fabricated answer.

But the model's mountain entered the lake in separated, mechanically prescribed motions. The real Mount Toc moved much faster, with far more of the mass acting together. The physical experiment did not supply the event that ultimately needed to be survived. Its **700-meter** assurance depended on those experimental conditions, and the study of the flood below the dam remained incomplete.

On October 9, the mountain supplied the missing experiment.

It was performed once. Longarone was downstream.

* * *

## **The Ruling.**

Vajont does not establish that models are useless or that modern electricity production is inherently destructive. The instruments all did valuable work here. The point of reconstructing the whole field is to know exactly what each one accomplished, and where its authority ended.

That is the distortion: **a succession of valid but bounded instruments supplied an increasingly powerful account of a system that remained dangerously under-described in the very relations capable of killing almost 2,000 people.**

There was no single actor who possessed the entire future of the mountain. Scientists disagreed about its history and failure surface. Operators observed behavior they could influence at times without being able to guarantee control. Public officials made requests for further study. Residents reported what was happening to the roads, buildings, and ground on which they lived.

Their evidence did not all have the same standing, or travel through the same offices, or answer the same question.

We cannot prove that one particular earlier drawdown would have prevented the October slide, or that any particular emergency evacuation could have been completed once the road began collapsing. The geological failure itself remains contested in some of its details. Foreseeability, culpability, and the scope of a technical model are separate questions.

We can still establish something narrower and sufficient: the project continued raising and lowering a reservoir beside a known moving mass without securing an adequate account of the rapid slide-wave event and its downstream consequences.

That unresolved hazard called for limits on further operation, a more demanding appraisal of wave scenarios, and protective plans reaching beyond the nearest shoreline. Those were prospective responsibilities; their absence does not prove that one particular alternative would have prevented the slide. In the final days, the visible deterioration did not produce protective action over the full area that the possible waves could reach.

The criminal courts eventually recognized more than a last-minute failure to warn. Their judgments changed as the record was examined. The first court distinguished failures of evacuation from the disaster's broader foreseeability; the appeal and final court extended criminal responsibility to the foreseeable catastrophic event itself. Those legal findings did not resolve every geological dispute or rebuild the destroyed town.

The original project needed a large lake because water could be carried through a connected electrical system. That was its strength. It was also the beginning of the obligation that the project did not meet: **every connected object that could carry harm belonged to the safety question, including the people and places outside the model.**

Mount Toc had a different shape from the laboratory's model.

And it had a different clock.

At 10:39 p.m., the water crossed the dam.

Longarone had to begin again.
