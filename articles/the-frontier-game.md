# Applied Case: The Frontier Game

On November 24, [Axios reported](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/24/anthropic-openai-unreleased-ai-safety-risks?ref=modalpathethics.com) that researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic were increasingly concerned about whether their unreleased models could ever be safely released.

One possibility being discussed is that frontier laboratories continue developing these systems and just use them internally.

Oh.

In August, Meta published [_The Future is for Everyone_](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/08/the-future-is-for-everyone/?ref=modalpathethics.com), in which Mark Zuckerberg made the opposite argument. Concentrating superintelligence inside a small number of institutions would itself constitute an enormous danger.

Zuckerberg gives the example of a superintelligent lawyer. A world in which **one person** has access to that lawyer is an absolutely awful place to be sued. A world in which _everyone_ has access to one could be a considerable improvement over the current legal system.

There is a very good argument here.

But I would also like to know who is supplying the judge's artificial intelligence.

And the court clerk's.

And whether the defendant can investigate the company supplying all three.

On November 25, [Axios reported a growing disagreement](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/25/anthropic-ai-human-control-debate-free-will?ref=modalpathethics.com) among frontier AI executives over how much authority humanity ought to surrender to a superior intelligence.

Dario Amodei has since [pushed back](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/25/anthropic-dario-amodei-ai-free-will-contro-axios?ref=modalpathethics.com), distinguishing the automation of particular functions from the surrender of democratic governance, and accusing his competitors of advertising human agency without doing much to actually distinguish their products.

Amodei has a point.

The emancipatory argument of [_The Inner Apocalypse_](https://modalpathethics.com/the-inner-apocalypse-has-been-published-free-download/) was that artificial intelligence might finally relieve human beings of the work civilization has historically required them to become.

Insisting that a human being continue performing every consequential function would defeat much of the purpose of the technology.

But the book also spent considerable time establishing why human beings can remain politically in charge of a civilization that has already lost the capacity to correct itself.

-   The companies are now arguing over who gets to drive.
-   I am still concerned about who owns the road.

## Bernie Has Arrived.

Axios has since [returned with the senators](https://www.axios.com/2026/11/25/democrats-ai-safety-restrictions-openai-anthropic-axios?ref=modalpathethics.com). Mark Kelly wants independent examiners inside the laboratories. Bernie Sanders wants advanced artificial intelligence development paused immediately.

On November 24, I published [_The Year of 0.7_](https://modalpathethics.com/the-year-of-0-7/), which contained a section entitled **Bernie Sanders Has Been Looking for This Fucking Thing.**

Sanders and Casar's proposed legislation already targets the capacity to automate or greatly accelerate artificial intelligence research. OpenAI is now publishing measurements of that acceleration, and its chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, anticipates another transformative emergence of capability around 0.68–0.70 on the company's internal pretraining metric.

This decimal measures prediction error. Lower is better. It is neither a general intelligence score nor an established safety threshold.

Pachocki does not know what those capabilities will be.

As I argued in [_The Superintelligence Ban Cannot Find the Superintelligence_](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-the-superintelligence-ban-cannot-find-the-superintelligence/), dangerous capacities can emerge through changes in tools, inference and institutional organization without the decimal moving at all.

And in October's [_Abdul El-Sayed and the 50/50_](https://modalpathethics.com/abdul-el-sayed-and-the-public-that-owns-half-the-machine/), Modal Path Ethics put a publicly controlled frontier laboratory into a dispute with a public safety regulator.

The laboratory wanted to release the model. The regulator wanted to stop it. Both institutions were acting under democratic authority.

-   Suppose the laboratory is right.
    -   How does it overturn the regulator's decision?
-   Suppose the regulator is right.
    -   How does it enforce its decision against the company in which the public now has an enormous financial interest?
-   Suppose neither possesses sufficient independent technical competence to settle the dispute without the intelligence being disputed.
    -   **Who gets to use the computer?**

## Return to the Release Gradient II.

On August 7, three days before Meta's announcement, Modal Path Ethics published [_Field Instruments: Open Weights and the Release Gradient_](https://modalpathethics.com/field-instruments-open-weights-and-the-release-gradient/).

That argument concerned two dangers.

-   A dangerous model released into independent custody may remain operational long after its developer discovers a reason to withdraw it.
-   A model retained by its developer may become the instrument through which the developer acquires extraordinary power over the institutions that come to depend upon it.

_The Release Gradient_ proposed widening access in stages, according to what each release makes possible and how effectively the resulting dangers can be addressed.

Later that month, [_Return to the Release Gradient_](https://modalpathethics.com/return-to-the-release-gradient/) gave a university the weights and discovered that the university still needed a computer capable of running the things.

This is where the **capability commons** entered the argument.

There must be institutions outside the frontier laboratories capable of actually working with advanced artificial intelligence. They need equipment, records, technical labor, operating budgets and sufficient access to produce findings that the developers themselves may find inconvenient.

-   Giving someone an account with a frontier provider could substantially improve their life.
-   Giving someone an independently maintainable intelligent instrument opens a whole different set of possibilities.

Both are valuable. The first arrangement, however, still permits a company to change the technology around which someone has organized their work. The company may be able to change its price, restrict its uses or discontinue it entirely.

A private laboratory continuing to develop **intelligence beyond the independent scientific and technical capacities of the rest of society** would occupy a pretty exceptional position.

Its decision to withhold a model may have prevented something terrible.

But at some point, other institutions may begin requiring the laboratory's cooperation to establish whether its decisions are justified.

And there is a problem with my own proposed solution.

Independent equipment costs money. Maintaining independent expertise costs money. Preserving an alternative way of performing a function becomes particularly expensive when somebody else is performing it considerably better.

> Why would an institution continue paying for all of this shit?

## So The Modal Path Ethical Alignment Observatory is Back.

In September's [_Field Instruments: Alignment_](https://modalpathethics.com/field-instruments-alignment/), I founded the **Modal Path Ethical Alignment Observatory** and cancelled its first proposed project immediately.

Humanity was not getting an official Alignment Score from me.

The true mandate was to determine whether intelligence was becoming better aligned with extant reality. This included human intelligence, which has furnished the Observatory with an extensive collection of examples of strategic competence exceeding moral competence.

The distinction becomes particularly interesting when the intelligence is improving the very institutions responsible for governing it.

In [_Four Futures_](https://modalpathethics.com/four-futures/), I distinguished destruction by superior intelligence from several different ways civilization might survive while losing the ability to determine or correct the conditions of its survival.

A safety program could successfully prevent the first disaster while contributing to another.

This is why _The Inner Apocalypse_ proposed institutions capable of exercising binding authority while remaining materially subject to correction and replacement.

The difficulty with experimentally investigating such an arrangement is that I do not have a spare hyperintelligence or even civilization to run against it.

I **do** have computers, considerable help from OpenAI's own models, and the ability to construct much smaller fields in which particular relationships can be examined exactly.

The initial Observatory investigations separated several problems that are easily confused:

-   Finding the correct answer
    -   _and_
        -   verifying an answer somebody else found.
-   Ordering the removal of a dangerous system
    -   _and_
        -   maintaining the service that depends upon it.
-   Reconstructing an institution after a failure
    -   _and_
        -   restoring its capacity to survive another failure.
-   Having independent ownership of a model
    -   _and_
        -   having the infrastructure required to use it.

These were useful preparations for game night. But they left something important outside the experiment.

> The political and economic arrangements that determine whether anybody will actually build those capacities.

So I gave the Observatory a government.

## _The Frontier Game._

There are three players: two competing artificial intelligence developers and a public authority.

The developers can continue offering their existing systems, train more capable models, develop new ways of extracting capabilities from their existing models, or pursue further research privately without offering a new product to the public.

They can also choose what kind of relationship their customers will have with their technology. They can rent access to the system or offer a contract that supplies a transferable copy.

These choices have different costs and potential returns. Each developer makes its decision in light of what the other developer is likely to do.

The government has a limited budget, which it can spend on independent examiners, equipment for investigating and replacing artificial intelligence systems, enforcement capacity, and resources for maintaining essential services during an interruption.

It must also decide which company's technology to purchase.

Once the service is operating, a technical failure may occur. The provider can disclose the failure or conceal it. Independent examiners may discover it anyway. The government may consequently order the system stopped, and the provider can cooperate or refuse.

If the government manages to remove the provider, it must find and activate a working replacement while carrying the existing service.

Then another emergency arrives.

The game does not give the government its money back, reconstruct its equipment or restore its depleted reserves between emergencies.

I examined several possible implementations of the proposals currently being discussed by the laboratories and legislators.

-   [Amodei's coordinated pacing proposal](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier?ref=modalpathethics.com) becomes an agreement restricting advancement until the corresponding safeguards have been established.
    -   Violating the agreement can carry financial or contractual consequences.
-   [Pachocki's proposed development limits](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/?ref=modalpathethics.com) become a ceiling beyond which further training cannot proceed without satisfying agreed safety requirements.
    -   The game also permits changes to the scope of that ceiling.
-   [Sanders and Casar's proposed legislation](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119s5493is/html/BILLS-119s5493is.htm?ref=modalpathethics.com) becomes an enforceable statutory pause and prohibition on specified activities and capabilities.

These are simplified implementations of particular mechanisms within the proposals. The **real** proposals contain other provisions, and several of their mechanisms could operate together.

The developers are physically capable of violating the rules.

Whether doing so is advantageous depends on the other player's decisions, the likelihood of being examined, the consequences of discovery and the government's actual ability to enforce its decisions.

The government cannot inspect everything for free.

### Why Game Theory?

Back in June, [_The Great Ludic Audit_](https://modalpathethics.com/the-great-ludic-audit/) opened with Modal Path Ethics putting game theory on trial for preserving the formal structure of games while discarding much of what made them playerly. Twelve reviews later, however, game theory was acquitted, so now I'm going for Game of the Year.

The real answer is that these proposals are difficult to evaluate in isolation because nobody involved will be standing still while the proposal is implemented.

A restriction changes the incentives of the laboratories. Their response changes the technology available to the government. The government's dependence on that technology changes the cost of enforcing the restriction. And the government has incentives of its own.

If I simply give the government a **functioning capability commons,** I have supplied the very thing whose existence this investigation needs to explain.

So the Observatory treats the problem as a strategic game.

-   **Players:** Two competing developers and a public authority.
-   **Strategies:** Different forms of technological advancement, commercial arrangements, public investment, disclosure, concealment and cooperation with intervention.
-   **Information:** The developers know about their own operating failures. The authority must obtain evidence through disclosure or examination.
-   **Payoffs:** The developers pursue profit. The authority evaluates production, safety, service continuity and other public consequences according to a specified set of priorities.
-   **Chance:** Technical failures, audit opportunities and the suitability of potential replacements introduce uncertainty.
-   **State:** Equipment, records, operating capacity, money and reserves persist between emergencies. What happens in the first emergency changes what can happen in the second.

The computer evaluates all 49 combinations of the developers' initial strategies. It works backward through the subsequent decisions to determine which choices serve each developer's interests, then examines which public investments produce the outcomes the authority prefers.

Both orders of developer decision-making are tested. The game also searches for pure Nash equilibria: arrangements in which neither developer would benefit from independently changing its initial strategy.

A further experiment removes the assumption that the developers pursue profit rationally and asks whether the institutional arrangement can survive every permitted choice and disturbance.

These are two very different standards. An arrangement can be stable because nobody presently benefits from disrupting it. That does not establish that it could survive somebody deciding to disrupt it.

The numbers are experimental parameters rather than estimates of actual corporate profits, public expenditure or the probability of AI disasters. The game also assumes that particular methods of examination, enforcement and technical repair are available.

The purpose of this is to discover how those conditions interact, where apparently reasonable decisions produce undesirable outcomes, and whether the institutions proposed by Modal Path Ethics can survive the same examination as everybody else's.

## The Government Would Prefer Not to Know.

> Give the government five units of purchasing power.

It has to buy independent examination, technical equipment, enforcement and emergency service capacity out of that budget.

In one version of the game, the government evaluates its choices according to a combination of productive output, harmful operation, service interruptions, regulatory breaches and other consequences.

The game examines the available allocations and the strategic decisions those allocations induce.

The government selects **zero independent examiners.** It buys enforcement capacity, several packages of temporary service resources and retains some money for later use.

Both companies pursue advancement in violation of the modeled pacing agreement. One supplies the public service through rented access while the other continues its research privately.

Under the specified assumptions, the arrangement produces eight units of expected output and no expected interruption of the protected service.

It also produces one expected period of harmful operation.

The government had enough money to employ independent examiners. It selected another allocation because of the consequences that would follow from discovering a problem.

-   An examination could establish that a productive system needs to be stopped.
-   Stopping the system would require somebody to maintain the service it was performing.

The government has insufficient resources to comfortably carry out both obligations. So the examination becomes economically unattractive.

> Change the government's objective.

Tell it to prioritize preventing harmful operation.

It now buys two independent audit teams.

Harmful operation falls to zero in this particular calculation. Expected productive output falls from eight to three.

Expected interruptions to the essential service increase from zero to 5.5 units.

The government has become considerably better at discovering the circumstances in which it needs a replacement. It still cannot afford the replacement machinery.

On November 16, [OpenAI announced its first phase of embedded assessments](https://openai.com/index/our-first-phase-of-embedded-assessments/?ref=modalpathethics.com), involving Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire. These arrangements give outside researchers deeper contact with selected unreleased models, records, evaluation tools and researchers.

The findings of those examiners may eventually require extraordinarily costly decisions.

How much more difficult does it become for a government to sustain independent examination once its hospitals, public administration and national economy have begun depending upon the systems being examined?

The Observatory has encountered a new incentive for allowing an institution to remain ignorant.

## The Decimal Has Not Moved.

> Return to the same five-unit budget in the Frontier Game.

This time, give the government two audit teams, limited independent technical capacity, an enforceable intervention mechanism and one package of reserve service capacity.

Allow it to favor companies offering transferable technology.

Under the modeled coordinated pacing agreement, one developer chooses to advance through training and offer a transferable contract. The other continues renting access to its existing technology.

Now introduce a development ceiling covering further training, leaving advancement through tools and other deployment machinery outside that particular restriction.

Both developers change their strategies.

They pursue advancement through the organization of their existing models.

The recorded development violations fall from one to zero.

Expected production, harmful operation and service interruption remain exactly the same.

The Observatory has improved the compliance report without improving any of those material outcomes.

This is the difficulty identified the other day in [_The Year of 0.7_](https://modalpathethics.com/the-year-of-0-7/).

An intelligence might acquire substantial new capacities through improved inference, tools, memory, coordination and access to infrastructure. Its pretraining loss could remain completely unchanged.

Pachocki's actual proposal recognizes the importance of additional indicators and safety examinations. The narrow ceiling in the game isolates what happens if one important route of advancement is regulated while another escapes the corresponding restriction.

The important point is that a development restriction becomes part of the environment in which technological development occurs.

The laboratories will respond to it. Some activities will become less attractive. Others may become considerably more attractive.

The subsequent distribution of intelligence depends partly on those responses.

Congress is regulating an extremely mobile object.

## Sanders Has Confiscated the Examiner's Computer.

Section 9 of the proposed _Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act_ would prohibit systems exhibiting one or more of the bill's specified precursor characteristics.

> Suppose an intelligent instrument capable of greatly accelerating artificial intelligence research falls within that definition.

> Suppose an independent public institution needs that instrument to investigate and replace the technology on which an essential service depends.

The Frontier Game permits us to examine this complication without changing the government's budget or inventing a different set of incentives for the companies.

-   In one version, the public institution can independently run a transferred model, but the advanced capabilities of that model make its use unlawful under the stipulated classification.
-   In another version, its use is permitted under a hypothetical exception.

The two developers select exactly the same strategies in both cases.

What changes is the government's ability to respond to an emergency.

-   With the instrument prohibited, the government can only perform some of the tests necessary to identify a working replacement.
-   With the instrument available, the government can examine every candidate.

The expected completion of replacements nearly doubles. Expected interruptions to the public service decrease substantially. Independent operation becomes considerably more reachable by the end of the second emergency.

There is also a small increase in expected harmful operation.

The reason is worth spending a moment on.

When the public institution fails to restore the service, the service remains unavailable. It consequently cannot experience another operating failure of the kind being measured.

Restoring the service makes its benefits available again. It also makes another operating failure possible.

A safety comparison examining **harmful operation** alone could therefore favor the arrangement that left the service unavailable.

Consider what that would mean in a hospital.

A treatment system is withdrawn after a dangerous failure. One hospital manages to replace it and resumes treatment. Another never manages to replace it.

-   The first hospital has another opportunity to make a treatment error.
-   The second has an interesting way of improving its treatment error statistics.

Neither statistic settles the condition of the patients.

This is why the Observatory cancelled the official Alignment Score. The resulting continuations have to remain visible in their own respects.

The game establishes no legal finding that any particular existing research instrument would qualify as a prohibited precursor under Sanders and Casar's bill.

It does identify something the legislation needs to confront. A restriction on dangerous research capabilities must preserve sufficient lawful technical capacity to investigate the restricted systems, evaluate subsequent developments and perform warranted correction.

Giving the public half the company does not answer that question.

## The Capability Commons Loses.

There is a danger in presenting the **capability commons** as the obvious solution to all these problems.

I have now spent considerable time arguing that independent institutions need their own intelligent instruments, equipment, evidence and means of enforcement.

**Fine.**

Let's give them those things and see if the arrangement survives.

Across the primary Frontier Game investigation, the Observatory examined 1,580 policy, resource and incentive configurations. For each configuration, both developers' seven possible strategies were played against one another.

Two different implementations of the calculation agreed on all 77,420 resulting strategy profiles.

None of the selected sequential outcomes established a guarantee that the essential service would survive every admitted history without harmful operation, an unexecuted warranted stop or a failed replacement.

-   The government could buy equipment and neglect independent examination.
-   It could discover a problem and lack enforcement capacity.
-   It could enforce a decision and lack the means to maintain the affected service.
-   It could successfully replace the provider and arrive at the next emergency with insufficient resources to do it again.

This is a considerably less convenient result for _The Inner Apocalypse_.

The book requires critical institutions to maintain actual paths through their own replacement. The experiment demonstrates why that requirement cannot be discharged by supplying one particularly impressive alternative computer.

The necessary resources have to be available together, and they have to survive their use.

I therefore expanded the government's budget and stopped requiring the developers to behave rationally.

Every permitted development strategy, every permitted disclosure or concealment decision, every refusal and every admitted disturbance would now have to be survivable.

The expanded government possesses enough independent examination capacity to inspect both laboratories and the public operator. It has a complete independent technical instrument, enforceable intervention powers and a larger temporary service reserve.

With sufficient time and resources, the arrangement succeeds across all 49 initial combinations of developer strategies in this finite two-emergency game.

But the attempts to reduce that reserve produced a nice little disaster.

## The Government Has Enough Money.

An essential service requires two units of reserve capacity per turn while its replacement is prepared.

The government begins with eighteen units of reserve and sufficient money to purchase another three.

The first emergency arrives.

Its independent technical instrument crashes. The government has to reload it, validate it, test the possible replacements and activate the correct one.

-   Five turns.
-   Ten units of reserve consumed.

The service survives. The government now operates it independently, with eight units of reserve remaining.

The procurement procedure has been designed to purchase additional reserve whenever the available stock falls below six units.

> **Eight** is more than **six.**

The government therefore purchases nothing.

The second emergency arrives. The independently operated service develops a problem of its own, and the examiner's instrument crashes again.

The government reloads it, validates it, tests the possible replacements, and identifies the correct one.

By the end of the fourth turn, the reserve is empty.

The government still needs one more turn to bring the replacement online.

-   The replacement succeeds.
-   Two units of essential service are lost.

The government had enough money to prevent the interruption. Its procurement procedure did not recognize the need to spend it.

This is almost annoyingly faithful to the arguments of _The Inner Apocalypse_.

An institution can possess the right resources in aggregate while the rules governing their availability prevent those resources from becoming useful at the required moment.

It is also why the **capability commons** has to include the institutions governing its own maintenance.

In the expanded test, seven reserve packages supply enough stock to survive both emergencies under the declared conditions. Reduce the repair deadline, compromise the independence of examination or remove the government's enforcement capacity, and the guarantee can disappear again. There is no single purchase that completes the arrangement.

And this experiment only contains two emergencies.

## What the Observatory Has Found.

A substantial part of the current artificial intelligence debate concerns who will possess authority over the technology.

The Observatory's investigation has uncovered several ways that authority can become ineffective even while its legal provisions remain intact.

-   There is the government that cannot afford to discover a danger because it cannot afford to remedy it.
-   There is the development restriction that redirects technological advancement into another activity.
-   There is the safety measure that deprives an outside examiner of the intelligence required to perform its work.
-   There is the independent public institution that successfully replaces a dangerous system and consumes the resources necessary to survive another emergency.

These are different failures requiring different interventions.

The underlying mathematics belongs to established work in game theory, resource allocation, computer science and [AI Control](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/greenblatt24a.html?ref=modalpathethics.com). The Observatory is examining how those problems interact with the particular constitutional requirements developed in Modal Path Ethics.

The research also included an experimental campaign involving actual language models, which tragically failed its qualification requirements after 4,896 recorded decisions. It produced no positive empirical finding about learned ethical alignment.

_The Frontier Game_ is a constructed strategic model. It assumes particular means of discovering hazards, executing decisions, testing alternatives and restoring equipment. It does not model every way a superior intelligence might undermine those means, nor does its successful expanded arrangement establish the safety of private research outside the represented public service.

Its useful result is considerably more limited.

It demonstrates arrangements in which meaningful independent correction is possible despite conflicting economic interests, and it exposes several ways those arrangements can fail.

That is enough to ask some better questions.

## The Ruling.

Amodei is right to distinguish delegating a particular function from surrendering political authority.

A human being does not need to spend forty years maintaining an administrative system in order to retain political standing within the civilization that system serves.

Meta is also right to recognize that widely available intelligent assistance could dramatically improve the position of individuals confronting powerful institutions.

And the legislators are right to insist that some dangers require enforceable restrictions rather than voluntary corporate promises.

The question is how these protections will interact once they become institutions with their own responsibilities, financial interests and technical dependencies.

Every one requires some way of surviving an adverse decision.

As I argued in [_The Humans Still Get to Vote_](https://modalpathethics.com/the-humans-still-get-to-vote/), meaningful human political authority can survive the disappearance of the independent instruments required to discover when that authority is wrong.

Artificial intelligence could help preserve those instruments. This is one of the most important opportunities available to the technology.

But the capacity for independent correction must be developed alongside the capacity being governed.

If a laboratory retains a particularly dangerous model, there need to be independently constituted means of examining the grounds of that retention and of addressing the dependencies it creates.

If developers agree to pause advancement until particular safety requirements have been met, somebody outside the developers needs the technical capacity to investigate whether those requirements have actually been satisfied.

If Congress establishes a new department capable of stopping artificial intelligence systems, the department's budget needs to account for the continued operation of the civilization that may eventually depend on those systems. This includes the regulator's computer, its replacement, and the reserve that allows the hospital to continue treating patients while the regulator discovers that it was wrong.

The cost of developing these capacities belongs inside the cost of developing advanced artificial intelligence. Leaving them until afterward could produce a situation in which every proposed safeguard requires the continued permission of the institution it was designed to constrain.

A laboratory may have excellent reasons to retain custody of a particular model.

The capacity to investigate, govern and eventually replace that model needs to grow outside the laboratory too.
