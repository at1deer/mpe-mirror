Unfortunately, the academic record is haunted.

404 Media recently reported on a new preprint from Michał Brzozowski and Neo Christopher Chung concerning what the researchers call ghosts: recurring names produced by large language models when asked to invent experts, researchers, collaborators, and fictional people.

Elena Vasquez.
Marcus Chen.
Amara Okafor.
Aris Thorne.
Lena Petrova.
Elara Voss.

These people have been extremely busy.

Elena Vasquez and Marcus Chen have appeared as scientists, clinicians, writers, academics, fictional characters, commercial experts, and co-authors across independently generated material on the open web. The researchers found model-specific ensembles: Claude strongly favored combinations involving Elena Vasquez and Marcus Chen; Gemini produced Aris Thorne and Lena Petrova; GPT showed a strong Elara Voss prior without the same stable partner structure.

This is already very funny.

Then the ghosts entered academia.

There Are Papers.

The researchers searched scholarly infrastructure for the recurring names.

They found ghost-associated records on ResearchGate and Semantic Scholar. They found synthetic research groups containing names associated with different model families. One ResearchGate profile listed thirty-five papers across fields ranging from Kubernetes security and oil-refinery decarbonization to agricultural extension and radiographic imaging, with rotating ghost-associated co-authors attached to the same persistent last-author identity.

So it seems academia has created a multidisciplinary institute staffed entirely by recurring autocomplete characters.

This is bad news.

Then they checked Zenodo.

Oh no.

Zenodo is an open research repository operated by CERN. Researchers use it for papers, datasets, software, presentations, preprints, and other research objects.

The researchers identified 1,655 ghost-authored Zenodo records carrying real 10.5281/zenodo.* DataCite DOIs.

The records claimed publication in journals that the researchers could not verify as real publication venues. Claimed publication dates ran from 2020 through 2023. Server-side DataCite registration timestamps placed 99 percent of the DOI registrations in March and April of 2026.

Elena Vasquez alone appeared on seventy-seven papers.

These were not strings that looked vaguely like DOIs.

They were real DOIs.

They resolved. They existed in DataCite. They could be harvested.

The academic ghost had acquired a permanent address.

  • The DOI is real.
    • The author is not.

Slime Watch Will Not Make Slime.

Before anybody reports that the entire scientific citation graph has been possessed by Elena Vasquez, stop.

The preprint does not establish that.

As of May 2026, the researchers reported that none of those 1,655 Zenodo records had been indexed by Semantic Scholar. The DOIs were active and harvestable, so the route into downstream scholarly infrastructure existed. The researchers specifically distinguished the available contamination infrastructure from completed propagation: the ghost papers cited real work, while real papers' cited-by lists had not yet begun returning those 1,655 records.

This distinction is important.

So the ruling is provisional.

The downstream contamination path is open.

The DOI Worked.

Zenodo itself is unusually helpful here.

Its documentation says that any user can create a record. A record contains metadata, files, and a persistent identifier. When a record is published, Zenodo normally registers a DOI with DataCite.

Zenodo describes that DOI as a globally unique persistent identifier that gives the record a permanent link, makes it citable, assists discovery, and allows research outputs to be interconnected.

That is exactly what happened.

The DOI worked.

This is the part of the story I do not want lost beneath a generic demand that repositories become more restrictive.

The DOI was asked:

Can you give this record a persistent identity and an address?

The DOI answered:

Yes.

Nobody asked the DOI:

Did Elena Vasquez personally perform this research?

Nobody asked:

Does this journal exist?

Nobody asked:

Did peer review happen?

Nobody asked:

Is this publication date honest?

Nobody asked:

Does this institution employ this author?

Those are different relations.

Zenodo's own terms make the boundary explicit. Uploaders remain responsible for what they upload, and CERN says that hosting material on Zenodo does not constitute approval or endorsement of the content.

The scholarly culture surrounding the identifier can still ask the identifier to carry more than it contains.

A DOI can establish that a record exists at a persistent address.

It cannot establish that the scholarship did.

A DOI is an address, not a witness.

That would be an excellent place to end this article.

We Have Now Built a Terrible Detector.

This is where the joke has to surrender the instrument.

Brzozowski and Chung are careful about the ghost names.

They explicitly state that real people named Elena Vasquez and Marcus Chen do in fact exist. Their claim concerns the combination of name, claimed expertise, institution, publication history, and the inability to trace the represented researcher behind the specific records they investigated.

They also acknowledge that some names classified as ghosts could correspond to real researchers whose names were absorbed into model priors.

That distinction matters more as these patterns feed back into the web.

Brzozowski told 404 Media that the model-family signal may become less reliable as generated material containing the recurring names enters the broader information environment and, potentially, later training corpora.

The detector can become contaminated by the thing it detects.

The ghost detector can become haunted.

And this is precisely where a real contamination response can become another slime machine.

Build a blacklist of suspicious names and eventually some real Elena Vasquez discovers that her career has been placed under additional friction because Claude once liked the rhythm of her name.

Build a classifier around stylistic regularities and writers learn to perform around it.

Build a single authenticity score and institutions start optimizing for the score.

Build a centralized author certificate and every independent scholar now needs permission from the certificate issuer to count as real.

So How Do I Prove I Exist?

Apparently, I don't.

At least not through one object.

There is no golden document I can place on the Author page that transforms the metaphysical proposition Aidan Lawson exists into a certified field.

  • A passport would establish that a government issued a passport to an identity under that name.
  • A university record would establish a relation with a university.
  • ORCID would establish a persistent researcher identifier.
  • A DOI would establish a persistent research record.
  • Nature establishes that Nature published work attributed to Aidan Lawson.
  • LinkedIn adds another externally hosted identity and social graph.
  • A photograph provides visual evidence.
  • A video provides temporal and embodied evidence.
  • A relative supplies another relation.
  • An old graduation list adds a timestamp long before the current inquiry.
    • None of these individually contains the whole person.
    • Together they form a path.

The path becomes increasingly difficult to explain without the person.

That is the actual evidentiary difference between the human author and the synthetic ghost.

The name points. The relations answer.

The Paper Was More Than the PDF.

This is where Ghostbusters rejoins Slime Watch.

A scholarly paper was never just the text.

It enters through a path.

  • Someone performed or assembled the work.
  • Methods constrain what can be claimed.
  • Evidence came from somewhere.
  • Data may remain inspectable.
  • An institution may stand behind an affiliation.
  • A venue has a history and scope.
  • A publication date belongs to an actual transition.
  • Peer review either occurred or did not.
  • A repository preserved a version.
  • Later work cited, criticized, replicated, contradicted, retracted, or ignored it.
  • An author could be contacted.
  • A correction could return through the path.

The ghost record reproduces the shape of those relations. It can therefore look extraordinarily scholarly while the causal history underneath those labels has disappeared.

The object has acquired provenance-shaped metadata without provenance.

This is the deeper contamination problem.

A fake DOI was easy to reject.

The researchers found fake journal sites issuing DOI-looking strings that did not resolve through a recognized DOI authority. Then the pipeline moved onto Zenodo and acquired real DataCite DOIs.

The preprint describes the transition almost perfectly: somebody found the gap between the fake journal website and the credible academic record and filled it with Zenodo.

That is what Slime Watch has to follow.

Always expand the inquiry.

The error did not stop when the DOI became real.

The real DOI became the next instrument in the false path.

Do Not Arrest Zenodo.

Zenodo remains useful.

I am using it.

Open repositories let independent researchers, students, laboratories, programmers, scholars outside wealthy institutions, and people working between formal publication systems preserve work and make it citable.

That is good.

The repository should not have to become an omniscient Ministry of Scholar Existence before someone can preserve a preprint.

The repair belongs in the distinctions.

The researchers themselves found one extremely useful distinction: the legitimate self-archived records in their query carried publisher DOIs and related identifiers, while the 1,655 ghost records lacked those publisher cross-references.

That is the kind of information a good instrument preserves.

No purity oracle required.
