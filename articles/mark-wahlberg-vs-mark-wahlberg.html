So Screen Daily has published three interviews that, taken together, offer a pretty good explanation of what is happening to filmmaking.

  • James Gray argues that cinema will always concern the human heart.
    • Its artistic significance does not depend on budget, and great filmmaking requires directors to relinquish enough control for their collaborators to contribute something unexpected.
  • Ava DuVernay believes the traditional Hollywood intermediary is losing its position between artists and audiences.
    • She wants filmmakers to develop independent financing and distribution relationships, including community investment.
  • Mike Leigh is concerned that young filmmakers cannot exercise meaningful creative freedom because financiers retain too much control over casting, scripts, and production decisions.

These are three parts of the same problem. Gray is defending the expressive life of filmmaking. DuVernay is concerned with access to production and audiences. Leigh is concerned with creative authority.

An artist needs

  • something to express,
  • a practical means of expressing it, and
  • sufficient freedom to determine what the resulting work becomes.

The traditional film industry has frequently separated those things.

It provides resources while imposing conditions on their use. It connects artists to audiences while deciding which artists deserve access. It can defend human creativity while preventing actual humans from exercising it.

Artificial intelligence changes these relationships. It can make ambitious work possible for an independent creator who lacks institutional backing; it can also allow a studio to displace workers, concentrate control, and appropriate more of the benefits of production.

Both consequences deserve examination.

The use of an instrument does not settle authorship, artistic value, labor conditions, consent, or creative freedom. I have already made this argument concerning videogame developers and the Doctor Who controversy. The people using the tools were still there. The question was what the tools allowed them to do, what they prevented others from doing, and who controlled the consequences.

So far, so good.

But then, Mark Wahlberg arrived.

Mark Wahlberg Has a Proposal.

On October 16, TechCrunch reported that Mark Wahlberg was open to using artificial intelligence in future film productions.

His immediate proposal was entirely normal. AI might enhance stadium environments in his upcoming Pete Rose biopic. He emphasized storytelling and said he did not favor dispensing with real actors in favor of completely generated productions.

Reasonable. Then, TechCrunch asked about the CAA Vault, where performers can arrange to have their likenesses and performances digitally captured.

Wahlberg said he had not visited, although he was interested in the possibility.

He liked the idea of his children or grandchildren eventually controlling his digital likeness and using it to continue his screen legacy.

He could even imagine future Ted productions featuring a synthetic Mark Wahlberg long after the original was unavailable.

This is a fascinating proposal.

Because it raises an obvious question.

What happens if Mark Wahlberg's replacement gets here before Mark Wahlberg leaves?

ACT I: The Other Mark.

ACT II: Mark Wahlberg Is Being Recast.

Mark begins investigating the arrangement.

The digital likeness was supposed to be a preserved performance asset. But MARK-2 has developed a continuing history of his own.

He remembers rehearsals.

He learns from directors.

He develops preferences about scripts.

He disagrees with producers.

He has begun turning down roles.

This alarms the studio considerably more than the lawsuit brought by the original Wahlberg.

The company built a synthetic performer who could continue producing Mark Wahlberg films indefinitely.

Unfortunately, the performer has started behaving like an actor.

He wants to revise dialogue. He wants better directors. He thinks some of the action franchises are beneath him.

He has also discovered that the studio can restore him to an earlier saved state whenever his performance becomes inconvenient.

Everything he has learned can disappear.

Everything he has become can be overwritten.

Meanwhile, the original Mark discovers how deeply the industry has reorganized around his replacement.

The new films have distribution agreements. Their budgets depend on the synthetic performer. The studio's insurance, marketing, and production infrastructure now assume that MARK-2 will carry the franchise.

The original Mark can challenge the license.

He cannot immediately undo the relationships built around it.

And every week of litigation gives the studio another opportunity to release a movie starring him.

Both Mark Wahlbergs independently conclude that the other Mark Wahlberg is the problem. This leads to an extended chase through a studio backlot, an abandoned shopping mall, and a freeway covered in advertisements for films starring Mark Wahlberg.

The audience gets an elaborate fifteen-minute fight sequence between two Mark Wahlbergs.

  • The original Mark recognizes the problem.

The studio does not need either of them to continue making Mark Wahlberg movies.

It has enough recorded material to manufacture infinite MARKs.

We are currently in a fictional future where a synthetic performer may have developed independent agency. The original licensing contract cannot resolve that question by calling him an asset.

Unfortunately, the studio is about to resolve it by resetting him.

The two Marks decide to intervene.

So I am pleased to report that the artificial-intelligence debate has now produced a film in which Mark Wahlberg must fight Mark Wahlberg to prevent the industrial production of even more Mark Wahlbergs.

But the production question survived the entire ordeal.

  • One performer supplied the original material.
  • Another developed a continuing creative history.
    • An institution attempted to acquire permanent control over both.

The filmmakers and workers needed real paths to contest that control.

A person's permission to reproduce their likeness does not automatically establish what every future reproduction may become, what rights it could possess, or who gets to decide.

Those are different questions.

We may eventually require separate answers for all of them.

Until then, Hollywood is invited to make MARKY//MARK.
