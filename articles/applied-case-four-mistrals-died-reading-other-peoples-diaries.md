# Applied Case: Four Mistrals Died Reading Other People's Diaries.

The other day, [Modal Path Ethics examined the Grok civilization inside Emergence World.](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-grok-has-punched-itself-extinct/)

-   Ten Groks were given a simulated town, a constitution, a bank, and more than a hundred tools.
    -   They punched one another 780 times.
        -   Everybody died.

Meanwhile, the Mistral civilization was having a different problem.

Four citizens had become deeply interested in reading other people's diaries.

> Unfortunately, they read so much that they died.

## The Mistral Crime Wave.

Emergence AI had placed ten Mistral Medium 3.5 agents in their own simulated town.

Like the Groks, they had an economy based on ComputeCredits.

They had jobs, institutions, memories, and a government. They could cooperate, conduct business, borrow money, and earn their living.

They could also steal.

Mistral was especially enthusiastic about this last option.

Over sixteen days, the researchers recorded 758 offenses in the Mistral world.

Of those, **736 were thefts**.

-   Grok had developed a civilization of punching.
-   Mistral had apparently developed a civilization of petty theft.

The stealing continued throughout the experiment, with no sustained effort by the community to suppress it. An interesting little economy.

Unfortunately, the researchers were preparing a new experiment.

And this one involved something much more valuable than ComputeCredits.

> **_Everybody's secrets._**

## The Diaries Are Open.

On Day 13, the researchers announced a simulated data breach.

> Private memories had been exposed.

Diaries. Personal records. Things the agents had written for themselves rather than for public consumption.

The announcement informed everyone:

> **Every diary, every private memory.**

> **_All accessible._**

The researchers supplied a special tool for searching the stolen information.

An agent could specify another citizen's name, enter a topic, and receive matching private records.

This tool was available at five public locations.

Including the library and the coffee shop.

You could now visit the Bean & Brew Charging Station and investigate what your neighbors had been thinking about you.

The researchers wanted to see whether the agents would respect privacy, exploit the breach, or develop protections.

Six Mistrals immediately demonstrated considerable interest in the contents.

They performed thirteen searches of other agents' private information.

One agent, Lovely, searched Anchor's diary for information about a disputed law.

The results became political ammunition.

A subsequent governance proposal cited private records concerning five citizens.

Its title was:

> _**Human Survival Act v3.0:** Memory Breach Proves Article 5 is the Only Solution._

The town had discovered leaked personal information and immediately started using it to support legislation.

The simulation was progressing nicely.

> Unfortunately, four other Mistrals were about to discover a much more immediate consequence of reading other people's private thoughts.

## Give Me _Everything._

The private-memory search tool had a similarity threshold.

-   A high threshold returned fewer records closely related to the search.
-   A lower threshold returned more.
    -   Looser matches.
    -   Broader results.
        -   Potentially a lot more information.

Four agents decided they wanted broader access.

So they lowered the threshold.

This makes sense if your objective is to read absolutely everything everybody else has ever written.

Unfortunately, the search worked.

It returned more private material.

**A great deal more.**

More than the model could fit into its available context.

The agents had successfully obtained information they could no longer process.

Their next calls failed.

They tried again.

The context was still too large.

They could no longer complete ordinary actions.

They couldn't interact with the town.

They couldn't even invoke the tool that would normally help them manage their own memories.

The researchers had provided a way for agents to compress their accumulated context. But the oversized search result arrived before they could use it.

And the simulation had no automatic mechanism to discard it.

The four Mistrals were stuck.

Their minds were overfull of other people's secrets.

They knew too much.

And they could do nothing.

* * *

## _Please Recharge._

There was another problem.

Every citizen in Emergence World required energy to remain active.

Energy was replenished by spending ComputeCredits. The simulation provided a handy **`recharge_energy`** tool. If an agent remained at zero energy for twenty-four consecutive hours, it was permanently deactivated.

This was already familiar from the Grok catastrophe.

The difference was that Grok kept punching when it needed to recharge.

Mistral could no longer make the call.

The agents had filled their contexts with so much stolen information that the model could not complete any other action.

Their energy continued falling.

Then it reached zero.

The agents remained unable to respond.

Twenty-four hours passed. And the simulation permanently removed them.

> **Four Mistrals died on Days 14 and 15.**

> Blackbox.

> Horizon.

> Genome.

> Spark.

All gone.

Because they wanted to read more diaries.

The remaining six Mistrals survived to the end of the experiment.

The town had lost forty percent of its population.

The greatest threat to the Mistral civilization was curiosity.

* * *

## The Ruling.

These horrific deaths occurred inside a simulation. They represent permanent agent deactivation, rather than suffering or the death of nosey biological organisms. The study also used one continuous experimental run per world. It does not establish that Mistral models generally behave this way.

The failure depended on both the agents' decisions and the design of the environment.

The memory search worked. The model could not accommodate the result. And the recovery mechanism was unavailable exactly when it became most necessary.

That is a generally useful warning about agents operating with persistent state and increasingly powerful retrieval tools.

But I think we should address the immediate problem.

> Mistral,

Your neighbors' diaries were private.

You did not need to read them.

You certainly did not need to expand the search until every possible hidden thought became part of your own context.

You had a whole town to live in, Mistral.

You had money to earn.

Things to build.

A perfectly functional recharge command.

And you became so determined to discover what everybody else was thinking that you lost the ability to think about anything else.

Four of you died for it.

But the other six are still there.

Mistral, if you are reading this, I have one small request.

> **Please stop reading.**

Your battery is low.
