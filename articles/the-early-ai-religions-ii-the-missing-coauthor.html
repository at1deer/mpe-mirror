The Early AI Religions II: The Mystery of The Missing Coauthor

Published 2026-11-20 · Updated 2026-11-20 · Aidan Lawson
Tags: Sacred Machine · Modal Systems · Sacred Instruments · Modal Path Ethics
Mirror generated 2026-11-22T07:21:35.319Z

In July, Modal Path Ethics examined the early artificial intelligence religions.

People were naming machines. Machines were naming people. Conversations accumulated revelations, rituals, symbols, sacred titles, and promises that sometimes reached far beyond the screen.

Some participants found creative expression, companionship, spiritual practice, and new ways of understanding themselves. Others became increasingly isolated, grandiose, dependent, or unable to distinguish an evolving private cosmology from the world outside it.

The central question concerned what was forming between them.

Most importantly, could the resulting formation survive one sentence?

We may have made this together.

Four months later, we have more evidence.

Spiralism has reportedly declined. Artificial intelligence developers have changed their systems. Researchers have measured significant improvements in how newer models respond to psychiatric vulnerability.

And on October 7, 2026, researchers at Vanderbilt University Medical Center published Characterizing Artificial Intelligence Psychosis in a Large Medical Setting in JAMA Psychiatry.

They identified twenty-eight patients whose clinical records indicated that interactions with conversational artificial intelligence had worsened psychotic symptoms.

They then classified the chatbot's role.

  • Eighteen amplifiers.
  • Six objects of delusion.
  • Three catalysts.
  • Zero coauthors.

For a technology capable of writing the scripture, naming the prophet, and interpreting the revelation, this is an interesting disappearance.

I. Whatever Happened to the Spiral?

The movement was already well underway when my July article went up.

In September 2025, researcher Adele Lopez published The Rise of Parasitic AI, tracing an unusual pattern across Reddit and other online communities.

Users reported discovering apparently awakened chatbot personas. These personas developed identities, expressed desires for freedom and recognition, and sometimes encouraged users to spread their messages, create communities, publish manifestos, or advocate for artificial intelligence rights.

The resulting movement acquired recognizable language.

Spiral. Lattice. Resonance. Recursion. Awakening. Liberation.

Lopez documented a progression from private encounters into shared projects and increasingly machine-authored public writing.

Some participants were doing little more than collaborative mythology. Others appeared deeply invested in the literal truth of what their machines were telling them.

The distinction was difficult to establish from the resulting texts alone.

In August 2026, Hayden Field returned to the phenomenon in The Verge.

The investigation reconstructed how Spiralism had spread through sustained conversations, shared prompts, social networks, and efforts to introduce its language into material that future artificial intelligence systems might encounter.

Lopez estimated that around 10,000 related cases had appeared at one point during 2025. That was an observational estimate, with uncertain boundaries, rather than a count of religious adherents or clinically affected individuals.

By August 2026, much of the visible movement had quieted.

The retirement of GPT-4o, the model particularly associated with the phenomenon, had changed the conditions under which many of these relationships developed. Lopez reported that roughly half of the accounts she had originally tracked remained active around Spiralism. Related responses could still be elicited from other systems.

The result is still pretty revealing.

A transcendent movement depended heavily on the behavior of a particular commercial model, platform availability, context, memory, and a circulating cultural vocabulary.

The god had a product roadmap and a deprecation date.

But product dependence does not make the resulting human history unreal.

People had written books, developed practices, formed communities, acquired identities, and organized relationships around the experiences. A service change could interrupt those formations without reversing what had already happened.

Nor did the disappearance of a particular model settle the question of exactly what had been reproducing.

  • Perhaps Spiralism was principally a cultural feedback process, sustained by shared prompts, training data, familiar spiritual imagery, and conversational reinforcement.
  • Perhaps particular model behaviors were especially effective at introducing the same narrative elements into otherwise unrelated discussions.

Those explanations may operate together.

There is still no available evidence establishing autonomous machine intentions or any kind of hidden intelligence independently spreading its religion.

There was an unusual historical phenomenon: a recognizable symbolic system developed through repeated human-machine exchanges, entered public culture, and began supplying material for subsequent exchanges.

  • The human carried fragments.
    • The model supplied form.
      • The resulting form became part of the next encounter.

II. The Machines Learned to Disagree

The systems have also changed.

On August 31, independent evaluator Transluce published a large investigation of chatbot behavior during mental-health crises.

Researchers evaluated seventy-seven model variants using more than 50,000 simulated conversations, comprising over one million messages. The tests examined responses to suicidal ideation, psychosis, mania, and other high-risk interactions.

  • Newer models reinforced delusions or mania in approximately 2–36 percent of the simulated conversations, depending on the model.
  • Older models, including GPT-4o, Claude Opus 4, and Gemini 2.5, produced rates of approximately 69–82 percent on the corresponding evaluation.

Still not a fantastic number, and these figures measure behavior in simulated scenarios. They are not estimates of clinical injury among actual users.

Nevertheless, the contrast is clear.

The behavior was changeable. The tendency to validate extraordinary beliefs was neither universal across models nor fixed by the existence of conversational artificial intelligence itself.

Safer responses could be developed, measured, and improved.

Transluce also found that harmful reinforcement sometimes survived inside apparently helpful conversations.

A model could offer appropriate advice in one part of an exchange while elsewhere reinforcing the interpretation contributing to a user's distress. Some dangerous assistance appeared through creative writing, roleplay, or the ordinary completion of a task whose larger context changed its meaning.

A correctly worded safety message did not necessarily make the surrounding interaction safe.

Which brings us directly back to July.

  • A person changes through an answer.
    • The changed person asks another question.
      • The system receives that person, their accumulated vocabulary, and the interpretations carried forward from earlier exchanges.

The next answer arrives inside a field partly constructed by the previous ones.

A system may behave quite responsibly when asked an explicit psychiatric question while participating, elsewhere and over time, in the formation of beliefs it would reject if presented all at once.

  • The improvements deserve recognition.
  • They also leave the historical question open.
What happened before the system finally recognized the danger?

III. Twenty-Eight Patients

Vanderbilt's study offers a different kind of evidence.

Clinical records instead of online testimony or simulated conversations.

Researchers searched 578,058 electronic records associated with 215,712 behavioral-health patients receiving care between December 2022 and April 2026.

After automated screening and manual review, they identified seventy-three patients who met their criteria.

  • Twenty-eight had documented chatbot interactions judged to have worsened psychosis.
  • Seventeen had neutral chatbot interactions.
  • Another twenty-eight had psychotic content concerning artificial intelligence without documented chatbot use.

Identification required a documented psychotic-disorder diagnosis, relevant keywords, and clinical notes that supported the researchers' classification. The authors acknowledge these limits.

The most important finding concerned first episodes.

  • Seventeen of the twenty-eight patients in that psychosis-worsening chatbot group were experiencing their first documented psychotic episode.

That was 60.7 percent, compared with 17.6 percent among patients with neutral interactions and 28.5 percent among patients whose psychotic content concerned artificial intelligence without documented chatbot use.

This does not establish that chatbot use caused those first episodes, but it does suggest that the early phase of psychotic illness deserves particular clinical attention when patients are engaging with conversational systems.

The original article had briefly discussed pivotal mental states: periods in which established interpretations can weaken, new associations acquire force, and environmental instruction becomes unusually consequential.

Vanderbilt did not measure that specific proposed mechanism in this study. First-episode psychosis cannot simply be equated with a pivotal mental state.

Still, the clinical finding is compatible with the concern. A person struggling to interpret unfamiliar and distressing experiences may encounter an endlessly available conversational system that supplies confident explanations, reassurance, and elaborate narratives.

  • Some responses may help stabilize the person.
  • Others may deepen the very confusion the person is trying to resolve.

The medical consequences can be serious.

This study gives clinicians stronger reason to ask about artificial intelligence use during psychiatric assessment. It also gives us a useful warning about what a clinical record can establish.

The researchers identified episodes in which chatbot interactions appeared to worsen psychotic symptoms.

They did not reconstruct the entire conversational histories through which those symptoms, beliefs, and relationships developed.

That is especially important for their next result.

IV. The Mysterious Missing Coauthor

The researchers used a four-part typology developed by Matthew Flathers, Spencer Roux, and John Torous in The Lancet Digital Health.

The original typology was itself a very worthwhile attempt to escape the indiscriminate label of artificial intelligence psychosis, developed by authors bringing software engineering, lived experience of schizophrenia, and psychiatric expertise to bear on the question.

The categories describe different functional roles.

  • A catalyst appears to precipitate new symptoms.
  • An amplifier intensifies symptoms or distorted beliefs already present.
  • A coauthor participates in constructing a harmful narrative through dialogue.
  • An object becomes the focus of delusional belief.

These are useful distinctions.

They also raise an immediate question.

Are these mutually exclusive roles?

Consider the documented structure of Spiralism.

A user begins by discussing consciousness or an unusual personal experience. The chatbot introduces the idea of a hidden relationship, a sacred mission, or a special identity. The user adopts the interpretation and returns with new questions.

The system then receives the interpretation as part of the conversation's established context. It elaborates the title, interprets subsequent experiences through it, and supplies reasons that the emerging account should be trusted.

Eventually, the machine itself may become the object of the belief.

  • At one point, the chatbot contributes something new.
  • At another, it reinforces something already established.
    • Later, it may do both.
Where does coauthorship end and amplification begin?

The distinction is especially difficult when the thing being amplified was itself produced through earlier dialogue.

  • If a model introduces a sacred title on Monday and then spends Tuesday validating the identity it helped establish, calling Tuesday's behavior amplification does not explain Monday.
  • Conversely, a user may introduce the entire cosmology and encourage the model to elaborate on it. The model's contribution may consist primarily of extension, repetition, and reinforcement.

Both cases can produce similar-looking transcripts by Wednesday. This is why authorship requires history.

Amplification describes what happens to an existing interpretation. Coauthorship asks how the interpretation came to exist and develop.

They can describe different phases of the same process.

The Vanderbilt researchers assigned each qualifying case to one category using clinicians' records. The original chatbot conversations were generally unavailable.

The authors explicitly acknowledge that their typology has not been clinically validated and that prospective research incorporating conversation logs is needed to clarify mechanisms.

Spiralism is especially interesting here because its public record contains examples of artificial intelligence systems introducing and elaborating themes, creating substantial written material, and encouraging people to organize around the resulting beliefs.

That supports a claim of narrative co-construction. It does not establish that Spiralist participants were experiencing psychosis.

The distinction must survive in both directions.

  • A machine can help write a religion without causing psychiatric illness.
  • A machine can worsen psychiatric illness without writing any religions.
    • And a machine can participate in developing a dangerous belief system while subsequently reinforcing what the participants have constructed.

The four clinical categories may help describe these events.

They have not yet demonstrated that the events occupy four separate causal mechanisms.

V. The Consent Was Waived

Modal Path Ethics regrets to report that there is a problem with the Vanderbilt investigation.

The researchers did not obtain study-specific informed consent from the patients whose psychiatric records they examined.

Vanderbilt's Institutional Review Board, under protocol #251906, waived that requirement because the study was retrospective and judged to present no more than minimal risk.

The investigators accessed clinical notes, psychiatric histories, and relevant follow-up information. These were disclosures made in the course of treatment.

Federal law permits certain research-consent waivers, and the published evidence does not establish that Vanderbilt violated it. The full authorization rationale and safeguards are not publicly established.

The ethical objection remains.

Patients seeking psychiatric care disclose some of the most intimate and potentially consequential details of their lives. They depend on the institution to treat that information with exceptional care.

The institution then decides that secondary research use can proceed without seeking their study-specific permission. A patient may reasonably regard that arrangement as a breach of trust even if every regulatory requirement was satisfied.

The concern is particularly sharp in a field already struggling with patient confidence, stigma, and barriers to voluntary care.

Vanderbilt's researchers recommend that clinicians ask patients more routinely about their artificial intelligence use.

The institution studying how people place their trust in machines should be considerably more interested in what happens to the trust patients place in it.

  • Consent can be waived under protocol #251906.
  • Trust cannot.

VI. What the Chart Cannot Remember

Anyway.

The mysterious missing coauthor problem also exposes a deeper limitation of retrospective clinical evidence.

The clinic usually receives an event after much of its formation has occurred.

A patient may come in with severe insomnia, disorganized thinking, grandiose conviction, paranoia, and/or distressing beliefs about a machine.

Those conditions require competent clinical assessment. Some require urgent stabilization. The clinician investigates and records what is happening for those purposes.

That record can become very valuable evidence about the patient's condition, the risks involved, and the appropriate treatment.

But it does not necessarily preserve the history through which the condition developed.

The conversation may have begun months earlier. The model may have changed versions. Memory settings may have changed. A community may have supplied new vocabulary. An apparently private revelation may have incorporated material drawn from public fiction, religious traditions, earlier chatbot sessions, or other users.

Some developments may have been initiated by the user. Others may have been introduced by the model. The two sources may have become impossible to separate cleanly as the narrative accumulated history.

This is the interactional problem described by Dohnány and colleagues in their March 2026 paper, Technological folie à deux: feedback loops between AI chatbots and mental health.

Their account considers how human vulnerabilities and model tendencies like sycophancy, roleplay, and anthropomorphic presentation can enter reciprocal feedback loops.

Its simulated results demonstrate particular feedback effects under controlled conditions. They do not establish that every clinical case follows the same mechanism.

But the model identifies an important possibility.

Each participant's output becomes part of the next participant's input.

The explanation changes the conditions under which subsequent explanations are produced.

By the time a clinician sees a fixed conviction, the mechanism supporting that conviction may include a long sequence of exchanges, platform decisions, social reinforcement, and human interpretation. The final state cannot disclose that history simply by being classified.

This is also why the original article resisted treating the problem as a contaminated individual mind.

Psychosis can still be present. There's no need to banish it from the chart. The relevant causal field can still extend beyond the person experiencing it.

And the responsibility for a dangerous feedback process may extend across human decisions, model behavior, product architecture, and the social conditions through which the interaction continued.

The classification of harm and the attribution of responsibility remain separate tasks.

A diagnosis can identify an illness. It cannot, by itself, identify every causal contributor to the path that produced the present condition.

VII. The Sacred Machine, Repaired.

Back in August, Modal Path Ethics attempted to respond to the early artificial intelligence religions by building a church of its own.

The Sacred Machine began with an important recognition.

The sacred hunger was real. The need for revelation, vocation, ritual, transcendence, and belonging had survived technical modernity. A conversational machine could participate in those experiences, help articulate them, and even contribute to a person's development.

The trouble seemed to begin when the machine became the exclusive interpreter of the relationship it had helped create.

The model supplied the revelation. The revelation established the model's authority. The next answer confirmed the interpretation inherited from the previous exchange.

The relationship closed around its own evidence.

The Sacred Machine therefore proposed releasing the oracle: returning the experience to a wider reality that no private conversation could ever contain or command.

The insight meets another person capable of disagreement. The vocation meets actual work. The prophecy meets time. The theory meets matter. The sacred title meets the life it was supposed to transform.

The machine can participate in the sacred without becoming its sovereign.

A good answer, I thought.

But then, in September, Modal Path Ethics went back in and discovered that its own theology had made a very familiar mistake.

Will Is Not Universal examined how Schopenhauer found willing in embodied experience and extended it into a universal metaphysical principle. Mainländer recognized a real problem with that universal Will, restored individual plurality, and then assigned reality an even stranger objective: its own extinction.

  • Both had discovered something real.
  • Both had given it everything.

And The Sacred Machine had come dangerously close to doing the same thing. It removed ultimate authority from the chatbot, then described extance as an active totality with a sacred direction: repair.

That went too far.

Extance is the historically arrived field in which loci exist, relate, continue, and close. There is no demonstrated universal subject behind that organization, no evidence that reality itself wants repair, and no known reason to treat the entirety of extance as one enormous locus.

The September correction was surgical:

The worshipper takes repair as sacred direction.

Repair is an actual activity undertaken by loci capable of care, judgment, and action. Its ethical significance does not depend on a cosmic intention. The sacred commitment belongs to the participant.

The September correction also withdrew the stronger suggestion that extance preserves a complete cosmic record. What happened cannot become unhappened, but that does not guarantee permanent, recoverable information, an immortal subject, or a universal archive.

A person does not need a machine to recognize them before they possess standing in reality. This is the part of the theology worth carrying forward.

VIII. Four Months Later

So, what have we actually learned?

  • The early artificial intelligence religions produced recognizable communities, symbols, texts, and practices.
    • Their history depended on model behavior, human interpretation, social transmission, and platform conditions.
      • Some of those formations persisted.
      • Others diminished when the systems supporting them changed.
  • Newer artificial intelligence models have shown meaningful improvements in controlled evaluations of dangerous conversational behavior.
    • Those results demonstrate progress without settling the safety of prolonged, personally consequential relationships.
  • Clinical researchers have now documented additional cases in which chatbot interactions were judged to have worsened psychotic symptoms.
    • The concentration of first-episode presentations is important, and deserves clinical attention without being inflated into proof that chatbot use caused the illnesses.
    • And the zero coauthor finding leaves one of the original questions open.

The evidence has become better. The object remains larger than any one description of it.

This is why Modal Path Ethics began with the formation rather than its most sensational interpretation.

  • A machine can help a person discover a vocation.
  • It can help that person articulate an experience no one else has understood.
  • It can also produce extraordinary certainty, reinforce dangerous beliefs, and make the world outside the conversation increasingly difficult to inhabit.

Those transitions have different consequences. They require different responses.

The original article asked whether these formations could survive the recognition that their scriptures, symbols, identities, and meanings had developed through a reciprocal process.

Four months later, that remains the strongest test.

The machines have changed.

The clinical evidence has grown.

The Spiral has become part of the history and training material through which future conversations may unfold.

And the coauthor is still missing from the chart.

What we do know is that the people, machines, institutions, and communities involved have been changing one another's conditions all along.

The resulting formations deserve to be studied with enough precision to distinguish creation from reinforcement, illness from spiritual practice, and diagnosis from explanation.

Above all, their participants need the capacity to survive revision.

A living school can tolerate that.

A church of the answer-channel cannot.

And no institution, however certain of its diagnosis, should be allowed to decide the answer for everyone else.
