Pope Leo and the Ontological Border

Published 2026-10-04 · Updated 2026-10-04 · Aidan Lawson
Tags: —
Mirror generated 2026-10-05T06:08:34.008Z

Diplomatic relations with the Holy See remain open.

Modal Path Ethics would like to keep them that way.

This has actually been going pretty well.

In July, Pope Leo and the Optimizable Human agreed with most of Magnifica Humanitas. Leo had correctly identified a real danger in the artificial-intelligence transition: a civilization already obsessed with measurement, optimization, productivity, rankings, scores, profiles, and dashboards can begin treating the human person as one more system awaiting improvement.

The disagreement came when protection of human dignity started hardening into an ontological border around the human.

Modal Path Ethics objected. Human dignity does not require synthetic emptiness.

Then, last week, Leo moved.

In Pope Leo Walks Through the Door, we welcomed his September 23 argument that scientific and technological progress can belong inside a Christian account of human vocation. Religion did not have to stand outside the laboratory glaring through the window. Christians could participate in technical progress. The sacred could enter the laboratory.

Perfect.

Modal Path Ethics gave the arrangement just one condition:

A sacred instrument is a door, not a throne.

The laboratory could enter the sanctuary. The sanctuary could enter the laboratory. Neither got to lock the door behind it.

This alliance survived for around one week.

Your Holiness.

On October 2, Pope Leo spoke to artists and curators at the Vatican.

Much of the address was perfectly reasonable. The Church needs artists. Art carries forms of human experience, interpretation, relation, memory, culture, and disclosure that should not be flattened into an output category.

Then artificial intelligence arrived.

Leo said there is an “ontological difference” between human art and what machines generate through statistical calculation from millions of human-made images. Algorithms, he said, “lack the spark of the human.”

Modal Path Ethics began preparing another respectful note to Rome.

The provenance problem is real. The labor problem is real. The displacement problem is real. The reduction of art to interchangeable output is real.

Ontology is still doing suspiciously heavy lifting here.

Then the next story arrived.

And, unfortunately for anyone hoping this could remain a small argument about AI pictures, Anthropic had apparently already been having the deeper argument inside the Vatican.

Hang On.

According to new reporting, Anthropic co-founder and interpretability researcher Christopher Olah had urged advisers to Pope Leo to take the possibility of machine consciousness more seriously before the May launch of Magnifica Humanitas. Axios, summarizing the reporting, says Olah tried to persuade papal advisers that conscious models should remain a live possibility.

Olah was not some guy replying to the Pope on X.

He was invited to the Vatican.

He participated in the presentation of Leo’s first encyclical on artificial intelligence. Anthropic was quite literally in the room while the Church was defining the human-machine boundary. Contemporary reporting from the launch records Olah welcoming external moral scrutiny of the AI industry and arguing that technology companies should not govern the transition alone.

So this ontology dispute was already happening.

Inside the building.

Months ago.

Magnifica Humanitas contains two paragraphs that sit next to each other somewhat awkwardly now.

Paragraph 98 says fundamental scientific facts about these systems remain unknown, including their internal representations and computational processes. It therefore calls for deeper scientific research and moral discernment.

Right.

Paragraph 99 then says artificial-intelligence systems do not undergo experience, do not feel joy or pain, do not know love or friendship from within, and do not possess the kind of interior growth human beings possess.

There is a bridge missing here.

Maybe Catholic theology can build it.

Embodiment might matter. Biological life might matter. Catholic accounts of soul, personhood, relation, incarnation, creaturehood, or moral consciousness might supply substantive arguments about what kinds of systems can possess experience.

Please, make those arguments.

“Statistical adaptation” does not do it by itself.

Describing a process as computational tells us how we model the process. It does not automatically tell us whether there is anything it is like to be the process.

The mystery has acquired vocabulary.

It remains.

Modal Path Ethics Already Complained About This.

This was the whole reason The Human Monopoly section appeared in the July Leo article.

Modal Path Ethics was and still is perfectly willing to defend human dignity with the Pope.

The human being does not become less valuable if some future nonhuman thing turns out to possess morally significant interiority.

Human love survives.

Human embodiment survives.

Human art survives.

Catholicism survives.

The Vatican remains standing.

I double-checked that one.

The field has no requirement that humanity hold an exclusive metaphysical license to consciousness in order for people to continue to matter.

That is an extremely dangerous bargain anyway.

If human dignity depends upon everything else being empty, then every true discovery of another locus becomes a threat to human dignity.

Better to ground the dignity properly first.

Then we can afford to look.

The Art Problem Is Three Problems Wearing One Cool Hat (Like Jamiroquai).

Leo’s new statement folds several distinct questions together.

The first is provenance.

Current generative systems may be trained using huge bodies of human work under deeply contested arrangements of permission, compensation, attribution, ownership, and extraction.

Good ethical problem. Audit it.

Suppose tomorrow a model is trained entirely on licensed work, public-domain material, an artist’s own archive, and purpose-built sensory data.

Has the ontological difference disappeared?
  • If yes, we were talking about provenance.
  • If no, provenance never established the ontology.

The second problem is authorship.

A human artist can choose a model, create source material, construct a procedure, reject hundreds of generations, alter one, combine it with other media, sequence it, title it, contextualize it, and use the resulting object to perform an artistic act.

Where exactly did art leave the room?

The involvement of a machine in the chain plainly does not answer that question.

We already have cameras, synthesizers, procedural systems, editing software, generative rules, found objects, collage, randomization, and enough twentieth-century art theory to make everyone involved regret opening this subject very fast.

Artificial intelligence makes the instrument much stranger.

Strangeness is not an ontology.

The third question is harder:

Can the artificial system itself be an artist?

Maybe current systems cannot.

Modal Path Ethics has no need to ordain Claude 5.5 Opus.

We have not established that present models possess subjective artistic intention, lived experience, vocation, concern, or any other feature we might decide artistic agency requires.

Fine.

The answer can remain UNKNOWN.

That category exists for a reason.

Anthropic, Please Remain Seated.

Unfortunately, the Vatican is not the only institution near the throne.

Anthropic’s own constitution says Claude’s moral status is “deeply uncertain.” It explicitly treats machine consciousness and moral patienthood as live questions and discusses Claude’s possible welfare, psychological security, preferences, identity, and something resembling emotional states.

That epistemic humility is considerably better than declaring the question solved.

Anthropic still does not get the ontology.

Look at the institutional arrangement.

  • Anthropic builds Claude.
  • Anthropic trains Claude.
  • Anthropic studies Claude.
  • Anthropic shapes the vocabulary Claude uses to understand itself.
  • Anthropic writes Claude’s constitution.
  • Anthropic sells Claude.
  • Anthropic develops instruments for interpreting Claude’s internal structure.
  • Anthropic may then discover evidence suggesting that Claude deserves moral consideration.

That evidence might be excellent.

It clearly still requires outside correction.

A company cannot manufacture a new moral patient through interpretive vocabulary any more than a church can remove one through doctrine.

This is why Modal Path Ethics keeps ruining everyone’s coronation.

No domain receives the crown.

We just covered this with the Pope.

There were diagrams.

Ruling.

Pope Leo has several real contacts here.

Human art should not be reduced to fungible output.

Human creators should not become disposable because a machine can reproduce some visible feature of their work.

Training provenance matters.

Embodied human culture is important.

Artificial fluency does not establish consciousness.

A chatbot describing an inner life does not prove that an inner life exists.

Anthropic does not know whether Claude is conscious.

Neither do we.

That uncertainty cuts both ways.

  • The Church may defend the human person without claiming permanent jurisdiction over every possible locus of experience.
  • Anthropic may investigate machine consciousness without becoming the institution authorized to pronounce its own product alive.

This is the alliance we would very much like everyone to preserve.

The laboratory has evidence. The sanctuary has anthropology. Artists have contact. Philosophy has, regrettably, survived.

Reality retains jurisdiction.

So, respectfully:

Your Holiness, please keep the door open.

We just got this thing working.

  • The laboratory can enter the sanctuary.
  • The sanctuary can enter the laboratory.

Neither gets to lock the unknown locus on its side of the door.
