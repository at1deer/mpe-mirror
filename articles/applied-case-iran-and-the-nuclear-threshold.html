Applied Case: Iran and the Nuclear Threshold

I. The Number

The inspectors knew how much uranium Iran had enriched to sixty percent.

The number was 440.9 kilograms.

That was the International Atomic Energy Agency’s estimate for June 13, 2025, the first day of the Israeli attack on Iran’s nuclear facilities. The figure did not arrive through clairvoyance, espionage mythology, or one very serious person squinting at a satellite image until the mountain confessed.

It came from a relation.

Iran supplied declarations. Inspectors entered facilities. Material was measured. Samples were taken. Operating records were compared against inventories. Cameras watched. Seals held. Equipment, containers, feed, product, waste, and movement entered an accounting structure that could be checked again later.

The Agency estimated Iran’s total enriched-uranium stockpile at 9,874.9 kilograms. Most of it remained at lower enrichment levels. The politically dominant part was the 440.9 kilograms enriched up to sixty percent uranium-235, including 432.9 kilograms that the Agency had directly verified in uranium hexafluoride form.

Every clause in that sentence matters.

  • Estimated means the number belonged to a disciplined evidentiary process rather than omniscience.
  • Up to sixty percent describes material composition, not an assembled weapon.
  • Verified means an external instrument had made contact with part of the declared stockpile.
  • As of June 13 means this number had a date.

A date is important when the material can move.

The number was therefore less like a photograph and more like a secure place on a path. The Agency could say where its knowledge had reached, when it had reached there, which material it had measured, which material it had estimated, and what later inspection would need to confirm.

That is the first thing this article needs from the nuclear field:

Verification is a relation across time.

The public argument often asks a simpler question.

Does Iran have the bomb?

That question has the emotional advantage of a switch.

  • Off: no bomb.
  • On: bomb.

The material field is less courteous.

A nuclear-weapons capability is assembled through a sequence of technical, political, industrial, military, and administrative transitions. Some are visible. Some can be monitored. Some can be hidden. Some require facilities. Some require knowledge that cannot be bombed out of a population’s head. Some depend on a state deciding to cross a line it has spent years approaching, denying, threatening, bargaining around, or attempting to keep permanently ambiguous.

The number did not tell the Agency that Iran had built a nuclear weapon.

The number also did not say everything was fine.

Sixty-percent enrichment sits far above ordinary power-reactor fuel and much closer to weapons-grade material in the technical work already completed. A large stockpile at that level compresses the remaining material-production path. It changes the time available for diplomacy, detection, deliberation, intervention, and mistake. It gives every intelligence service, military command, neighboring government, alliance planner, sanctions office, and frightened public a shorter clock to argue over.

That clock was already acting.

Israel treated the program as an approaching existential danger. The United States had restored maximum pressure and declared that Iran could not be allowed to acquire a nuclear weapon. Gulf states lived inside the possibility of regional war and proliferation. Enrichment functioned as sovereign capacity, bargaining power, a possible hedge against attack, and a field in which Western promises had already proven reversible. The International Atomic Energy Agency kept trying to preserve the narrower object that all of those actors required and repeatedly endangered:

A material history capable of being checked.

Then the war reached the file.

The last verified number did not disappear when the first missile arrived.

It became the beginning of an unknown interval.

II. The Places Are Hit

On June 13, 2025, Israel began military attacks on Iran that included several nuclear facilities.

The International Atomic Energy Agency stopped its in-field verification activities for safety reasons. The attacks continued through June 24. The United States entered the direct attack on June 22, striking Fordow, Natanz, and Esfahan.

The facilities known to have contained declared nuclear material and to have been affected included

  • the Fordow Fuel Enrichment Plant,
  • the Fuel Enrichment Plant and Pilot Fuel Enrichment Plant at Natanz,
  • the Uranium Conversion Facility,
  • the Fuel Manufacturing Plant,
  • the Fuel Plate Fabrication Plant, and
  • the Enriched Uranium Dioxide Powder Plant.
    • The Khondab Heavy Water Research Reactor, then under construction and not containing nuclear material, was also affected.

That list sounds industrial because it is industrial.

A nuclear program does not live inside one dramatic room with a large red device under a cloth. It occupies a chain of places where material is converted, enriched, fabricated, stored, measured, assembled into fuel forms, processed for research, or held under safeguards.

An attack on that chain can destroy equipment, collapse access routes, interrupt electricity, kill personnel, damage records, scatter responsibilities, force emergency movement, and make ordinary facility declarations impossible to interpret from the outside.

The United States government described the June operation as a decisive success. The White House said the facilities had been obliterated. Senior officials said Iran’s ability to create a nuclear weapon had been destroyed or set back by years.

Physical damage was real. Iran itself acknowledged severe damage. The Agency expected very significant damage at some sites and identified damaged buildings and infrastructure through satellite imagery and official reporting.

The word obliterated performed a different job.

It converted battle damage into a completed answer.

The inspectors had a narrower sentence available.

They could no longer determine the current quantity or whereabouts of Iran’s enriched-uranium stockpile with the same confidence and accuracy as before. They could not access affected facilities. They could not verify the status of the nuclear material previously declared there. They could not determine whether enrichment continued elsewhere, whether material had been transferred for protection, whether equipment had survived, or what had happened during the interval in which the verification system was absent.

Iran’s foreign minister had informed the Agency that Iran would adopt special measures to protect nuclear equipment and materials. The Director General answered that transfers of safeguarded nuclear material had to be declared. By the end of June, the Agency had withdrawn its inspectors from Iran because of the security situation. On July 2, Iran enacted a law suspending cooperation with the Agency pending conditions set through its own national-security process.

The attack and the Iranian response therefore changed two fields at once.

The first was physical.

  • Facilities were damaged.
  • Equipment was struck.
  • Access was made dangerous.
  • Operations were interrupted.

The second was epistemic.

  • Inspectors left.
  • Declarations stopped arriving normally.
  • Cameras, seals, records, and prior inventories could no longer be joined through routine access.
  • Material that had been visible inside an international accounting relation entered uncertainty.

These are not identical outcomes.

A destroyed centrifuge and an unverified centrifuge are different objects. A damaged container and an absent declaration are different problems. A collapsed tunnel and a gap in the material account do different kinds of work in the field.

The bombing campaign could produce massive physical destruction and still fail to produce reliable knowledge of what remained.

That is exactly what happened.

During the following months, Iran and the Agency negotiated partial procedures for resuming safeguards. Inspectors eventually regained access to facilities the Agency considered unaffected by the June attacks. By February 2026, each unaffected facility containing nuclear material had been accessed at least once, although access, declarations, and verification remained incomplete and contested. The affected facilities and their associated material remained outside normal verification.

Then the war reached the file again.

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel launched further attacks on Iran, including sites connected to nuclear facilities. The Agency stopped in-field verification again. Iran later reported additional attacks around Natanz, Bushehr, and Khondab. Satellite imagery showed damage at several locations. The Agency managed one routine inspection at the Bushehr Nuclear Power Plant in early June 2026.

Outside Bushehr, the verification field remained largely closed.

The Director General told the Board of Governors that the Agency had gone almost a year without access to the declared nuclear facilities damaged in June 2025. It had lost continuity of knowledge over the previously declared material at those sites, including roughly 440 kilograms of highly enriched uranium. With the exception of Bushehr, the Agency could no longer effectively perform its safeguards obligations across the declared Iranian nuclear field.

That statement did not mean the material had been diverted into weapons.

It meant the instrument built to determine whether declared material remained in peaceful activity could no longer make the required determination.

In July 2026, the Agency could still draw a peaceful-use conclusion for declared material at facilities and locations outside facilities that had not been affected by the June 2025 attacks. That precision matters. The whole Iranian nuclear field had not become one black box.

The affected field had.

  • One institution declared the threat crushed.
  • The institution responsible for measuring the material said it had lost the path.

III. The Knowledge Relation

An inspector is pretty easy to misunderstand.

The cartoon version walks into a facility, opens one suspicious cabinet, looks underneath the uranium, writes NO BOMB FOUND on a clipboard, and returns to Vienna before lunch.

International safeguards are more difficult and much less cinematic.

The International Atomic Energy Agency evaluates state declarations about nuclear material and nuclear activities. Its inspectors verify quantities, locations, movements, facility designs, operating records, and relevant changes. They count items, take measurements, collect samples, compare reports, inspect equipment, and use containment and surveillance systems to preserve knowledge between visits.

  • A seal says that a protected container, enclosure, valve, or piece of equipment has not been opened without detection.
  • A camera says that movement through a monitored area can be reviewed across the interval when an inspector was elsewhere.
  • A detector says that a material or process left a measurable trace.
  • A design-information verification says that the facility being built, modified, operated, or decommissioned still corresponds to the facility the state has declared.
    • Nuclear material accountancy says that beginning inventory, additions, transfers, processing, losses, waste, product, and ending inventory must fit together well enough for the declared material history to remain credible.

None of these instruments works through trust in the ordinary sense.

The inspector does not have to believe that the state is nice.

The state does not have to believe that the inspector is politically innocent.

The relation works when declarations can be tested against matter, records can be tested against movement, and yesterday’s verified condition can be joined to today’s verified condition without an unexplained hole between them.

This is continuity of knowledge.

The phrase sounds boring. This is good.

Boring language can sometimes protect a field from the people who want every uncertainty to arrive already screaming.

Continuity of knowledge means the Agency can preserve confidence that previously verified material or equipment has not changed status without detection between inspections. Cameras, seals, detectors, records, and repeat access all contribute. The chain reduces the amount of the field that has to be rediscovered from the beginning every time an inspector returns.

A break in that continuity creates verification debt.

The missing interval has to be reconstructed.

  • Was material moved?
  • Was it converted into another chemical form?
  • Was it split among containers?
  • Was it buried beneath damaged infrastructure?
  • Was it destroyed, contaminated, dispersed, or removed?
  • Did centrifuge production continue?
  • Were new cascades assembled?
  • Did a declared facility resume operation?
  • Did work occur somewhere that had not been declared?

A later visit may answer some of these questions.

It cannot make the missing interval unhappen.

This is why the 440.9-kilogram figure retained such power after the facilities were hit. The number was the final stable point before the chain broke. Every later estimate had to begin from that point and account for everything that could have happened after it.

Satellite imagery can show construction, vehicle activity, structural damage, excavation, repair, new entrances, demolished buildings, and movement around a site.

It cannot reliably perform material accountancy through a mountain.

Intelligence can intercept communications, recruit sources, analyze procurement, watch transport, model production, and estimate intent.

Intelligence is not an internationally administered safeguards conclusion. It may be more secret, more penetrating, more politically contested, or more capable in a specific case. It does not create the same public correction mechanism.

Bomb damage assessment can determine that a roof collapsed, a tunnel entrance was struck, electrical infrastructure failed, or machinery likely experienced destructive shock.

It cannot count every gram of safeguarded material that entered the event.

The verification field therefore survives through plurality.

  • State declarations.
  • International inspection.
  • Technical measurement.
  • Remote monitoring.
  • Satellite observation.
  • Intelligence.
  • Open-source analysis.
  • Diplomatic reporting.
  • Physical return.

Each instrument has limits. The dangerous move is allowing one to impersonate all the others.

  • A military command can know that it hit the target.
    • That does not make the command an inspector.
  • An Iranian official can know that the state moved material to protect it.
    • That does not make the movement declared.
  • An inspector can verify declared material at an accessible facility.
    • That does not prove the absence of undeclared work across an entire state unless the relevant legal authorities, access, information, and analytic conditions exist.

The field needs each statement to remain inside its own jurisdiction long enough for the statements to answer one another.

The June 2025 attack broke this relation at the exact moment the material history mattered most.

The February 2026 attack broke it again while repair remained incomplete.

This is the first great distortion of the threshold war:

Destruction was allowed to impersonate verification.
  • If the facilities were destroyed, the program was gone.
    • If the program was gone, the material no longer required an account.
      • If the account could not be produced, the uncertainty became evidence that Iran had hidden what the attack failed to reach.

That loop can feed itself forever.

  • Attack because the program may be hidden.
    • Lose access because of the attack.
      • Treat lost access as proof of hidden continuation.
        • Attack again because the uncertainty has grown.

A correction instrument becomes progressively harder to rebuild after each use of force intended to correct the underlying danger.

The threshold then acquires a strange defense.

The harder outsiders try to erase it physically, the less able they become to prove what remains.

IV. A Bomb Is a Sequence

A nuclear weapon is a political symbol powerful enough to make the machinery beneath it disappear.

The public sees the bomb.

The field contains a sequence.

A state needs access to nuclear material and the industrial capacity to process it. Uranium has to move through mining, milling, conversion, enrichment, and forms suitable for whatever civilian or military use is intended. A weapons path then requires further material production or preparation, specialized design and engineering work, components able to function together, testing or substitute forms of validation, a means of delivery, and a command structure capable of controlling the result.

Each step can contain several steps of its own.

Each step creates different evidence.

Each step is vulnerable to different instruments.

  • Safeguards concentrate on nuclear material and declared nuclear activities.
  • Export controls concentrate on procurement and transfer.
  • Intelligence services concentrate on secret organization, facilities, personnel, communications, and intent.
  • Military planners concentrate on sites, timelines, delivery systems, dispersal, hardening, and retaliation.
  • Diplomats concentrate on agreed limits, access, sanctions, guarantees, and defection.
  • Religious and political institutions concentrate on legitimacy, prohibition, sovereignty, national dignity, and survival.

The word program can compress all of this into one object.

That compression is sometimes necessary. It is also where analysis starts losing organs.

Iran has developed extensive nuclear knowledge, enrichment infrastructure, advanced centrifuges, a large stockpile of enriched uranium, conversion and fuel-cycle capabilities, missile forces, and institutions capable of preserving technical work under pressure. Iran has also remained under a comprehensive safeguards agreement, repeatedly declared that its program is peaceful, and invoked a religious prohibition against nuclear weapons.

The field therefore contains capability, obligation, denial, restraint, opacity, violation, inspection, sabotage, diplomacy, and threat at the same time.

A clean binary cannot hold it.

This matters because different interventions act on different parts of the sequence.

  • A strike can damage a facility.
    • It cannot erase every engineer’s training.
  • An assassination can remove expertise.
    • It cannot guarantee that the expertise was unique or unrecoverable.
  • A sanctions regime can slow procurement and raise cost.
    • It can also reward smuggling networks and make hidden acquisition more institutionally valuable.
  • An inspection can verify material and facility status.
    • It cannot replace a political settlement capable of keeping access open.
  • A sacred prohibition can make weapon production religiously illegitimate within a governing tradition.
    • It cannot tell an external inspector where the uranium is.
  • A state can possess the material capacity to move toward a weapon while choosing not to do so.
    • That choice is morally and strategically significant.
      • It remains reversible.

This is why the nuclear threshold is an instrument rather than one line painted on a laboratory floor.

The threshold exists across several distances:

  • the distance from declared peaceful use
    • to safeguards violation;
  • the distance from lower enrichment
    • to highly enriched material;
  • the distance from material
    • to device;
  • the distance from device
    • to deliverable weapon;
  • the distance from technical possibility
    • to political decision;
  • the distance from secret decision
    • to detectable action;
  • the distance from suspicion
    • to preventive war.

A state can shorten one distance while leaving another intact.

An adversary may decide that the remaining distances no longer provide safety.

The threshold begins acting when the possibility of later stages reorganizes the present field.

Iran did not need to assemble a weapon for Israel to plan attacks around the possibility.

It did not need to declare a weapons decision for the United States to build sanctions and military options around preventing one.

It did not need to cross the final line for neighboring states to consider their own future capabilities.

The imagined successor state entered the current one.

That is how possibility becomes strategy.

V. Breakout Is Not Possession

Breakout time is one of the nuclear field’s most useful and most abused clocks.

In policy usage, it generally means the estimated time required for a state to produce enough weapons-grade fissile material for one nuclear device if it decides to move rapidly from its current position.

The definition is already narrower than the headlines built from it.

Breakout does not include the entire path to an operational nuclear weapon.

It does not by itself include designing and fabricating a device, integrating components, validating performance, producing a delivery system, building command arrangements, or deciding how an actual arsenal would be controlled and used.

A breakout estimate also depends on assumptions.

  • Which facilities remain operational?
  • Which centrifuges are installed?
  • How efficiently do they perform?
  • Which material stocks are available?
  • Can the state use declared facilities, hidden facilities, or both?
  • Does it prioritize speed, secrecy, reliability, or survivability?
  • What has been damaged?
  • What has been moved?
  • What can inspectors see?

Change the assumptions and the clock changes.

That does not make the clock fake.

A short material-production timeline places real pressure on every other part of the field. If the time required to produce weapons-grade material becomes shorter than the time required to detect, confirm, consult, decide, mobilize, negotiate, or intervene, then decision-makers begin acting before certainty arrives.

This is where the threshold becomes dangerous even without possession.

A shrinking breakout estimate can:

  • increase pressure for preventive attack;
  • reduce tolerance for inspection disputes;
  • make routine technical activity look like the opening move of a dash;
  • reward worst-case intelligence;
  • encourage dispersal and secrecy in the targeted state;
  • strengthen hard-liners who argue that latency invites attack without preventing it;
    • and make every delay in diplomacy appear irreversible.

The clock can become a weapon against the time needed to understand the clock.

Still, breakout is not possession.

The distinction protects the field from two opposite lies.

  • The first lie says:
Iran has no assembled bomb, so the program creates no urgent danger.

That erases the significance of accumulated highly enriched material, technical capability, inspection limits, undeclared-location disputes, and a compressed warning interval.

  • The second lie says:
Iran could produce weapons-grade material quickly, so Iran already possesses a nuclear weapon in every morally relevant sense.

That erases remaining technical transitions, political decisions, command questions, and the possibility of verified restraint.

Both lies destroy diplomacy from different directions.

  • The first removes the reason for an agreement.
  • The second removes the object an agreement could still preserve.

The threshold exists inside that remaining difference.

  • As long as material has not become a weapon, a path remains available for
    • limits,
    • dilution,
    • export,
    • monitored storage,
    • dismantlement,
    • conversion,
    • inspection, and
        • agreed distance from the final line.

As long as the capability remains advanced, that path requires stronger instruments than a declaration of peaceful intent.

The correct sentence is harder:

Iran had reached a position where the material path could become dangerously short while weapon possession remained unproven and preventable.

This sentence offers nobody the emotional satisfaction of total innocence or completed guilt.

Good. Those satisfactions were helping the field move toward war.

VI. The Threshold Already Acts

The nuclear threshold began reorganizing the region before any declared Iranian weapon existed.

It reorganized budgets.

Israel developed intelligence, sabotage, covert-action, missile-defense, civil-defense, and strike options around the Iranian program. The United States built sanctions, naval posture, airpower, bunker-penetration capability, diplomacy, intelligence cooperation, and alliance commitments around preventing acquisition. Gulf governments adjusted security relationships, air defense, diplomacy, and their own civilian nuclear ambitions around the possibility of an Iranian weapon and the possibility of an attack meant to stop one.

It reorganized Iranian politics.

The nuclear program became a sovereignty object, a technological achievement, a bargaining instrument, a symbol of resistance to foreign control, a source of sanctions burden, and a protected jurisdiction of institutions formed through revolution and war. External pressure could weaken the economy while strengthening the domestic claim that only guarded, opaque, and militarized capacity could preserve independence.

It reorganized law.

Safeguards reports, Board resolutions, Security Council measures, sanctions authorities, waivers, procurement restrictions, inspection arrangements, and disputed legal interpretations accumulated around material that had not become a declared arsenal.

It reorganized ordinary life.

Sanctions entered currency, medicine, trade, employment, infrastructure, family plans, academic exchange, industrial supply, and the daily work of remaining economically connected to the world. Regional escalation entered airspace, shipping, energy prices, travel, conscription, mourning, and the possibility that a nuclear facility near a city could become a battlefield.

It reorganized war.

By June 2025, the possibility of future possession had become grounds for present attack. By February 2026, the attacks had widened into another direct campaign. Facilities, missiles, naval forces, regional partners, and the government itself entered an operation publicly described as ending the nuclear threat.

The threshold had acquired many of the bomb’s political effects without acquiring the bomb’s stable deterrent status.

This is the terrible middle position.

  • A completed arsenal can make attack catastrophically risky.
  • A distant program may be containable through ordinary monitoring and diplomacy.
  • A mature threshold can look dangerous enough to attack and vulnerable enough to attack successfully.

That combination invites prevention.

Prevention then changes the threshold.

  • Facilities harden.
  • Knowledge disperses.
  • Access becomes a security risk.
  • Inspectors become vulnerable to military timing.
  • Material may be moved for protection.
  • Leaders receive evidence that latency did not deter attack.
  • Adversaries receive evidence that delay allows reconstruction.
  • Neighboring states receive evidence that treaty restraint may provide less protection than possession.

The instrument begins generating the transition it was built to prevent.

This does not mean the attacks automatically caused a weapons decision. The public evidence does not support that claim.

It means the incentive structure changed in that direction.

  • A state under attack because it might someday acquire a weapon can conclude that the most dangerous condition is remaining permanently capable and permanently unarmed.
  • Its adversaries can conclude that the most dangerous condition is any surviving capacity at all.

No stable stopping point exists inside those conclusions.

The war then keeps reaching for proof through destruction.

The destruction keeps reducing proof.

The threshold becomes harder to measure, harder to negotiate, and more valuable as protection.

This is why Iran belongs immediately before The Nuclear Veto.

  • Russia showed the field after possession.
    • An existing arsenal placed a ceiling over how directly outside powers could correct invasion without risking civilizational escalation.
  • Iran shows the field before possession.
    • The approach to an arsenal drew military action, reorganized alliances, and began creating veto-like effects through uncertainty, retaliation, geography, and the danger of what the next transition might produce.

The bomb was absent.

The nuclear field was already present.

VII. The First Refusal

  • The absence of a declared Iranian nuclear weapon does not make the field safe.
  • The existence of an Iranian nuclear threshold does not make war corrective.

Both statements have to survive the whole article.

Iran’s accumulation of uranium enriched up to sixty percent matters.

Its safeguards failures matter.

The unresolved undeclared-location questions matter.

Restrictions on access matter.

Advanced centrifuge capacity matters.

Missile forces, regional armed relationships, threats, secrecy, and state repression matter.

None of these becomes less real because Israel possesses overwhelming conventional capability, because the United States previously withdrew from a nuclear agreement, or because Iranian security fears have a history.

The attacks on safeguarded nuclear facilities also matter.

Inspector withdrawal matters.

Lost continuity of knowledge matters.

Civilian and environmental risk matters.

Claims of obliteration cannot replace a material account.

None of these becomes less real because Iran violated obligations, because the program had reached an alarming threshold, or because the states conducting the attacks believed delay had become intolerable.

The first refusal is therefore double.

  • A peaceful-use claim cannot substitute for verification.
  • A bomb cannot substitute for an inspector.
  • The threshold required correction.
    • Correction required a functioning evidence relation.
      • The evidence relation required access, safety, declarations, measurement, repeated return, and enough political restraint for technical contact to survive.
        • Iran weakened that relation through concealment, unresolved safeguards failures, access restrictions, and the strategic use of opacity.
        • The attacking states weakened it through force directed at the facilities and conditions on which verification depended.

Responsibility is not equal in every act simply because several acts entered the failure. The point is causal before it is distributive.

The field lost the instrument it needed.

That loss cannot be repaired by choosing the preferred villain and assigning the whole missing inventory to them.

  • The material remains somewhere in reality.
    • The inspectors’ access does not.

The argument has begun at the end: after the revolution, after the Shah’s nuclear program, after the Iran-Iraq War, after the fatwa, after Natanz, after the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, after maximum pressure, and after the regional threshold became a war.

The object those histories keep acting upon is already here:

  • a measured stockpile;
  • a compressed path;
  • a set of damaged facilities;
  • an interrupted verification relation;
  • and a war conducted against what the material might become.

The threshold had already crossed the region.

Its Iranian history begins under a different flag.

VIII. Atoms for the Shah

The nuclear program entered Iran wearing a suit from Washington.

This is an inconvenient opening for every account that wants the Iranian atom to begin as a secret religious weapon crawling out of the Islamic Revolution.

Iran’s first modern nuclear institutions belonged to the monarchy. The United States and Iran signed a civil nuclear cooperation agreement in the 1950s under the Atoms for Peace program. In 1967, the United States supplied the Tehran Nuclear Research Center with a five-megawatt research reactor fueled at the time with highly enriched uranium. The reactor trained personnel, supported research, and later became important for the production of medical isotopes.

The machine did not arrive as an accusation.

It arrived as development.

  • Science.
  • Training.
  • Medicine.
  • Modern industry.
    • A state joining the technical future.

Mohammad Reza Shah’s government expanded the ambition in the 1970s. The Atomic Energy Organization of Iran was created in 1973. Foreign contractors were invited. Agreements were made for reactors, fuel-cycle assistance, training, and infrastructure. Work began on two large reactors at Bushehr through the German firm Kraftwerk Union. The Shah spoke in the language of oil wealth, electricity demand, modernization, and national capacity.

There were strategic ambiguities around the program even then. Great technical programs always carry futures larger than the purpose printed on the first brochure. A state that masters reactors, fuel, materials, and nuclear administration acquires knowledge with more than one possible continuation. That does not make every research reactor a bomb in childhood. It means the field has acquired depth.

The important fact is simpler:

The atom crossed the regime change.

The monarchy built nuclear prestige through alliance with the United States and Europe. The Islamic Republic later rebuilt nuclear sovereignty partly through opposition to the United States and Europe.

The same general instrument could serve two incompatible state stories.

  • Under the Shah,
    • nuclear technology testified that Iran was a trusted modernizing power welcomed into the high-technology order.
  • Under the Islamic Republic,
    • nuclear technology would testify that Iran could not be excluded from that order by sanctions, pressure, sabotage, assassination, or foreign permission.

The atom did not select the story.

It entered the stories available to the state carrying it.

That is why this article has to begin the revolution before it returns to enrichment. The technical program did not become a threshold by chemistry alone. It passed through a political field already trained to associate foreign access with foreign control, national weakness with foreign intervention, and unguarded institutions with the possibility of another state entering through them.

That training did not begin in 1979.

It had a date everybody in the argument still carries.

IX. 1953

In 1951, Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh and the Iranian parliament nationalized the oil industry.

The issue was not a minor contract dispute with unusually dramatic hats.

Iran’s oil had long been organized through a concessionary relation dominated by the British-controlled Anglo-Iranian Oil Company. Oil revenue, labor, ownership, sovereignty, and the state’s ability to act without foreign permission were all inside the conflict. Nationalization made the relation answerable to an Iranian political process that Britain did not accept.

Britain answered with economic pressure, legal action, covert planning, and efforts to remove Mosaddegh. The United States initially moved between mediation, Cold War anxiety, concern over instability, and fear of communist influence. By 1953, the Eisenhower administration and the British government were cooperating in the operation known in American files as TPAJAX.

This is no longer an allegation surviving on a smuggled pamphlet.

The United States Department of State’s own published historical record contains an entire documentary section titled Planning and Implementation of Operation TPAJAX, March-August 1953. The archive includes memoranda, intelligence planning, contacts, propaganda activity, political maneuver, and the coordinated attempt to remove Mosaddegh and strengthen the Shah.

  • Iranian actors remained actors throughout the event.
    • Clerics, royalists, military officers, politicians, crowds, merchants, nationalists, communists, opponents of Mosaddegh, supporters of Mosaddegh, and people trying to survive the instability all occupied the field.
      • The coup cannot be described as two foreign intelligence services pressing a button that converted a sovereign country into scenery.
        • The first attempt failed.
          • The later overthrow depended on Iranian networks, divisions, institutions, and force.
  • Foreign intervention remains foreign intervention even when it finds local handles.
    • That is usually how intervention works.
      • The intervention does not need to invent every grievance.
      • It needs to enter a grievance field,
        • fund some routes,
          • close others,
        • coordinate people who could not otherwise move together,
        • amplify selected stories, and
          • make one successor state more reachable than the alternatives.

Mosaddegh was removed. The Shah returned with stronger power. The monarchy’s relation to the United States deepened. The Iranian political field learned something it would keep teaching itself in larger and smaller forms for generations:

A foreign power could enter an Iranian constitutional crisis and help decide which government survived it.

The conclusion does not require every later suspicion to be true.

That distinction matters.

  • A historical conspiracy can train a culture toward conspiracy explanation.
    • Once external capture has happened, later actors can invoke the possibility against real operations,
      • imagined operations,
      • organic dissent,
      • rival factions,
      • independent journalism,
      • protest,
      • elections,
      • technical inspection,
      • academic contact, and
      • every embarrassing fact that arrives with an accent.
        • The wound can preserve contact with danger.
        • It can also become an interpretation machine.

1953 therefore creates two obligations that later Iranian politics repeatedly separates.

  • The first is the obligation to take foreign interference seriously.
  • The second is the obligation to prove that a particular opponent, protest, institution, or disagreement is foreign interference before treating it as captured.

The Islamic Republic would inherit the first obligation as a central sacred memory.

It would often weaken the second.

This is the beginning of the guardian problem.

A state formed around protection from capture needs instruments capable of identifying capture. Intelligence services, political vetting, security law, revolutionary institutions, clerical authority, and public vigilance can all serve that function.

The danger begins when the guardian receives the power to define disobedience as proof of the danger it was created to guard against.

Then the wound becomes self-confirming.

  • Opposition proves penetration.
  • Demands for openness prove softness.
  • Negotiation proves surrender.
  • Cultural difference proves infiltration.
  • Economic failure proves sabotage.
  • Protest proves foreign design.
  • The guardian’s own excesses prove that enemies forced the guardian to become more powerful.

The room seals itself through the memory of a door that was once genuinely breached.

This does not make 1953 an all-purpose explanation for the Islamic Republic. More than a quarter-century of monarchy, modernization, repression, oil transformation, religious organization, class change, and political struggle still had to occur.

It makes 1953 part of the field’s active information.

Foreign capture had happened.

Every future guardian could point to the scar.

X. The Monarchy’s Security Machine

The Shah did not rule for another twenty-five years by holding up a little photograph of the coup and hoping the population became respectful.

The monarchy built.

It expanded education, infrastructure, urban industry, military capacity, state administration, public health, and the technical institutions of a modernizing country. The White Revolution redistributed some land, extended voting rights to women, promoted literacy and rural health, and attempted to reorganize the relation among monarchy, landlords, clergy, peasantry, markets, and state.

These changes altered real lives.

They also arrived through an increasingly centralized political order.

Modernization did not make power more corrigible in proportion to the new capacities it created. Political parties were controlled or suppressed. Independent labor organization faced restriction. Censorship narrowed public correction. The National Front and left organizations were repressed. The security and intelligence organization known as SAVAK entered political life through surveillance, detention, interrogation, censorship, and fear.

The monarchy’s problem was not a total absence of development.

Its problem was development routed through a state that treated autonomous political organization as a threat to development, the monarchy, national stability, or all three at once.

The security instrument and the modernization instrument began protecting each other.

  • The country was changing too quickly for instability.
    • Instability required stronger security.
      • Stronger security weakened peaceful correction.
        • Weakened correction pushed opposition underground, into exile, into mosques, into clandestine cells, into revolutionary language, or toward the belief that ordinary politics had already been closed.
  • The resulting militancy confirmed the security diagnosis.

The monarchy became more capable and less reachable.

Oil revenue intensified the contradiction. The state could purchase arms, industry, expertise, infrastructure, prestige, and technological ambition at extraordinary scale. It could also outrun the institutions needed to distribute change, hear grievance, correct corruption, and let newly educated populations participate in deciding what modernization was for.

The Shah’s Iran therefore contained several truths that later propaganda would try to separate.

  • It was a United States ally and an Iranian state.
  • It modernized and repressed.
  • It expanded some women’s public and legal possibilities while remaining authoritarian.
  • It built universities and narrowed political speech.
  • It pursued nuclear technology and depended heavily on foreign technical systems.
  • It created a middle class, industrial workers, students, professionals, and administrators whose expectations exceeded the political routes available to them.
  • It confronted clerical reaction, secular opposition, nationalist memory, Marxist organization, market resentment, and ordinary anger produced by uneven change.

By the late 1970s, the monarchy’s security machine had preserved the state and damaged the state’s ability to know itself.

Foreign governments also misread the field through the order the machine displayed. A censored country can look stable to an ally because the ally receives fewer visible arguments. A security service can report that opposition is controlled because uncontrolled opposition has become harder to observe. A monarch can hear that the nation is modernizing while the places where people might safely answer have been closed.

The state mistook suppressed feedback for consent.

Then millions of people entered the street and corrected the report at once.

XI. The Revolution

The Iranian Revolution was plural before it became singular in memory.

Workers struck. Students organized. Clerics mobilized networks of mosques, seminaries, religious associations, mourning ceremonies, and trusted local communication. Bazaar merchants supplied resources and commercial pressure. Nationalists carried the memory of constitutional government and oil sovereignty. Marxists and other left organizations carried class analysis, anti-imperialism, underground experience, and competing revolutionary futures. Liberal religious thinkers sought forms of political Islam different from clerical rule. Women participated across ideological lines. Families entered mourning cycles that became new demonstrations. Provincial cities developed their own tempo of protest, repression, grief, and return.

  • These groups did not discover one shared constitution hiding beneath the Shah.
    • They discovered one shared obstruction.

That distinction shaped everything after victory.

Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini became the central revolutionary authority through a combination of religious standing, long opposition to the Shah, exile, political clarity, communication networks, symbolic force, and the ability to make different grievances legible inside one revolutionary answer.

He could name the monarchy as tyranny.

He could name foreign domination.

He could speak in the language of Islam, martyrdom, justice, independence, the oppressed, and national humiliation. His distance from Iran became a broadcasting position. Sermons, cassettes, messages, clerical networks, funerals, and political repetition carried a voice the monarchy could not silence without enlarging it.

Khomeini did not create every cause of the revolution.

He became the most powerful interpreter of what the revolution meant.

That is a different form of causal reach.

A coalition can overthrow a state while remaining unable to prevent one participant from becoming the authorized reader of the victory. The movement’s plurality survives long enough to open the political field. Then the struggle shifts from removing the old sovereign to determining who can speak for the transition.

The Shah left Iran in January 1979. Khomeini returned in February. The monarchy collapsed soon after. The country entered an interval in which many futures remained active at once:

  • parliamentary democracy;
  • an Islamic republic with elected institutions;
  • clerical guardianship;
  • liberal nationalism;
  • socialist transformation;
  • workers’ councils;
  • ethnic and regional autonomy;
  • revolutionary committees;
  • military counterrevolution;
  • restored authoritarianism;
  • civil war;
  • and combinations nobody had yet stabilized.

A revolution is often described as an event because events are easier to draw.

The field experienced a sequence.

Old institutions failed at different speeds. New institutions arrived with overlapping jurisdictions. Committees, courts, militias, ministries, mosques, parties, newspapers, councils, military units, local authorities, and charismatic offices all claimed pieces of the transition. The state was being rebuilt while the struggle over who had the right to rebuild it remained live.

This is where religious authority performed real integrative work.

It supplied shared language, moral seriousness, local organization, historical depth, discipline, and a way to carry the memory of those killed by the monarchy. It could bind neighborhoods, families, clergy, activists, merchants, and veterans of repression into a common political field. It could tell people that suffering had not been wasted and that foreign domination was not the horizon of Iranian life.

  • A sacred instrument expanded the field.
    • Then the instrument began deciding which other instruments were allowed to remain.

Secular newspapers, liberal nationalists, left organizations, religious dissenters, women’s groups, ethnic movements, competing clerics, former allies, and critics of the emerging constitutional order did not all disappear in one decree. Their paths narrowed through argument, street violence, institutional capture, emergency, war, bans, arrests, executions, candidate exclusion, and the growing claim that preserving the revolution required one authorized guardianship over its meaning.

The revolution’s great political question became:

How does a movement protect itself from the forces that would reverse, corrupt, purchase, invade, or hollow it out?

That question was real.

The answer became constitutional.

XII. Guardianship

The Constitution of the Islamic Republic does not hide the sacred source of the political order beneath neutral language.

It places the revolution, Islam, law, popular participation, clerical authority, anti-imperial independence, and the leadership of the jurist inside one constitutional machine.

This is important because the Islamic Republic is neither well described as a normal electoral republic with some religious decoration nor as a single cleric issuing every ordinary instruction to a passive state.

It is a layered order.

People vote for presidents, parliamentarians, local councils, and members of the Assembly of Experts. Elections produce real competition within boundaries, real policy differences, real constituencies, real disappointments, and real consequences. Presidents have mattered. Parliaments have mattered. Turnout has mattered. Factional conflict has mattered. Reformist, conservative, technocratic, populist, and hard-line projects have changed what the state does.

The electoral field operates beneath and through a guardianship field.

  • The Constitution gives the Leader power over the general policies of the state, command of the armed forces, declarations of war and peace, appointments across the military, judiciary, state broadcasting, and the Guardian Council, as well as other powers of supervision and arbitration.
    • The President is identified as the country’s highest official after the office of Leadership and exercises executive authority except in matters directly connected to Leadership.
  • The Guardian Council examines legislation for compatibility with Islam and the Constitution.
    • Six clerical jurists are selected by the Leader.
    • Six legal jurists are elected by parliament from nominees presented by the head of the judiciary, who is also appointed by the Leader.
      • The Council supervises national elections and referenda and interprets the Constitution.
  • The Assembly of Experts formally chooses and can remove the Leader.
    • Its own elections occur within the supervisory structure of the Guardian Council.

This is an architecture of guarded popular sovereignty.

  • The people authorize institutions.
    • The guardians authorize the acceptable routes through which authorization can operate.

The structure did not arise from one abstract desire to prevent voters from having fun. It answered a revolutionary fear.

  • Elections can be captured.
  • Legislatures can pass unjust law.
  • elites can return under new names.
  • Foreign powers can purchase institutions.
  • A republic can retain its shell while losing the revolution that made it.
  • A majority can be manipulated away from divine justice.
  • Administrators can turn liberation into ordinary corruption.

Guardianship proposes that a qualified religious-juridical authority must keep the political order answerable to a standard deeper than electoral appetite and harder for hostile power to purchase.

This is a serious sacred-instrument claim.

Every constitutional order protects some commitments from ordinary majorities. Rights, courts, federal arrangements, military subordination, amendment rules, independent agencies, and entrenched provisions all perform forms of guardianship. No durable state submits every foundation to a fifty-one-percent mood each morning.

The Iranian form differs in the location and reach of the guardian.

The sacred standard is institutionally interpreted through bodies whose membership and authority are deeply connected to the office they are meant to support, constrain, reproduce, and eventually replace. The correction loop repeatedly returns through the same guardianship network.

This creates an asymmetry.

  • An elected institution can fail the revolution.
    • The guardian can classify that failure.

When the guardian itself fails the field, correction must pass through institutions the guardian can shape, veto, supervise, command, or describe as threats to the revolution.

That does not make all correction impossible. Iranian political life has never been still enough for that claim. Elite factions resist one another. Elections produce surprises. Clerics disagree. Citizens organize. Institutions leak. Local authorities improvise. Governments negotiate against the preferences of security actors. Public pressure changes enforcement. The system bends, delays, selectively retreats, and sometimes opens.

The problem is the distribution of finality.

Who receives the last word when the evidence threatens the instrument itself?

The Constitution’s answer places extraordinary weight on guardianship.

Modal Path Ethics marks the point carefully.

A guardian may protect the door. A guardian may not become the only door through which reality is allowed to enter.

XIII. The State Protects the Revolution

The new order did not inherit one settled state and add a religious preamble.

It inherited a military shaped by the monarchy, revolutionary committees, armed organizations, political factions, local militias, separatist conflicts, institutional collapse, and the possibility that forces trained under the Shah could become the route back to the Shah’s world.

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps emerged in 1979 inside that problem.

Its constitutional role was stated directly:

Preserve the Revolution and its achievements.
  • A conventional army protects territory, state institutions, and the command order assigned to it.
  • A revolutionary guard protects a political transition’s authorized meaning.

The distinction there can narrow under invasion, when both forces defend the same border and population. It widens during domestic conflict, elections, protest, factional struggle, cultural change, and disputes over whether the existing order still embodies the revolution it claims to preserve.

The Guard therefore began with a doubled object:

  • Iran.
  • The Islamic Revolution governing Iran.
    • Those objects can support one another.
    • They can also diverge.

A citizen may oppose invasion and oppose the ruling institutions. A worker may demand wages without seeking foreign capture. A woman may reject compulsory dress enforcement while remaining attached to Iranian sovereignty. A cleric may dispute the doctrine or reach of guardianship. A veteran may believe the state has consumed the values for which the war was fought. A student may resist foreign coercion and domestic repression in the same sentence.

The revolutionary security instrument repeatedly faces a choice:

Is this person correcting the state, or attacking the revolution?

A guardian protected from correction has a strong incentive to choose the second answer.

Then the state does not only defend itself against enemies.

It manufactures enemies from the corrections it cannot absorb.

XIV. The Hostage Field

On November 4, 1979, Iranian students seized the United States embassy in Tehran.

Fifty-two American diplomats and citizens would remain captive for 444 days.

The event belonged to the active memory of 1953. The embassy was understood by the students who occupied it as a possible center of renewed intervention, espionage, counterrevolution, and another attempt to decide Iran’s government from inside a diplomatic compound. The Shah had been admitted to the United States for medical treatment. To many revolutionaries, the sequence looked less like humanitarian travel than the opening move of a restoration story they believed they had already read.

  • That fear was historically intelligible.
  • The seizure still captured actual people.

A diplomat is not a symbol of foreign interference with a pulse added for dramatic weight. A hostage is a human locus whose movement, time, body, family relation, work, sleep, fear, and future have been made available to another actor’s demand.

The students converted the possibility of American action into leverage over American persons.

The revolutionary leadership then converted the seizure into a political instrument.

Khomeini endorsed the action. Prime Minister Mehdi Bazargan’s provisional government resigned. Political forces that opposed or questioned the seizure lost ground. Documents recovered from the embassy were published and used to identify alleged networks of influence. The crisis became a test of revolutionary loyalty, an arena of anti-imperial mobilization, a tool in the constitutional struggle, and the event through which much of the United States would interpret the Islamic Republic for decades.

The embassy had contained intelligence activity.

States conduct intelligence through embassies.

The United States had participated in a prior coup.

Iranian suspicion did not come from nowhere.

The field still required distinctions.

  • Evidence of intelligence activity is not evidence that every contact is treason.
  • A government’s prior intervention does not transfer guilt onto every person within reach.
  • A foreign policy grievance does not grant title over a captive’s continuance.
  • Revolutionary necessity does not make indefinite confinement into diplomacy.

The hostage crisis strengthened a political grammar the Islamic Republic would keep using:

Contact with the outside can become penetration.

Sometimes, it is penetration.

The ethical and epistemic burden lies in preserving a route by which contact can be tested without treating isolation as proof of sovereignty.

The hostage field closed that route. It made capture feel like vigilance and made any call for release vulnerable to the charge that the revolution was being asked to disarm before the power that had violated Iran.

The United States answered with sanctions, a failed rescue attempt, military preparation, and a long political memory of humiliation.

Each side acquired a wound the other side could later cite as evidence that only pressure worked.

The relation became easier to harden than to repair.

Then Iraq crossed the border.

XV. The Imposed War

On September 22, 1980, Iraq invaded Iran.

The phrase Imposed War, common in the Islamic Republic’s memory of the conflict, names a real opening fact.

Saddam Hussein’s government initiated a major interstate attack against a country still reorganizing its army, purging old command structures, settling revolutionary conflicts, and recovering from the collapse of the monarchy. Iraqi forces crossed the border. Khorramshahr was devastated and occupied after fierce resistance. Iranian territory, towns, oil infrastructure, soldiers, and civilians entered a war chosen by the Iraqi state.

Iran’s defense was not manufactured by propaganda.

People defended their country.

The regular military, the Revolutionary Guard, the Basij, local forces, volunteers, conscripts, and civilians entered a field where the survival of the new republic and the territorial integrity of Iran were both at stake. Institutions that had distrusted one another had to fight together. Revolutionary organization acquired battlefield discipline. The Guard gained legitimacy through defense.

The war supplied the Islamic Republic with the most powerful possible confirmation of its founding security story:

The revolution was surrounded by forces willing to destroy it.

There were reasons for that conclusion.

Foreign governments supported Iraq in different forms and at different times. Gulf states financed Baghdad. Major powers feared revolutionary Iran, protected strategic interests, sold arms, shared intelligence, managed shipping, or tilted toward Iraq. The United States and Iran remained locked in hostility. The Soviet Union, France, and others had their own relationships with Iraq. The international response to aggression was slower and softer than Iranian memory could accept as justice.

Then Iraq used chemical weapons.

United Nations investigations confirmed large-scale Iraqi chemical-weapons use during the war. Sulfur mustard, tabun, and later sarin entered the battlefield. Chemical attacks struck Iranian forces and civilians, including the population of Sardasht. Survivors carried respiratory damage, eye injuries, skin damage, cancer risk, disability, trauma, family burden, and medical need long after the ceasefire made the war look finished on a calendar.

The chemical field taught another durable lesson:

  • International law could prohibit.
  • International institutions could investigate.
  • Foreign governments could still hesitate, balance, qualify, supply, or look elsewhere while Iranian bodies absorbed the evidence.

A state living inside that history has reason to distrust security promises made by states that did not stop the gas.

This reason does not authorize every later instrument built from the distrust.

The war itself changed after Iran recovered most occupied territory by 1982.

The initial defense had succeeded in reopening a path that the invasion tried to close. Iranian forces then carried the war into Iraq. The leadership pursued goals that included punishing the aggressor, removing Saddam Hussein, securing reparations, and preventing a future attack from ending in another temporary line.

The decision had a security logic.

It also extended the war for six more years.

This is where the clean national memory becomes difficult.

  • Iraq initiated the invasion.
  • Iran had a right to defend itself.
  • Iraq’s regime remained dangerous.
  • A ceasefire without accountability could leave the aggressor capable of returning.
  • Continuing the war beyond recovery of Iranian territory exposed additional generations to death, injury, chemical attack, economic depletion, and state mobilization.

A wound can create a real repair claim.

It does not automatically identify how long war remains repair.

The Islamic Republic increasingly organized politics, economy, culture, family life, and dissent through the emergency. Opposition could be classified as aid to the enemy. Shortage could become sacrifice. Institutional consolidation could become wartime necessity. The Guard expanded. The Basij expanded. Martyrdom entered public space with unmatched force. The dead became evidence that the state, revolution, nation, and sacred history had been joined through blood.

The war did not invent every authoritarian feature of the Islamic Republic. Repression of rivals and constitutional consolidation were already underway.

War made those processes harder to contest and easier to sanctify.

This is the Security Instruments lesson inside the Sacred Instruments track:

Protection can preserve the field and still become sovereign over the field it preserved.

Iran survived. The survival was real.

The surviving institutions learned that emergency could make them indispensable.

XVI. Martyrdom Enters Administration

Martyrdom did not begin with the Islamic Republic.

Shi’a memory carries Karbala: Husayn, injustice, refusal, sacrifice, mourning, and the obligation to remain answerable to a wound that political victory did not erase. Ritual remembrance can preserve contact with suffering against every state that would prefer the dead to become administratively quiet.

This is one of sacred memory’s greatest strengths.

It keeps power from owning the past.

The revolution and war placed that memory inside modern state administration.

Martyrs appeared in street names, murals, cemeteries, schoolbooks, television, sermons, public ceremonies, foundations, family benefits, recruitment, battlefield language, and the visual environment of ordinary cities. The state recognized families of the dead and injured. Sacrifice became a source of care, status, obligation, and legitimacy. Communities received forms for loss that could otherwise have become private ruin.

That work cannot be reduced to propaganda without throwing away the actual dead.

Many people died defending towns, comrades, families, territory, revolution, and country. Families did carry losses that the state had obligations to acknowledge. Religious language could make courage durable under conditions where ordinary strategic calculation offered little consolation.

The martyr could remain a witness against invasion and abandonment.

The instrument became dangerous where witness turned into command.

  • The dead had sacrificed, so the living must continue.
  • The family had lost a child, so questioning the war dishonored the child.
  • The revolution had been purchased in blood, so political revision became theft from the dead.
  • A leader stood with the martyrs, so opposition stood outside the sacred community.
  • More sacrifice became evidence that the path was true.

This is dead-command.

The dead may obligate memory.

They may not own future agency.

The distinction matters especially around the Basij. Volunteers entered the war through faith, patriotism, community pressure, revolutionary commitment, economic circumstance, youthful idealism, and the social field available to them.

The Basij cannot be rendered as one hypnotized mass or one heroic body.

It contained persons.

The state’s language often compressed them into sacrifice as a category.

A category does not feel cold, fear gas, write to a parent, carry an injured friend, distrust an officer, believe a sermon, regret volunteering, or choose to advance anyway.

Persons do.

Martyrdom remains a sacred instrument where it keeps those persons from becoming expendable numbers.

It becomes a consumption instrument where the sacred title of sacrifice makes expendability difficult to name.

The Islamic Republic emerged from the war with a vast archive of sacrifice and institutions authorized to interpret it.

The guard could now say:

We remember who died when the world abandoned Iran.

That sentence can protect a nation from easy dependence.

It can also close every argument about what the dead would have wanted next.

XVII. The Guards Become a State Within the State

The Revolutionary Guard survived the war as more than a military formation.

It had commanders, veterans, intelligence, logistics, engineering capacity, local networks, political prestige, martyrs, families, procurement experience, and a public claim to have protected the country when the revolutionary state was most vulnerable.

Postwar reconstruction gave these capacities new work.

Organizations associated with the Guard entered major construction, infrastructure, energy, telecommunications, transport, and industrial projects. Sanctions and restricted foreign access increased the value of institutions able to move goods, finance, technology, and personnel through difficult channels. Privatization and state contracting created further routes into the economy. The Guard’s external branch developed regional networks and military relationships. Missile forces became central to deterrence. Intelligence and internal-security roles deepened. Nuclear and missile sites acquired protection inside the same wider security field.

This is sometimes described by saying the Guard became a state within the state.

The phrase catches something real and can still mislead.

There was no clean civilian state on one side and one separate military parasite on the other. Political institutions, clerical offices, ministries, businesses, foundations, parliamentarians, veterans, contractors, security agencies, elected governments, and Guard-linked organizations interacted through alliances, rivalry, family networks, law, patronage, ideology, and shared dependence.

The Guard itself was not one mind. Generations, commands, business interests, ideological tendencies, professional specialties, regional experiences, and factional alignments differed.

The structural issue does not require a single mind.

It requires a concentration of capacities that make the guardian difficult to correct.

  • It protects strategic infrastructure.
  • It can classify information about the infrastructure.
  • It benefits institutionally from security priority.
  • It possesses force.
  • It influences the economy on which sanctions operate.
  • It participates in the regional relationships sanctions and threats are meant to change.
  • External attack strengthens the claim that these capacities must remain insulated.
  • Internal criticism can be described as weakening national defense in front of enemies who are visibly attacking.

The nuclear threshold would later sit inside this architecture.

That does not mean the Guard alone designed, operated, or politically controlled every element of the nuclear program. Scientists, civilian agencies, presidents, diplomats, the Atomic Energy Organization of Iran, the Supreme National Security Council, the Leader, military institutions, and industrial networks all mattered.

It means the threshold became protected by an order in which the protector had acquired broad standing to define what security required.

The program could therefore become more than technology.

It could become a test of whether Iran remained sovereign, whether the revolution remained alive, whether sacrifice had been honored, whether foreign pressure worked, and whether the guardian still deserved its place at the center.

A centrifuge can be turned off.

A civilizational credential is harder to negotiate.

XVIII. Woman, Life, Freedom

In September 2022, Jina Mahsa Amini died after being detained by Iran’s morality police.

Her death became a contact event.

The slogan that moved through Iran was Woman, Life, Freedom.

Each word pressed directly against the guardianship machine.

  • Woman named the people whose bodies had been treated as a visible surface of the state’s sacred order.
  • Life refused the conversion of political existence into permanent sacrifice, obedience, purity, or preparation for death.
  • Freedom asserted that the guarded revolution had become a field from which people needed release.

The protests spread across cities, universities, schools, workplaces, neighborhoods, ethnic regions, prisons, cemeteries, families, and digital networks. Women and girls removed head coverings in public, cut their hair, confronted security forces, organized, documented, mourned, sang, walked, struck, and refused the state’s account of what compulsory virtue was protecting.

Men participated. Workers participated. Students participated. Kurdish communities, Baluch communities, and other minorities carried their own histories of marginalization and state violence into the uprising. Families of those killed kept memory active. Iranian diaspora networks amplified testimony and sometimes introduced their own political struggles, simplifications, and demands into the field.

The state answered through force, arrest, prosecution, surveillance, intimidation, internet restriction, school pressure, executions, and renewed enforcement.

The United Nations fact-finding mission concluded that the repression involved serious human-rights violations and found reasonable grounds that some violations amounted to crimes against humanity, including persecution on gender and political grounds. Its work documented unlawful deaths, detention, torture, sexual and gender-based violence, enforced disappearance, and harms directed against children and protesters.

The phrase foreign-backed unrest could point toward real external attention, funding, messaging, influence operations, cyber activity, exile politics, and governments that openly desired regime change.

It could not perform the evidentiary work required to transform every Iranian participant into an instrument of those powers.

This is where 1953 returns as active information.

  • The state knows that foreign powers have interfered in Iran.
    • Many protesters know it too.
      • A person can reject foreign intervention and reject compulsory dress.
      • A woman can oppose bombing and oppose the morality police.
      • A student can distrust the United States and distrust the Revolutionary Guard.
      • A family can mourn a child killed in protest and a relative killed in war.
      • An Iranian can want the nuclear sites safe from attack and want inspectors admitted.
        • None of these positions is structurally incoherent.

The guardianship machine tends to make them difficult to hold together.

It offers a compressed choice:

  • defend the state as the vessel of sovereignty;
  • or weaken Iran before its enemies.

Woman, Life, Freedom refused the menu.

This is why the movement belongs inside the nuclear-threshold article.

The nuclear program is routinely presented as a national right and a sovereign defense against foreign domination. Those claims can be true in part. The nation whose right is invoked contains people whose political agency the guardianship order has narrowed in the name of protecting the nation.

The threshold therefore has an internal field.

  • Who decides what danger Iran should bear?
  • Who can question the costs of enrichment, sanctions, secrecy, proxy war, military hardening, or preventive attack?
  • Who receives the economic burden?
  • Who can inspect the inspector relation politically?
  • Who can say that a security instrument has stopped protecting life and started protecting its own authority?
    • A state may have a legitimate claim against external coercion.
    • Its citizens retain claims against internal coercion.
      • Neither claim erases the other.

The attacks of 2025 and 2026 made this distinction even more urgent. Iranian civilians faced armed hostilities from outside and intensified repression inside. The external threat did not suspend their standing. Domestic repression did not turn them into available material for foreign war.

This is the double field Modal Path Ethics refuses to flatten. The Iranian people are not the regime. The Iranian people are also not raw material for another state’s theory of liberation.

The sacred instrument proves its worth through the living continuance it protects.

A compulsory order that cannot receive the testimony of women living under it has begun treating its own sacred answer as more real than the field.

XIX. The Internal Threshold

The nuclear threshold is usually drawn outward.

How close is Iran to a weapon?
How close is Israel to attacking?
How close is the United States to joining?
How close is the region to proliferation, retaliation, or war?

The Islamic Republic contains another threshold inside itself.

It is the point at which protection from foreign capture becomes protection from domestic correction.

The transition does not occur all at once.

  • A real coup becomes a permanent suspicion architecture.
  • A real dictatorship produces a revolution.
  • A plural revolution becomes one authorized constitutional meaning.
  • A real invasion strengthens institutions built to guard that meaning.
  • Real martyrdom becomes administrative legitimacy.
  • Real foreign pressure strengthens economic and military guardians.
  • Real sabotage and attack make secrecy easier to defend.
  • Real protest enters the same security language as penetration.

Each step contains something true.

The generated structure can still become false to the field.

This is why the nuclear question cannot be separated from the political order carrying it. Verification is technical, but permission, access, secrecy, declarations, negotiation, and response to discrepancy all move through institutions shaped by revolutionary guardianship.

The state can treat inspection as a sovereign relation and still participate in it seriously.

The state can also treat technical correction as another route by which hostile powers seek access, humiliation, intelligence, delay, and eventual attack.

After attacks on inspected facilities, that fear acquires further evidence.

After safeguards failures and missing explanations, external suspicion acquires further evidence.

The internal and external guardians begin feeding each other.

  • Foreign threat strengthens the security state.
  • The security state’s opacity strengthens foreign threat perception.
  • Threat perception produces sanctions, sabotage, and attack.
  • Attack validates the security state’s warning.
  • The warning narrows domestic correction.
  • Narrowed correction makes the program harder to contest and the state harder to trust.

The threshold is no longer located only in uranium.

It is located in the point where every side’s protective instrument generates evidence for the other side’s protective instrument.

XX. The Guardian’s Ruling

The Islamic Republic did not invent Iran’s nuclear program.

It inherited an atom introduced through alliance with the United States, built for a monarchy that presented technological capacity as proof of modern statehood.

The revolution inherited something else.

It inherited the memory of foreign intervention, a monarchy strengthened after a coup, an authoritarian modernization project, and a political field whose peaceful correction had been narrowed until revolution became reachable.

The new order built guardianship against those failures.

It also built guardianship against the plurality that had made the revolution possible.

Then Iraq invaded.

Iranian defense was real. Chemical attack was real. International abandonment was real. Martyrdom carried real persons whose deaths obligated memory. The Revolutionary Guard preserved a state under threat and emerged with the capacity to make preservation one of the state’s organizing principles.

The guardian became load-bearing.

Load-bearing institutions are difficult to correct because every correction can be described as removing support from the whole structure.

By the time the nuclear program became a threshold, it no longer belonged only to engineers, reactors, uranium, and electrical demand.

It belonged to:

  • sovereignty after 1953;
  • independence after the Shah;
  • revolution after foreign domination;
  • survival after invasion;
  • memory after chemical attack;
  • sacred obligation after martyrdom;
  • and institutions whose authority grew through the act of guarding them all.

That history makes the program intelligible.

It does not make the program unanswerable.

A state can resist foreign capture and still capture correction inside itself.

A sacred instrument can preserve a wounded people and still become sovereign over the people it claims to preserve.

A guardian can protect the door.

It cannot become the only door through which reality is allowed to enter.

The Islamic Republic was built to prevent Iran from being captured again.

It eventually placed capture inside every serious correction.

The atom then entered the legal and technical field in which peaceful capacity became strategic threshold.

XXI. The Peaceful Atom

The treaty gave Iran a right and an obligation in the same breath.

That breath has been held for decades.

Iran joined the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons as a non-nuclear-weapon state. Under the treaty, it committed itself not to manufacture or acquire nuclear weapons or other nuclear explosive devices. It also accepted safeguards intended to verify that nuclear material remained in peaceful use.

The same treaty recognizes an inalienable right to research, produce, and use nuclear energy for peaceful purposes without discrimination, in conformity with the treaty’s non-proliferation obligations. It also commits the parties to facilitate peaceful nuclear cooperation.

Those provisions belong together.

They also contain a legal ambiguity that has carried astonishing political weight. The treaty recognizes peaceful nuclear development. It does not separately write the phrase an inalienable right to uranium enrichment into Article IV. Iran has long argued that domestic enrichment falls within the protected peaceful fuel cycle. Other governments have argued that no treaty party receives an unconditional right to any particular sensitive technology regardless of safeguards compliance, Security Council action, or proliferation risk.

That dispute cannot be solved by pretending the treaty says more plainly than it does. Iran has a protected peaceful-use claim. Enrichment can serve peaceful uses. The same activity can also create a rapid weapons option. The safeguards relation exists because the legal category and the physical machinery overlap.

  • The right does not erase safeguards.
  • Safeguards do not erase the right.

This should be simple enough to fit on a modest little plaque in Vienna. The political field has spent half a century trying to pry the sentences apart.

  • Iran emphasizes sovereign equality, technological development, research, medicine, electricity, scientific training, and the refusal of a small group of powerful states to decide which countries are mature enough to possess difficult technology.
  • Western governments emphasize the same technology’s capacity to shorten the route toward a weapon and the long history of Iranian declarations, undeclared activities, access disputes, and material the International Atomic Energy Agency has been unable to account for completely.
    • Both sides are touching the instrument.
    • Neither side gets to hold only the half that flatters it.

A peaceful nuclear program is a real category. Nuclear reactors produce electricity. Research reactors can support medicine, isotope production, material science, and training. A country does not become guilty by wanting a fuel cycle, engineers, reactors, or technical independence.

A peaceful-use claim is also a claim about future conduct. The same enrichment infrastructure can produce material at different concentrations. The same technical workforce can continue learning. The same industrial chain can be expanded, dispersed, hardened, or repurposed. The same stockpile can remain under inspection, be diluted, be fabricated into fuel, be moved, be further enriched, or enter an unknown interval.

The word peaceful therefore cannot do the work of an inspection.

It names an intended and legally required use. Verification establishes contact with the material field strongly enough to support that description.

This distinction is not hostile to sovereignty.

This is how sovereignty enters a cooperative instrument without asking every other state to trust its self-description.

The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty was built around an unequal bargain. Five states were recognized as nuclear-weapon states under the treaty’s historical definition. Other parties renounced acquisition. Peaceful nuclear cooperation and safeguards were placed beside a disarmament commitment whose incomplete fulfillment has damaged the treaty’s legitimacy for generations.

Iran can point to this inequality without inventing it.

  • Nuclear-weapon states retain vast arsenals.
  • Modernization continues.
  • Disarmament advances slowly or reverses.
  • Security guarantees remain uneven.
  • States outside the treaty possess nuclear weapons.
  • A non-nuclear state may be attacked while nuclear possession appears to protect others from comparable correction.

That field teaches a dangerous lesson.

It teaches that abstention is a rule for the exposed while possession is an exception for the powerful.

Article VI adds another live obligation. Every party undertook to pursue negotiations in good faith on ending the nuclear arms race and on nuclear disarmament. That language did not promise instant abolition.

It did prevent the treaty from becoming a permanent caste system with one side inspected forever and the other side indefinitely entitled to threaten the planet.

The weak performance of the nuclear-weapon states under that obligation is therefore not atmospheric hypocrisy. It acts directly on the treaty’s durability.

A non-nuclear state is easier to hold inside restraint when restraint belongs to a shared direction rather than a fixed subordinate status.

The conclusion does not follow automatically. An unequal instrument can still block catastrophic proliferation. A broken promise by nuclear powers does not transmute proliferation into repair. It does, however, increase the political resistance surrounding every demand that a non-nuclear state remain permanently non-nuclear.

Iran’s program entered that resistance.

The atom could become energy, medicine, national prestige, scientific capacity, industrial independence, bargaining power, strategic insurance, or a path toward a weapon.

The machinery did not choose among those futures.

The field around the machinery did.

XXII. Enrichment

Natural uranium is mostly uranium-238. Only a small fraction is uranium-235, the isotope commonly used to sustain the chain reaction required by many reactor designs and, at much higher concentration, to make a uranium weapon possible.

Enrichment increases the proportion of uranium-235.

The process sounds clean in one sentence because engineering has done the reader the favor of hiding several thousand arguments inside the centrifuge hall.

  • Uranium hexafluoride gas enters rapidly spinning centrifuges.
    • The slight mass difference between uranium isotopes allows repeated stages to produce streams with different concentrations of uranium-235.
      • Centrifuges are linked into cascades.
        • Material can pass through multiple stages.
          • The output from one arrangement can become the feed for another.
            • The percentage matters.
            • The amount matters.
            • The starting concentration matters.
            • The number and efficiency of the centrifuges matter.
            • The arrangement of cascades matters.
            • The chemical form matters.
            • The time available matters.
            • The monitoring relation matters.

Most commercial power-reactor fuel uses uranium enriched below five percent uranium-235. Uranium enriched above twenty percent is generally categorized as highly enriched. Material enriched to sixty percent remains below the concentration usually associated with weapons-grade uranium, while a large part of the separative work needed to move from natural uranium toward very high enrichment has already occurred.

This is why public argument handles percentages so badly.

  • A listener hears that sixty is closer to ninety than to five and concludes that the weapon is almost sitting on the table.
  • Another listener hears that sixty is below weapons grade and concludes that the concern is propaganda.

The centrifuge declines both invitations.

Enrichment is nonlinear in strategic significance. The early climb from natural uranium through low enrichment requires a great deal of work. Material already enriched to sixty percent can be further enriched much faster than the original feed could be brought from natural levels to the same point, assuming the necessary facilities, equipment, configuration, and decision.

That still leaves several distinct transitions.

  • The material must be enriched further if a weapons-grade route is selected.
  • It must be converted into a form suitable for a device.
  • A functioning explosive design must exist.
  • Components must be manufactured and integrated.
  • The device must be reliable enough for the state to treat it as usable.
  • Delivery, command, custody, safety, doctrine, and political authorization must be arranged.
    • A stockpile is not a warhead.
    • A centrifuge cascade is not a command authority.
      • A short material-production path is still a serious change in the field.

The danger is not created by pretending all of these steps are identical. The danger is created when enough of them become reachable quickly, opaquely, and under conditions where every actor assumes delay may be fatal.

Iran’s enrichment capacity therefore had two political lives.

  • Inside Iran, it could stand for scientific independence, sovereign equality, resistance to technological exclusion, and proof that pressure had failed to erase national capacity.
  • Outside Iran, it could stand for shortened warning, uncertain intention, dispersible capability, and the possibility that a political decision might outrun diplomacy.

The facility architecture matters too. A large visible enrichment plant can be watched, inspected, and attacked. A dispersed set of smaller cascades can be harder to locate and easier to reconstruct. A deeply buried facility can survive conventional attack while convincing adversaries that only more extreme weapons can reach it. Hardened infrastructure protects a peaceful program from war and protects a weapons option from prevention using the same concrete.

The engineering does not reveal the final intention. It changes which intentions remain executable under pressure.

The centrifuge hall became a national symbol and a foreign countdown at the same time. That is how technical infrastructure enters a threshold.

XXIII. The Undeclared Places

  • Iran’s strongest legal argument begins with a treaty right.
  • Its strongest safeguards problem begins with places it did not declare.

The International Atomic Energy Agency spent years seeking technically credible explanations for uranium particles and nuclear-related activity connected to locations outside Iran’s declarations. By 2025, the Agency concluded that Iran had failed to declare nuclear material and nuclear-related activities at three undeclared locations and that those locations formed part of an undeclared structured program carried out until the early 2000s.

The names changed slightly across the long documentary path as individual questions were resolved, reframed, or separated.

The surviving problem did not.

  • The Agency found man-made uranium particles where Iran’s declared nuclear history said they should not have been.
    • It asked how the particles arrived, what activities had taken place, where associated material or contaminated equipment had gone, and whether anything remained outside safeguards.
  • Iran offered explanations, challenged information sources, alleged hostile fabrication and sabotage, disputed the Agency’s reasoning, and insisted that it had declared all nuclear material and activities required under its safeguards agreement.
    • The Agency found the answers technically insufficient.

This is the exact type of dispute in which every side is tempted to leave the instrument and enter a story.

Iran’s story says hostile intelligence services, political pressure, forged claims, assassination, sabotage, sanctions, and selective enforcement have turned safeguards into a route for accusation. The Agency’s story, at its best, is narrower: particles were detected; the declarations did not account for them; explanations failed technical review; the material history remained incomplete.

The narrower story should govern.

A safeguards dispute is not resolved by deciding which government has the more emotionally persuasive history.

It is resolved when the material account closes.

The dispute also belongs to Iran’s comprehensive safeguards agreement rather than only to the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action. That distinction matters when the political agreement later collapses. A political agreement can expire, collapse, or be abandoned. The underlying safeguards obligations continue while Iran remains a party to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty and its safeguards agreement remains in force.

This is why undeclared locations matter even when the associated activities are old.

Nuclear material is not absolved by age. A past undeclared program can leave material, contaminated equipment, records, technical networks, and unexplained movements behind it. The International Atomic Energy Agency does not need to prove that a current weapon program is running at the location in order for the missing account to remain serious.

  • The Agency stated that it had no credible indication of an ongoing undeclared structured program of the earlier type.
    • That is an important restraint on the allegation.
  • It also stated that it could not determine whether related nuclear material had been consumed, mixed with declared material, or remained outside safeguards.
    • That is an important restraint on reassurance.

Both findings belong in the same sentence.

The safeguards problem therefore sits in a difficult region between two propaganda machines.

  • One machine converts unresolved material into proof of an active bomb project.
  • The other converts the absence of a proven current project into proof that the material account no longer matters.

Modal Path Ethics leaves both machines running unattended in the hallway where they can argue with a plant.

The field requires a complete declaration, technically credible explanation, access where required, and a material balance the Agency can defend.

The reason is larger than Iran.

Safeguards work because a state cannot answer every discrepancy by saying the inspector is politically motivated, and an inspector cannot answer every ambiguity by promoting suspicion into guilt. The institution survives through narrower contact:

  • sample;
  • record;
  • location;
  • equipment;
  • declaration;
  • explanation;
  • return.

Every unresolved location raises resistance against the next agreement. Every refusal makes later cooperation look naive. Every politicized allegation makes legitimate inspection easier to dismiss.

The undeclared places became small rooms carrying the weight of the whole relationship.

XXIV. The Fatwa

The Supreme Leader of Iran has repeatedly stated that the production and use of nuclear weapons are prohibited under Islamic law.

This declaration should be taken seriously.

That sentence will upset people who believe seriousness means either obedience or ridicule. They have survived worse.

A religious prohibition can shape elite discourse, political legitimacy, institutional permission, military doctrine, and the range of actions a state can publicly justify.

In a political system where the Supreme Leader retains final authority over major security decisions, a stated prohibition against nuclear weapons is not decorative by definition.

The International Atomic Energy Agency itself noted statements by Iran’s highest officials that nuclear weapons are incompatible with Islamic law. The 2025 United States intelligence assessment likewise judged that Iran was not building a nuclear weapon and that the Supreme Leader had not reauthorized the weapons program the assessment said had been suspended in 2003, while warning that pressure and public advocacy for weapons were increasing.

The fatwa therefore belongs inside the evidence field.

It does not close the evidence field.

A sacred prohibition can constrain an authorized decision while enrichment, stockpiling, missile development, research, political pressure, and technical learning continue below the line the prohibition draws. A future leader can interpret the prohibition differently. A state can preserve threshold capability while refusing weapon construction. Institutions can prepare options they are not currently authorized to complete. Public theology can remain stable while strategic doctrine changes around it.

The public status of the prohibition has itself produced argument. Iranian officials describe a binding religious judgment expressed repeatedly by the Supreme Leader. Outside analysts debate its exact juridical form, whether a single written instrument exists in the form foreigners expect, and how readily a ruling could be reinterpreted under an emergency doctrine. That debate can become another lazy exit. A fatwa does not become unreal because it lacks the bureaucratic shape preferred by a foreign ministry. Its practical force still depends on the institutions that carry, interpret, and obey it.

The religious claim has another vulnerability.

It is difficult for external actors to verify.
  • An inspector can sample uranium.
  • An inspector cannot sample a fatwa for hidden isotopes of future exception.

This does not make the sacred claim worthless. It assigns the claim its proper role.

The prohibition is an internal moral instrument. It can make a weapon decision harder to authorize. It can give Iranian officials, clerics, citizens, and institutions a standard against which weapon acquisition would have to answer. It can preserve a non-weapons identity within the state’s own sacred language.

Safeguards are an external evidentiary instrument. They make the material field available to actors who do not share the theology and are not positioned to trust the leadership.

Both instruments can cooperate.

  • The prohibition says the weapon must not be built.
  • Verification shows whether the material remains inside peaceful activity.
    • Political contestability tests whether the prohibition can be questioned, interpreted, or manipulated.
    • Technical transparency reduces the burden placed on sacred assurance.

The danger begins when one instrument demands the throne.

  • A foreign government that treats the fatwa as meaningless because it is religious throws away a possible restraint.
  • An Iranian government that treats the fatwa as sufficient proof asks the region to accept sacred authority in place of material contact.

Modal Path Ethics recognizes the instrument and refuses the substitution.

A sacred prohibition can guide the state.

It cannot replace inspection.

XXV. The Three Thresholds

The phrase nuclear threshold can hide several different crossings.

This article needs three.

1. The material threshold

A state possesses enough suitable nuclear material, enrichment capacity, and associated infrastructure that producing weapons-grade material could become possible within a strategically short period.

2. The weapon threshold

The state has completed, or can rapidly complete, the additional design, conversion, manufacturing, integration, and testing work required for a functioning nuclear explosive device.

3. The strategic threshold

Other actors begin behaving as though acquisition is plausible enough to alter military planning, alliances, sanctions, diplomacy, intelligence, and escalation.

Iran crossed deeply into the third threshold without publicly crossing the second.

That distinction explains why the nuclear argument can look insane from every direction.

  • One side points to the absence of an assembled Iranian bomb.
  • The other points to stockpiles, centrifuges, missile capacity, prior weapon-relevant work, unresolved safeguards issues, and shortened material-production time.
    • Both sides are answering different thresholds.

The strategic threshold begins acting before possession because other states cannot wait for certainty at the moment certainty would arrive too late for their preferred response.

That is the structural force of latency.

A latent capability is not empty possibility. It is technical possibility carried by extant institutions, trained personnel, material, facilities, and political history. It can be delayed, constrained, inspected, bombed, hidden, negotiated, expanded, or converted into an actual arsenal.

Threshold status is therefore relational. A state does not become a threshold state only by reaching one universally agreed technical line. It becomes one when its extant capacity and the surrounding field begin interacting as if conversion could occur within the decision horizon of other actors.

  • A month can be strategically short for one government and politically usable for another.
  • A year can be an eternity in an election and an instant in reactor construction.

The threshold becomes dangerous when the field loses the ability to distinguish those movements in time.

XXVI. Sixty Percent

Iran became the only non-nuclear-weapon state producing and accumulating uranium enriched to sixty percent uranium-235.

The International Atomic Energy Agency said so plainly and described the situation as a serious proliferation concern.

Iran did not arrive at sixty percent because a power reactor requested unusually ambitious fuel.

The enrichment escalated through the collapse of the 2015 agreement, reciprocal pressure, attacks, sanctions, monitoring loss, and Iran’s calibrated movement beyond prior limits. Iran presented the increase as a response to hostile action and failed commitments. Outside governments saw material approaching weapons grade without a persuasive civilian requirement.

The two descriptions can coexist.

A transition can be retaliatory and dangerous.

Sixty percent is not a magical moral number. Matter does not become evil when a gauge crosses it.

The number matters because it changes the remaining path.

Most of the separative work required to move from natural uranium to very high enrichment has already been invested by the time material reaches sixty percent. With suitable centrifuge capacity and configuration, further enrichment can occur rapidly compared with the original climb from natural uranium.

The exact breakout estimate depends on assumptions:

  • how much material is available;
  • its chemical form;
  • centrifuge type and performance;
  • cascade configuration;
  • feed and tails assumptions;
  • facility condition;
  • whether all relevant activity is visible;
  • whether the state wants enough material for one device or several;
  • and what losses occur during conversion and production.

This is why a public countdown should never be mistaken for a clock mounted on a wall in Fordow.

Breakout estimates are models of a material-production path.

They are still useful. They tell decision-makers how quickly the first threshold could be crossed under stated assumptions.

They do not tell us whether the political decision has been made, whether a device design is ready, whether integration will work, or when a deployable arsenal would exist.

The 2025 United States intelligence assessment is important here. It said Iran was not building a nuclear weapon and that the Supreme Leader had not reauthorized the earlier weapons program. The same assessment warned that public discussion of weapons had become less taboo and that advocates within the decision-making apparatus had gained confidence.

The material path shortened while the political prohibition weakened at the edges.

That combination is the threshold.

  • The strongest alarmist account compresses enrichment, weaponization, and deployment into one act.
  • The strongest reassuring account separates them so completely that shortened material time appears strategically irrelevant.

The field joins them without collapsing them.

  • A weapon decision remains distinct.
  • The time available after that decision matters.
  • Verification may detect preparation.
  • Verification may arrive too late if access is weak and the material path is short.
  • Military attack may delay facilities while increasing motive and opacity.
  • Diplomatic limits may lengthen the path while leaving knowledge intact.

The percentage is therefore not the bomb.

It is resistance removed from one route toward the bomb.

XXVII. Threshold Deterrence

A state can receive deterrent value from a weapon it has not built.

This sounds like getting interest on money that does not exist, which is ordinarily called finance.

In nuclear strategy, the underlying structure is latency.

If adversaries believe Iran could move toward a weapon quickly, disperse or rebuild capacity, impose regional costs through missiles and partners, and survive the first attempt to destroy the program, then uncertainty enters their planning.

The uncertainty can discourage some actions. It can also accelerate them.

Threshold deterrence works through a dangerous double effect:

  • The capability raises the cost of attacking Iran because attack may trigger rapid weaponization, regional retaliation, or an uncontrollable race.
  • The capability raises the incentive to attack before Iran crosses a line that would make later attack far more dangerous.

The balance depends on what adversaries think the threshold is for. A stable hedge may persuade them that Iran wants insurance and bargaining power. A clandestine sprint may persuade them that delay grants the state an irreversible advantage. Since intention cannot be inspected directly, the material account, access rules, public doctrine, intelligence record, and regional behavior all become proxies for the unseen decision.

Those proxies can conflict. A state may cooperate at declared facilities while obstructing older safeguards questions. It may retain a sacred prohibition while expanding material capability. It may describe enrichment as insurance while supporting forces that make adversaries expect future coercion. The deterrent signal and the proliferation signal can be emitted by the same transition.

The same instrument deters and invites prevention.

That is why the threshold does not provide the stable protection its defenders sometimes imagine. It can create strategic insurance while generating a permanent preemption clock around the insured state.

Iran also gains bargaining power from the threshold. Enrichment can be capped, diluted, exported, frozen, inspected, or expanded in exchange for sanctions relief and security concessions. The reversibility gives the state something to trade.

Then the instrument begins eating the agreement.

  • If every concession requires restoring a capability first, escalation becomes the admission ticket to negotiation.
  • If every attack proves the need for greater deterrence, the threshold begins arguing for its own conversion into possession.

Latency can remain a hedge.

It can also become a machine for teaching every side that the next step is safer than restraint.

XXVIII. Israel’s Opaque Arsenal

The regional field contains a nuclear state that does not officially present itself as one.

Israel is not a party to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. Its nuclear activities are not subject to the comprehensive safeguards required of non-nuclear-weapon states inside the treaty. Israel maintains a long-standing policy of nuclear opacity, refusing public confirmation or denial while preserving a widely understood deterrent.

The International Atomic Energy Agency states the treaty difference with institutional restraint: every state in the Middle East except Israel belongs to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, while Israel has item-specific safeguards rather than comprehensive safeguards covering all nuclear activities.

That asymmetry is central to Iran’s security argument.

It is also commonly handled with one of two bad instruments.

  • The first excludes Israel’s nuclear status from the Iran discussion, as though Iranian enrichment developed in a regional room where the strongest military power had politely left its strategic capabilities outside.
  • The second invokes Israel’s opacity as a solvent that dissolves every Iranian safeguards obligation.
    • Neither works.

Israel’s arsenal does not authorize Iran to violate its treaty commitments.

Iran’s treaty commitments do not make Israel’s arsenal irrelevant to Iranian threat perception.

A regional order that demands permanent nuclear abstinence from Iran while treating Israeli opacity as an unchangeable fact asks one state to understand non-proliferation as obligation and the other as discretion.

This is politically unstable even when the distinction has a legal explanation.

Israel did not join the treaty as a non-nuclear-weapon state. Iran did. The treaty obligations differ.

The security effects do not stop at the legal border.

Iranian leaders observe a nuclear-armed adversary with advanced conventional forces, close relations with the United States, a history of preventive attack, and no comprehensive regional safeguards obligation. Israeli leaders observe an Iranian state that has called for Israel’s disappearance in varying formulations, supports armed groups fighting Israel, accumulates highly enriched uranium, develops missiles, and leaves safeguards questions unresolved.

Each state can build an internally coherent fear from the other.

The coherent fears do not produce a coherent region.

That is why the eventual repair horizon has to reach beyond the Iranian program toward a regional weapons-of-mass-destruction-free architecture, reciprocal security commitments, and safeguards capable of applying to the whole field.

The asymmetry also pressures other regional states.

Saudi officials have periodically warned that a nuclear-armed Iran could force a matching response. The United Arab Emirates built a civilian nuclear-energy program around a renunciation of domestic enrichment and reprocessing, presenting one model of fuel-cycle restraint. Türkiye, Egypt, and other states possess their own industrial ambitions and security concerns.

Iran’s threshold therefore does not end at Iran. It changes the value other governments assign to latent capacity.

A proliferation cascade need not begin with five governments ordering bombs on the same Tuesday. It can begin with fuel-cycle programs, hedging doctrines, research institutions, missile investments, and the quiet conclusion that the regional rules may not survive the next crisis.

The horizon may remain distant.

The asymmetry remains present while everyone waits.

XXIX. The Gulf and the American Umbrella

Iran does not face Israel alone.

  • The United States maintains forces, bases, access agreements, naval power, air power, intelligence partnerships, and missile-defense relationships across the region.
    • Gulf monarchies purchase advanced American weapons and rely in different degrees on United States protection while pursuing their own relations with Iran, China, Russia, and one another.
  • Pakistan, east of Iran, possesses nuclear weapons.
  • Russia has supplied diplomatic support, military cooperation, and nuclear assistance while remaining its own strategic actor.
  • The Persian Gulf contains energy infrastructure whose disruption can move prices and politics around the world before most people have found the Strait of Hormuz on a map.

This is the environment in which Iran asks what non-nuclear security is supposed to mean.

American officials describe the posture as defense of partners, forces, shipping, and regional stability. Iran experiences much of the same posture as encirclement, intervention capacity, and evidence that hostile military force can be assembled near its borders under another state’s definition of order.

Gulf states experience Iranian missiles, drones, naval pressure, armed partners, and revolutionary rhetoric as reasons the American umbrella remains necessary.

The Gulf states are not passive clients inside this structure. Oman has repeatedly served as an intermediary. Qatar balances a major United States military presence with working relations across regional divides. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates have pursued both deterrence and diplomatic accommodation. Bahrain, Kuwait, and Iraq carry different domestic and geographic exposures.

Their choices cannot be reduced to a single American command.

Still, the United States supplies a large part of the military ceiling under which those choices occur. Carrier groups, aircraft, bases, missile defense, intelligence, arms sales, and explicit commitments to Israel make American power part of every Iranian estimate of escalation.

This umbrella generates rain.

  • United States presence reassures partners and threatens Iran.
  • Iranian response reassures the revolutionary state and threatens partners.
  • Partner procurement expands.
  • Missile defense expands.
  • Proxy and direct attacks expand.
  • Every defensive system becomes evidence for another defensive system.

The threshold enters this architecture as one more attempt to make attack expensive.

It also gives the architecture its organizing enemy.

XXX. Iran’s Strongest Defense

Iran’s case has to be stated in a form strong enough to survive outside Iranian state media.

The country experienced foreign intervention in 1953, followed by a monarchy whose security institutions were supported by Western allies. It endured an Iraqi invasion, mass casualties, attacks on cities, and chemical weapons while powerful states armed, financed, or politically protected Iraq. It faces an Israeli state with nuclear opacity, overwhelming conventional capability, and a record of preventive operations. Iranian nuclear scientists have been assassinated. Nuclear facilities have been sabotaged and attacked. Sanctions have targeted the economy across political administrations. The United States left the 2015 nuclear agreement after the International Atomic Energy Agency had repeatedly verified Iranian implementation of its nuclear commitments during the agreement’s early period.

Then the United States restored maximum pressure.

From inside this history, the nuclear program can be understood as several things at once:

  • a right promised by the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty;
  • a national scientific project;
  • proof that external powers cannot permanently assign technological dependency;
  • bargaining leverage against sanctions;
  • strategic latency beneath the conventional superiority of adversaries;
  • and insurance against regime-change war.

Iran can also point to the global nuclear order.

Nuclear-weapon states have not disarmed on the schedule implied by the treaty’s moral bargain. Israel remains outside the treaty. Pakistan and India possess nuclear weapons outside it. North Korea left and built an arsenal. Ukraine surrendered inherited nuclear weapons and later suffered invasion by a state that had given security assurances. Iraq and Libya remain cautionary stories in the minds of governments that fear disarmament can make coercion easier.

The examples are imperfect, and states use them selectively. Ukraine did not surrender an independently operated, mature national arsenal in the simple form later rhetoric suggests. Libya’s nuclear history and 2011 war cannot be reduced to one disarmament decision. Iraq did not possess the weapon it was accused of pursuing when the United States invaded in 2003. North Korea’s survival has causes beyond its arsenal.

The simplified lesson can still act even when the historical comparison is rough. Governments do not need a graduate seminar’s approval before absorbing a pattern into doctrine.

The lesson offered by the field is brutal:

Nuclear possession appears to preserve regimes that conventional power might otherwise destroy.

Iran does not have to endorse that lesson for the lesson to act on Iranian decision-makers.

The program’s defenders can therefore argue that complete surrender of enrichment would exchange extant capacity for promises already shown to be reversible. They can argue that external fuel dependence creates a pressure point. They can argue that inspections have been politicized, intelligence has entered the safeguards field, and declared facilities have become target folders. They can argue that every concession has produced another demand while every hostile act has strengthened the officials who warned against concession.

This is a serious defense.

It explains why the threshold is attractive.

It does not make the threshold safe.

The defense contains its own trap.

  • Every reason Iran has to preserve strategic ambiguity is a reason neighboring states have to fear the preserved ambiguity.
  • Every attack makes Iranian opacity easier to justify.
    • Every opacity makes another attack easier to sell.
  • Every failed promise raises the value of sovereign capability.
    • Every increase in capability raises the perceived cost of waiting.

A security instrument can be rational for the actor carrying it and catastrophic for the field generated around it.

That is the threshold’s strength.

That is also its failure.

XXXI. The Threshold’s Strongest Indictment

Iran’s strongest defense cannot answer several facts by repeating the word sovereignty.

The safeguards agreement is a legal obligation Iran accepted. The International Atomic Energy Agency concluded that Iran failed to declare nuclear material and nuclear-related activities at undeclared locations. It found explanations technically insufficient and could not determine whether associated material remained outside safeguards.

Iran accumulated uranium enriched to sixty percent without a persuasive civilian need commensurate with the scale and form of the stockpile. It reduced monitoring and stopped provisional application of the Additional Protocol after the broader agreement collapsed. It withdrew the designation of experienced inspectors. It delayed or restricted access. It treated portions of the verification relationship as leverage.

The state developed a broad missile force and embedded the nuclear threshold inside a regional strategy capable of imposing costs through allied armed organizations, direct missile and drone attacks, pressure on shipping, and threats against neighboring infrastructure.

Iranian officials have maintained a religious prohibition against nuclear weapons. Former officials and public figures have also spoken increasingly about possessing the technical capability to build them. The 2025 United States intelligence assessment said the state was not building a weapon while warning that the taboo around public advocacy had eroded.

This is not the behavior of a field that can be stabilized by reassurance alone.

The regional record adds another charge. Iranian support has strengthened armed organizations that possess their own local agency while also serving Tehran’s capacity to impose costs beyond its borders. Missile and drone transfers, training, finance, and political coordination allow Iran to distribute deterrence through civilian fields that Iran does not govern and cannot fully control. The regional network becomes decisive once the threshold goes to war. Here it is enough to mark the consequence: threshold deterrence is larger than the uranium stockpile.

The internal political order adds further resistance.

A program defended as the achievement of the Iranian people is governed through institutions that severely restrict those people’s ability to contest national-security policy. Journalists, women, students, workers, minorities, reformists, dissidents, and families cannot all safely enter the decision process that claims to act in their name. The Revolutionary Guard participates in military, political, economic, intelligence, missile, and regional policy. The same institution positioned to defend the threshold also benefits from the security environment the threshold helps sustain.

That concentration matters.

  • A state can be threatened and still become dangerous.
  • A population can be wronged by foreign powers and still be burdened by its own government.
  • A treaty can be unequal and still impose valid obligations.
  • A sacred prohibition can be sincere and still require inspection.
  • A peaceful program can exist beside a strategic hedge.

The indictment is therefore wider than a secret bomb accusation.

Iran built a field in which the material path shortened, the evidence relation weakened, the regional retaliation network expanded, and internal correction narrowed. It did not need to assemble a weapon for this combination to become strategically coercive.

The threshold already forced other people to plan around futures Iran said it had not chosen.

That is a form of power.

It is also a burden placed on the whole region.

XXXII. The Threshold Acts

Iran’s nuclear program entered history as a peaceful technology under the Shah and survived the revolution as a different sovereign instrument.

The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty gave Iran a right to peaceful nuclear development and placed that right inside safeguards and non-acquisition obligations. The wider treaty order promised disarmament and delivered too little of it. Israel remained outside the treaty with an opaque arsenal. American military power remained across the region. Iranian memories of coup, invasion, chemical attack, sabotage, assassination, sanctions, and abandoned agreements made technological dependence look like strategic exposure.

Iran had reasons to want the threshold.

The threshold gave every adversary reasons to attack it.

Enrichment made the material path shorter. Undeclared locations made reassurance harder. The fatwa preserved an internal prohibition without supplying an external material account. Sixty-percent uranium remained distinct from a weapon while removing resistance from one route toward one. Missiles, regional partners, and uncertainty gave latency deterrent value before possession.

The program became powerful in the interval between two sentences:

Iran has not built the bomb.

Iran could move closer faster than the field can safely ignore.

That interval reorganized sanctions, intelligence, diplomacy, alliance planning, military posture, and public fear.

The nuclear threshold had begun acting.

The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action tried to make the threshold visible, limited, and slow enough for politics to return.

XXXIII. The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action

The agreement did not ask Iran to become trustworthy.

It asked uranium to remain countable.

This was a much better idea.

Trust is useful in ordinary human life. It can lower resistance, shorten negotiation, and make cooperation possible before every action has been independently verified by a committee carrying three clipboards and a very serious little seal.

Trust is also a terrible foundation for a nuclear settlement between states that have spent decades sabotaging, sanctioning, threatening, deceiving, detaining, surrounding, and interpreting one another through the worst available memories.

  • Iran did not trust the United States.
  • The United States did not trust Iran.
    • Israel did not trust the agreement to survive Iran’s intentions.
    • Iran did not trust the agreement to survive an American election.
  • European governments trusted the agreement more than their firms trusted exemption from United States sanctions.
  • Russia and China trusted the agreement partly because it constrained American freedom of action while preserving their own relations with Tehran.

Everybody had reasons.

The uranium still needed somewhere to go.

That is the level at which the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action became serious. It was signed in July 2015 by Iran, the European Union, China, France, Germany, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States after a long negotiation that had already passed through interim limits, secret contacts, public threats, failed proposals, sanctions escalation, and enough diplomatic choreography to give Vienna permanent lower-back pain.

The agreement did not settle whether Iran was morally entitled to mistrust the West, whether the United States was strategically entitled to mistrust Iran, whether Israel’s nuclear opacity was acceptable, whether the Gulf security order was coherent, whether sanctions had been just, whether the Islamic Republic represented the Iranian people, or whether the entire Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty had been faithfully honored by the nuclear-weapon states.

It took one narrower relation and made it technical.

  • Iran would accept limits on enrichment, stockpile, centrifuges, reactor design, reprocessing, procurement, and certain weaponization-relevant activities.
  • The International Atomic Energy Agency would verify those limits through declarations, access, monitoring, seals, records, sampling, and repeated return.
  • The United Nations, European Union, and United States would lift or suspend specified nuclear-related sanctions as the verified steps were completed.
  • A Joint Commission would receive implementation disputes.
  • A Security Council mechanism would preserve a route back to sanctions if significant non-performance could not be resolved.

The relation was conditional from the beginning.

Iran did not receive sanctions relief because the negotiating states had finally discovered an agreeable Persian soul beneath the centrifuge hall.

The other parties did not receive nuclear restraint because Tehran had suddenly accepted their account of regional order.

Action answered action.

Measurement answered declaration.

Relief answered verified restriction.

This was the agreement’s moral intelligence.

It declined the invitation to make friendship a precondition for inspection.

On January 16, 2016, the International Atomic Energy Agency reported that Iran had completed the nuclear-related measures required for Implementation Day. Sanctions actions followed. Centrifuges had been removed from operation. Enriched uranium stockpiles had been reduced. The core of the unfinished heavy-water reactor at Arak had been made unusable and prepared for redesign. Fordow had been reassigned under agreed restrictions. The Agency began the expanded verification and monitoring work established by the agreement.

Nothing about Iran became innocent that morning.

Nothing about the United States became reliable forever.

A field that had been approaching military action received a machine for returning to the material.

That was enough to change what remained reachable.

XXXIV. A Narrow Agreement for a Wide Conflict

The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action was narrow on purpose.

This fact became one of the strongest arguments against it.

Iran’s missile program remained.

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps remained.

Hezbollah remained.

The war in Syria remained.

The conflict in Yemen remained.

Israel remained outside the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty with its policy of nuclear opacity.

United States forces and bases remained across the region.

Prisoners remained.

Sanctions unrelated to the nuclear file remained.

Women, dissidents, workers, minorities, journalists, students, political prisoners, and families inside Iran remained answerable to a state whose capacity for internal correction had narrowed.

The agreement did not turn the Islamic Republic into a normal liberal democracy, did not end Iranian support for armed organizations, did not remove American military predominance, did not create Palestinian freedom, did not solve Israeli security, did not settle Gulf rivalry, and did not write a regional constitution underneath the nuclear facilities while everyone waited politely in the lobby.

Critics called this weakness.

They were describing scope.

A diplomatic instrument can fail by being too narrow. It can also fail by being forced to carry every conflict between its parties at once.

The agreement had been built around one question:

Could Iran’s nuclear path be made long, visible, and difficult enough that a weapon decision would become detectable before possession?

That was already an enormous task.

A wider bargain would have required agreement on the meaning of resistance, occupation, terrorism, sovereignty, missile defense, foreign bases, recognition of Israel, Syria’s government, the future of Lebanon, the political order of Yemen, the security of the Gulf monarchies, American detainees, Iranian detainees, and the domestic legitimacy of the Islamic Republic.

This was not a treaty negotiation.

This was a request for the Middle East to finish becoming peaceful before anyone could count uranium.

The narrowness therefore protected the instrument. It separated the material relation from every grievance capable of poisoning it.

The narrowness also made the agreement vulnerable. Every excluded conflict remained available to attack the included one.

An Iranian missile test could be used to prove the nuclear agreement had failed even when the test did not violate the nuclear provisions.

An Israeli strike in Syria could intensify Iranian security pressure without entering the agreement’s formal machinery.

A crackdown inside Iran could make sanctions relief politically intolerable to people who understood, correctly, that the Iranian state was harming its own citizens.

An attack by an Iranian partner could become evidence that Tehran had been rewarded for aggression.

A United States administration could declare that the agreement ignored the behaviors it had never been designed to govern.

The agreement was a narrow bridge across a very wide fire.

That bridge did not extinguish the fire.

It only created one path that did not require walking through it.

XXXV. The Machine

The agreement’s force lived in machinery.

This deserves attention because public argument often converted the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action into one large mood.

  • Supporters called it diplomacy.
  • Opponents called it appeasement.
    • The document itself kept listing centrifuges.

For ten years, Iran would keep no more than 5,060 operating IR-1 centrifuges at Natanz for enrichment. Excess centrifuges and associated infrastructure would be removed and placed under International Atomic Energy Agency monitoring. Advanced-centrifuge research and development would proceed under staged limits. Enrichment-related activity would remain at Natanz under agreed conditions.

For fifteen years, enrichment would remain at or below 3.67 percent uranium-235. Iran’s stockpile of uranium hexafluoride enriched to that level would remain under 300 kilograms or the equivalent in other chemical forms. Excess material would be sold, exchanged, or down-blended.

Fordow would stop enriching uranium for fifteen years and become a nuclear, physics, and technology center. A limited number of centrifuges would remain there under arrangements that excluded uranium enrichment and nuclear material.

The heavy-water reactor at Arak would be redesigned so it did not produce weapons-grade plutonium in its original configuration. Its original core was removed and rendered unusable. Spent fuel from the redesigned reactor would leave Iran. Iran would avoid new heavy-water reactors and excess heavy-water accumulation for fifteen years.

Iran would not engage in spent-fuel reprocessing or construct a reprocessing facility during the agreed period. It also accepted restrictions on activities that could contribute to development of a nuclear explosive device.

The International Atomic Energy Agency received a long-term presence, access under Iran’s safeguards agreement and provisional application of the Additional Protocol, online enrichment measurement, electronic seals, access to records and facilities, monitoring of uranium ore concentrate for twenty-five years, and monitoring of centrifuge rotors and bellows for twenty years.

A procurement channel placed approved nuclear-related transfers inside a review process rather than leaving sensitive imports to informal trust.

The machine contained time.

Different parts of it lasted for different periods. Some obligations belonged to the permanent safeguards relation. Some restrictions lasted eight, ten, fifteen, twenty, or twenty-five years. Some were attached to particular facilities. Some changed as Iran’s long-term enrichment plan evolved. Some depended on later ratification or a broader International Atomic Energy Agency conclusion about the peaceful nature of Iran’s program.

The machine also contained a dispute path.

A participant claiming non-performance could bring the issue to the Joint Commission. Ministers could review it. An advisory board could issue a non-binding opinion. If the dispute remained unresolved and the complaining participant treated it as significant non-performance, that participant could reduce its own performance and notify the Security Council.

The Security Council procedure was deliberately inverted. The Council would vote on continuing sanctions relief. If that resolution was not adopted within thirty days, earlier United Nations sanctions would return unless the Council decided otherwise.

The design meant a permanent member could not protect Iran from reimposition simply by vetoing a sanctions resolution. The veto would work in the opposite direction.

This was a brutal little mechanism.

Iran accepted it because sanctions relief mattered and because the alternative field contained military attack, deeper isolation, and no recognized enrichment settlement.

The other parties accepted limits that left Iran with domestic enrichment because zero enrichment had not been reachable through negotiation.

The document was full of compromises that no participant would have selected from a clean board.

That is why it was an agreement.

XXXVI. Verification

Verification was the agreement’s best idea.

This word sounds administrative because modern civilization has spent a long time hiding some of its most important moral achievements behind office language.

Verification is a practice of making claims answerable to repeated contact.

  • Iran could say its program was peaceful.
  • The United States could say Iran was cheating.
    • Israel could say the agreement concealed a future arsenal.
    • Iranian officials could say sanctions relief was being obstructed.
      • European governments could say they remained committed.

Those statements all entered politics.

The inspector entered the facility.

The International Atomic Energy Agency did not determine whether Iran had become good. It measured material, equipment, access, inventory, enrichment, and declared activity. It checked whether the agreed physical conditions obtained. It compared one visit with another. It used seals, cameras, sampling, records, and monitoring systems to preserve continuity over time.

The agreement also did not create the unrestricted inspection regime often summarized by the phrase anywhere, anytime. Iran remained a sovereign state with a safeguards agreement, managed-access procedures, and routes for disputing requests. At declared sites, access and monitoring could be extensive and recurrent. At an undeclared location where the Agency had concerns about prohibited activity, the process could move through requests, Iranian proposals for alternative means, consultation, and ultimately the Joint Commission.

Critics focused on the possibility that this sequence could consume up to twenty-four days. That criticism identified a real delay. It did not establish that a site could be cleaned of every relevant trace on the twenty-third day. Nuclear material, conversion work, equipment, waste, environmental particles, records, and structural alterations can leave evidence that survives hurried concealment. The agreement’s purpose was not to give inspectors the powers of an occupying army. It was to create a time-limited route through which a denied request could become an enforceable compliance issue rather than an endless diplomatic shrug.

The distinction matters. Verification was strong because it combined declared-site monitoring, supply-chain visibility, material accounting, long-duration surveillance, and a procedure for suspicious locations. It was not omniscience. No inspection regime is. The serious question was whether the whole architecture made clandestine work more detectable, more expensive, and more likely to trigger a collective response.

This is the difference between reassurance and verification.

  • Reassurance asks whether a statement should calm the listener.
  • Verification asks what process would expose the statement to correction.

During the agreement’s functioning period, the Agency repeatedly reported that Iran was implementing its nuclear-related commitments. The Agency continued to verify the non-diversion of declared nuclear material under the safeguards agreement while conducting the additional monitoring established through the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action.

This did not establish that every historical safeguards question had vanished or that the Agency had reached the broadest possible conclusion about the absence of undeclared nuclear material throughout Iran.

It established something more exact:

The agreed limits could be checked, and the Agency was checking them.

That sentence should have carried more political force than it did.

A field can become addicted to intention-reading. Every party thinks the truth lies in what the other side secretly wants. The agreement did not abolish that desire. It made secret intention less decisive for a defined period by placing material conditions around the path.

Iran could retain hostile intentions and still remain farther from a weapon.

The United States could retain hostile intentions and still lift specified sanctions.

The parties did not need to agree about one another’s souls.

They needed to keep the machine running.

Modal Path Ethics recognizes this as a repair instrument because it lowers resistance without requiring innocence.

It does not say, “trust us.”

It says:

  • declare the material;
  • expose the equipment;
  • preserve the record;
  • allow return;
  • compare the field;
  • correct the mismatch;
  • attach relief to verified action;
  • keep the exit from escalation open.

A good verification system does not eliminate uncertainty.

It disciplines uncertainty.

That is why destroying or abandoning verification creates more than an information gap. It raises the resistance around every later agreement. The next inspector must reconstruct what the previous inspector had been allowed to keep continuous.

The machine remembers only while the relation survives.

XXXVII. Sunsets

The restrictions did not last forever.

This was true on the day the agreement was signed.

It remained true on every day critics announced that they had discovered it.

The criticism still mattered.

Some centrifuge restrictions were scheduled to relax after eight or ten years. The 3.67-percent enrichment ceiling, the 300-kilogram stockpile limit, the prohibition on enrichment at Fordow, and several reactor and reprocessing limits were designed around fifteen-year periods. Monitoring measures had different horizons. The procurement channel and United Nations framework had their own timelines. Transition Day and Termination Day were written into the implementation plan.

The agreement delayed and constrained capability. It did not erase Iran’s scientific knowledge, industrial base, or long-term enrichment claim.

This produced three real dangers.

  1. First, delay could be mistaken for resolution.
    1. A future Iranian program with more advanced centrifuges, larger stocks, and fewer exceptional restrictions could recreate threshold pressure after the most important limits expired.
  2. Second, the parties could use the agreement’s breathing room without building the next arrangement.
    1. Fifteen years sounds long to an exhausted negotiator.
      1. It is very short to a nuclear program, a security bureaucracy, or a state that expects to survive several governments.
  3. Third, the scheduled easing of limits could become a political countdown.
    1. Critics could describe every passing year as movement toward permission,
      1. while Iranian officials could treat each expiration as the restoration of a sovereign right denied under coercion.

The sunset problem was therefore partly a sequencing problem. Advanced centrifuge research did not remain frozen forever. Iran’s plan allowed staged testing and later manufacturing of agreed advanced machines under monitoring. A future negotiator would eventually face a state with more efficient technology, a recognized enrichment program, and fewer exceptional restrictions.

The agreement bought time against that future. It did not specify how the time would be converted into a successor bargain.

Time can be a strategic good. It can also become a warehouse where every party stores the work it refuses to do now.

The agreement therefore needed a successor path.

It needed the verification relationship to become ordinary, the Additional Protocol to become durable, the regional security field to become less coercive, and the parties to negotiate later limits before the original limits became cliffs.

That work did not happen.

The sunset criticism also acquired a false form.

Some arguments spoke as though every restriction simply disappeared one morning and Iran emerged holding a bomb in a decorative basket.

The safeguards agreement remained. The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty remained. The prohibition on nuclear weapons remained. International Atomic Energy Agency inspection remained in forms required by the enduring safeguards relationship. Some transparency measures continued longer than the central enrichment limits.

The problem was not that law ended.

The problem was that the most restrictive threshold-management instrument had a calendar and the wider security field did not use the calendar to prepare its successor.

A sunset is manageable when the next light has already been built.

Here, every party spent the afternoon arguing about who had betrayed the morning.

XXXVIII. The United States Just Fucking Leaves

On May 8, 2018, the United States left the agreement.

Iran had not yet begun the later stepwise reductions in its nuclear commitments. The International Atomic Energy Agency was still reporting implementation of the nuclear restrictions.

The American decision therefore did not answer an International Atomic Energy Agency finding that Iran had broken the central nuclear limits.

It answered a broader political judgment that the "agreement was inadequate."

The Trump administration argued that the accord failed to address ballistic missiles, regional armed activity, terrorism, detention of foreign citizens, and the expiration of important restrictions. It also disputed the long-term sufficiency of the inspection and enforcement architecture and argued that sanctions relief gave resources and legitimacy to a hostile regime.

Several of these concerns were real.

They had also been outside the agreement’s negotiated scope.

The withdrawal attempted to replace a narrow functioning instrument with a wider coercive demand.

The United States reimposed sanctions that had been lifted or suspended under the agreement. Secondary sanctions restored the threat that non-American firms, banks, insurers, shipping companies, and investors could lose access to the United States financial system by doing business with Iran.

The American state possessed a kind of jurisdiction the document had not fully contained. It could leave the multilateral agreement and still shape whether other parties could deliver the economic exchange written into it.

Iran remained formally inside the agreement for another year while the economic side contracted around it.

This point must be handled without propaganda.

  • Iran did receive some sanctions relief and trade after Implementation Day.
    • The agreement did not guarantee every investment, oil sale, banking relation, or commercial expectation.
    • Many firms remained cautious because non-nuclear sanctions, compliance risk, political volatility, and the possibility of renewed American sanctions never disappeared.

The United States withdrawal still changed the field decisively. It reimposed the nuclear-related pressure that Iran had traded nuclear limits to remove.

The agreement’s exchange relation had been cut by one of its central participants while the nuclear verification machine continued operating.

Iran learned that verified performance could fail to preserve the promised economic path. The other parties later learned that Iran would respond by making nuclear performance conditional again.

The contract had begun teaching everyone the worst lesson available.

XXXIX. Maximum Pressure

Maximum pressure was wider than the nuclear file because it was designed to be.

The United States demanded changes across enrichment, missiles, regional armed groups, Syria, Yemen, detainees, threats to neighbors, and the Revolutionary Guard’s external activity. Economic pressure would continue until the Iranian state altered a broad range of conduct.

The twelve demands announced by Secretary of State Mike Pompeo showed how far the replacement project extended. They included a full account of the nuclear program, an end to enrichment, unrestricted access, an end to ballistic-missile proliferation, release of detainees, withdrawal of forces from Syria, and termination of support for several armed organizations. The list assembled nuclear restraint, regional rollback, missile limits, detention policy, and ideological behavior into one coercive package.

A package this wide can be morally attractive because it refuses to isolate one danger from the others. It can also make agreement unreachable because every concession becomes dependent on victory across several different fields. Iran could not obtain meaningful relief by returning to the nuclear limits alone. The state would have to accept a much broader reordering of its security posture while under pressure explicitly designed to make refusal economically painful.

This gave the policy moral and strategic appeal.

  • Why should sanctions relief be protected while the Islamic Republic supported violent partners, repressed its population, detained people, threatened shipping, and built missile capacity?
  • Why should a nuclear agreement create a safe administrative room around one program while the same state acted destructively elsewhere?

The answer is uncomfortable:

Because the uranium remained in that room.

Maximum pressure treated the agreement’s narrowness as a defect and then used access to the global financial system to enlarge the negotiation by force. Iran would face rising costs until it accepted a more comprehensive settlement.

The policy did impose costs.

Oil exports fell sharply. Currency pressure deepened. Inflation and contraction intensified. Trade became harder. Firms withdrew. Financial channels closed. The state’s revenues and the population’s ordinary economic field were both burdened.

Pressure did not distribute itself according to culpability.

A sanctions designation may target a ministry, bank, company, official, shipping network, or security organization. Its effects travel through currency, employment, medicine, imports, savings, transport, and family life. Humanitarian goods may be formally exempt while banks and firms avoid transactions because the risk environment has become prohibitive.

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps could use sanctions evasion, informal networks, controlled companies, and security authority to strengthen its position inside the constrained economy. People with fewer connections absorbed higher prices and narrower futures.

Maximum pressure also created a measurement problem.

If the goal was to force a broader agreement, success required Iran to conclude that compliance would produce durable relief.

The United States had just shown that one administration could withdraw from a functioning agreement despite International Atomic Energy Agency verification.

More pressure increased the cost of refusing.

It also increased the value of a deterrent that could survive the next election.

This is the recurring danger of coercive leverage.

The instrument may punish the target into concession. It may also teach the target which capability must never again be traded for a promise.

XL. Iran Leaves by Steps

Iran did not respond by immediately leaving the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty or announcing a weapon program.

It left the agreement in pieces.

On May 8, 2019, one year after the American withdrawal, Iran announced that it would stop performing some commitments. The sequence then moved through the limits the agreement had made visible.

  • The low-enriched uranium stockpile exceeded the agreed ceiling.
    • Enrichment rose above 3.67 percent.
      • Research and development expanded beyond the agreed plan.
        • Uranium enrichment resumed at Fordow.
          • Advanced centrifuges multiplied.

Later steps included enrichment to twenty percent and then sixty percent, uranium-metal work, and increasingly severe departures from the agreement’s restrictions.

This was escalation.

This was also communication.

Iran designed many of the early steps to remain reversible in principle.

The state announced measures, waited, increased pressure, and tied restoration to sanctions relief and the return of other participants to performance.

The centrifuge became a diplomatic sentence.

Each cascade said the agreement’s benefits had not arrived strongly enough.

Each increase also shortened the material path and made the security field more dangerous.

Calibrated non-performance is still non-performance.

A state cannot preserve an agreement by progressively violating the conditions that made the agreement valuable.

The context remains binding.

Iran’s reductions followed the United States withdrawal and reimposition of sanctions. That sequence explains the policy and matters for responsibility.

It does not make enriched uranium into an innocent grievance letter.

  • The more Iran expanded the program, the more European governments and the United States could point to the physical field as clear proof that relief without stronger limits had become unsafe.
  • The more relief remained blocked, the more Iran could point to the contract as proof that nuclear restraint had produced exposure without exchange.

The machine was generating reasons for its own abandonment.

XLI. Europe Cannot Carry the Exchange

The European parties tried to preserve the agreement.

They maintained their formal commitment, continued sanctions relief under European law, activated a blocking regulation intended to limit the effect of United States extraterritorial sanctions, and created the Instrument in Support of Trade Exchanges.

INSTEX was supposed to facilitate legitimate trade through a clearing mechanism that reduced the need for direct cross-border payments exposed to American sanctions.

The European blocking regulation exposed the difference between legal permission and reachable commerce. European law could prohibit compliance with certain extraterritorial sanctions and allow firms to seek recovery of damages. It could not make a board of directors accept the possibility of losing access to American banks, markets, clearing systems, suppliers, insurers, and investors.

A right to trade remained on paper while the path to trade thickened in practice. This was clever.

It was also very small compared with the field it was asked to replace.

European governments could create a special-purpose vehicle. They could not make major banks ignore the United States financial system. They could not guarantee that global companies would accept the compliance risk of entering Iran. They could not recreate oil revenue, large-scale investment, ordinary banking, insurance, shipping, and commercial normalization through one clearing house with a nice acronym and the moral support of several foreign ministries.

INSTEX completed one publicly announced transaction in March 2020, facilitating the export of medical goods to Iran. The European shareholders later said Iran had blocked further proposed transactions and liquidated the mechanism in 2023.

Iran had its own complaints. A mechanism confined largely to humanitarian or limited legitimate trade did not deliver the economic normalization Tehran believed it had been promised. It did not restore oil exports at scale or give Iran ordinary access to the proceeds. A trade channel that could move medicine while the wider economy remained sanctioned looked less like exchange than a carefully supervised survival tube.

Both readings contain structure.

Europe did more than nothing.

Europe could not carry the economic side of the agreement against American secondary sanctions.

Iran did not convert the limited mechanism into a durable substitute.

The exchange had depended on power the document did not distribute evenly.

The European Union could coordinate diplomacy and lift European restrictions. The United States could shape access to the financial architecture through which much of the promised normalization had to move.

The agreement was multilateral. The money was not.

This was the market version of a field intelligence gap. The text said sanctions relief. The firms saw exposure. The governments saw commitment. The banks saw penalties. The commercial path disappeared between the categories.

XLII. Access Becomes Leverage

The most dangerous step came when verification itself entered the bargaining field.

In February 2021, Iran stopped implementing its nuclear-related commitments under the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action, including provisional application of the Additional Protocol. The International Atomic Energy Agency lost the expanded access and monitoring that had made the agreement unusually transparent.

Iran and the Agency reached a temporary technical understanding.

Some monitoring equipment would continue operating. Data would be stored under seal in Iran. If diplomacy restored the agreement, the Agency could receive the data and attempt to reconstruct continuity. If relief did not return, Iran could withhold or erase the record.

This arrangement was an emergency bridge built from a future conditional.

  • The cameras kept seeing.
    • The inspectors could not see what the cameras saw.
      • The data became leverage.

Iran had reasons for this move. The state was refusing to give extraordinary transparency for free while sanctions remained. Verification measures beyond the baseline safeguards agreement had been part of the exchange, and the exchange had failed.

The danger was larger.

An inspector is most valuable when access is ordinary. Once access becomes a concession, every visit acquires bargaining value. Every camera becomes a hostage surface. Every record becomes something one side can withhold to force performance from the other.

The correction instrument is recruited into the dispute it was built to clarify.

The temporary arrangements frayed. Iran declined to extend parts of them, restricted access to facilities, and later removed much of the Agency’s equipment installed for monitoring under the nuclear agreement. By 2022, the Agency warned that its capacity to recover continuity of knowledge had been seriously damaged.

This is a one-way cost.

  • A centrifuge count can be restored after access returns.
  • The missing interval cannot always be reconstructed with the same confidence.
    • Components may have been produced, moved, stored, damaged, or replaced while the monitoring relation was broken.
      • Records can help.
      • Declarations can help.
      • New inspections can help.
        • The field is still the field that went unseen.

The agreement had succeeded by making verification independent of trust.

Its collapse made verification dependent on negotiation.

That reversal carried the threshold toward war.

XLIII. Resolution 2231 Enters Legal Fog

The United Nations framework had a clock.

Resolution 2231 endorsed the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action in 2015, established the schedule for lifting and terminating prior nuclear-related Security Council measures, and contained the mechanism by which those measures could return after a claim of significant non-performance.

The mechanism had always been politically unstable.

The United States tried in 2020 to invoke snapback after leaving the agreement. Most other Council members rejected the attempt on the ground that the United States was no longer a participant entitled to use the mechanism.

The dispute waited.

In August 2025, France, Germany, and the United Kingdom notified the Security Council that they considered Iran in significant non-performance and invoked the snapback process. The Council did not adopt a resolution continuing sanctions relief within the required period. The European parties and the European Union treated the earlier United Nations sanctions and restrictions as reimposed at the end of September.

Russia, China, and Iran rejected that account. They argued that the European parties had forfeited or lacked authority to invoke the mechanism because of their own non-performance, the American withdrawal, and the later history of the agreement. They treated Resolution 2231 as reaching Termination Day on October 18, 2025, with no valid restoration of the earlier sanctions regime.

The Security Council entered 2026 holding meetings in which members disagreed about whether the legal architecture under discussion still existed in the same form.

The legal disagreement also reached backward into the agreement’s broken sequence. The European parties argued that Iranian non-performance had become sufficiently grave to justify the mechanism and that they had preserved their standing by remaining participants after the American withdrawal. Iran argued that the European parties had failed to deliver sanctions relief and could not invoke an enforcement mechanism after tolerating or participating in the agreement’s destruction. Russia and China added a Security Council argument: the procedure had been abused and could not recreate expired resolutions through a contested notification.

Each position used part of the instrument. None could compel the others to inhabit the same legal reality.

This is more than lawyer weather.

A verification and sanctions regime depends on common institutional reality.

  • Which resolutions are in force?
  • Which sanctions lists are active?
  • Which committee exists?
  • Which states are legally required to restrict what transfers?
  • Did the snapback process complete?
  • Did the resolution terminate?
  • Which participant retained standing to trigger which mechanism after years of reciprocal non-performance?

The Council could not give one answer accepted by all of its permanent members.

The agreement had begun as a machine for turning mistrust into common procedure.

It ended with the parties unable to agree on the procedure’s legal status.

This is the deepest institutional loss in the section.

A legal instrument can survive political hostility if the hostile parties continue to share the instrument.

Once the parties inhabit different versions of the law, even compliance becomes difficult to name.

The fog did not erase the uranium. It erased the common route by which states had agreed to respond to it.

XLIV. The Broken Contract Teaches Everyone

The agreement’s collapse produced lessons.

They were almost all terrible.

Iran learned that compliance verified by the International Atomic Energy Agency could fail to protect sanctions relief after a change of government in Washington.

The United States learned that leaving the agreement did not, it turned out, produce a broader agreement. It produced a larger Iranian program, shorter breakout estimates, weaker monitoring, and a state less willing to trade capability for executive promises.

Europe learned that diplomatic commitment could not substitute for financial power. The European parties could remain inside the agreement and still fail to deliver the economic path Iran expected.

Israel learned that the agreement could constrain Iran for a period while leaving knowledge, infrastructure, missile capacity, regional armed relationships, and later-expiring limits in place. From that perspective, delay could look like concealment beneath legitimacy.

The International Atomic Energy Agency learned that extraordinary access could be withdrawn in a political dispute and that continuity once broken might not be fully recoverable.

Russia and China learned that the agreement’s dispute machinery could become another arena in the larger struggle over Western power and Security Council authority.

Other states learned the worst proliferation lesson.

  • Ukraine had already shown that security assurances can fail after nuclear relinquishment.
  • Iran now showed that verified nuclear restraint can fail to preserve promised economic relief.

Neither case proves that acquiring nuclear weapons is wise, safe, lawful, or inevitable.

Both cases increase the apparent value of a capability that does not depend on another government’s continued performance.

This is how the nuclear order consumes its own credibility.

It asks states to remain non-nuclear through law, assurance, trade, inspection, restraint, and common security.

Then powerful states violate, abandon, narrow, or selectively enforce the instruments carrying that bargain.

The threshold begins to look like the only promise a state can keep by itself.

Iran also taught its adversaries a corresponding lesson.

When Tehran answered sanctions with expanded enrichment and answered diplomatic failure by restricting access, it made nuclear opacity useful as leverage. A state could accumulate pressure, shorten warning time, and offer restoration as the price of relief.

That strategy increased Iran’s bargaining power.

It also made preventive attack easier to defend.

The parties did not simply "fail to trust one another."

They trained one another away from verifiable restraint.

XLV. The Agreement’s Ruling

The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action did not solve Iran.

It did something more useful.

It made a dangerous relation inspectable.

Iran retained enrichment, scientific knowledge, an industrial base, missiles, regional partners, sacred-revolutionary authority, internal repression, and a state security machine built around resistance to foreign capture.

The United States retained military power, sanctions jurisdiction, regional bases, alliances, hostility toward the Islamic Republic, and the capacity to reverse policy after an election.

Israel retained its opaque nuclear arsenal and its willingness to act against hostile regional capability.

The Gulf remained armed. The wider conflicts remained unresolved.

The uranium became countable.

The agreement placed limits around enrichment, stockpile, centrifuges, Fordow, Arak, reprocessing, procurement, and weaponization-relevant activity. It created repeated access. It tied sanctions relief to verified action. It gave disagreement somewhere to go before disagreement became war.

Its narrowness allowed it to function.

Its narrowness also left every surrounding conflict available to destroy it.

The restrictions had sunsets. The parties did not build the successor architecture in time.

The United States withdrew while the International Atomic Energy Agency was still reporting Iranian implementation of the nuclear commitments. Maximum pressure widened the demand and narrowed the economic field. Iran answered through calibrated nuclear non-performance. Europe tried to preserve the exchange and could not reproduce the financial access the agreement required. Verification became leverage. The Security Council mechanism entered legal fog.

The contract taught every party that restraint depended on someone else’s future choice.

That is the opposite of durable security.

The agreement’s failure does not prove diplomacy was naive.

The failure shows what the diplomacy had successfully contained.

Once the machine weakened, enrichment rose, access narrowed, continuity broke, sanctions returned, military planning hardened, and the threshold acquired more political force.

The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action was narrow enough to work.

It was too narrow to survive every conflict placed on top of it.

Then, the conflict reached the facilities.

To understand how that conflict reached the facilities, the threshold has to be followed outward through forward defense, armed partners, missiles, sacred title, and the civilian fields made available to retaliation.

XLVI. Forward Defense

Iran could not build the regional military field it feared.

The United States possessed bases, fleets, intelligence networks, strategic airpower, long-range strike capacity, allied governments, and a defense budget large enough to make most comparisons look like a clerical error. Israel possessed technological superiority, an opaque nuclear arsenal, close American support, and a long record of acting against capabilities it considered intolerable. Gulf monarchies possessed money, advanced imported weapons, and security relationships built partly around protection from Iran.

Iran possessed geography, population, missiles, drones, endurance, revolutionary institutions, technical knowledge, and a state memory that treated encirclement as an active political fact.

It also possessed a problem.

The conventional balance did not favor Iran in a clean contest.

So a clean contest was therefore not the instrument.

Iran built distance through relation.

  • Hezbollah in Lebanon.
  • Palestinian armed organizations.
  • Iraqi militias and political networks.
  • Syrian routes, institutions, and military access before the collapse of the Assad government severed part of that architecture.
  • The Houthi movement in Yemen.
  • Missile and drone capabilities able to place bases, cities, ports, ships, and energy infrastructure inside retaliatory reach.

The common strategic description is forward defense.

The phrase is revealing.

Defense normally sounds like a perimeter around the defended object. Forward defense moves the perimeter outward. It tries to make an attack on the center produce danger somewhere else, earlier, wider, and through more than one route.

That can be intelligent.

A state facing conventionally stronger adversaries has reasons to prevent every conflict from beginning over its own cities. A dispersed retaliatory network complicates military planning. It raises the number of actors an attacker must consider. It makes the first strike less likely to end the war. It buys warning, uncertainty, bargaining power, and the possibility that the opponent will decide the cost is too difficult to contain.

Whose continuance carries the distance?

Forward defense becomes dangerous when another society’s political life is treated as space through which the center may absorb, redirect, or return violence.

The partner country receives weapons, training, money, protection, prestige, bargaining strength, and a place inside a larger story of resistance. It also receives surveillance, sanctions, rival intervention, internal militarization, contested sovereignty, and the possibility that local civilians will be made to answer for decisions taken across several command structures at once.

  • The strategic depth is real.
  • So is the living field placed inside it.

Iran’s network was therefore neither one imperial machine with many remote-controlled hands nor a loose collection of entirely independent movements that happened to receive very similar weapons from the same quarter.

It was a distributed security instrument.

Distributed instruments are difficult because control is real without being complete.

Iran can arm, fund, train, advise, coordinate, encourage, constrain, and sometimes direct. Local organizations retain histories, leaders, constituencies, rivalries, territorial obligations, religious commitments, electoral roles, criminal economies, survival interests, and independent reasons to fight. The relation varies by organization, by period, and by operation. A command sent from Tehran does not explain every action. Local agency does not erase Iranian material support.

Forward defense can preserve Iran from attack.

It can also distribute the cost of Iran’s security into Lebanon, Iraq, Syria, Yemen, Palestine, Israel, Jordan, the Gulf, the Red Sea, and every maritime route placed within retaliatory range.

That distribution becomes the bridge between nuclear threshold and regional war.

A nuclear facility may stand in Iran.

The consequences of striking it have been placed throughout the region.

XLVII. The Axis of Resistance

The name Axis of Resistance performs political work before any missile is launched.

It says the network is joined by refusal.

Refusal of Israel. Refusal of American military predominance. Refusal of occupation. Refusal of the regional order built around United States security partnerships. Refusal of governments that normalize relations while Palestinians remain under domination. Refusal of the idea that only states with superior air forces are allowed to define legitimate force.

There is real contact inside that language.

  • The Israeli occupation and dispossession of Palestinians are real.
  • The invasion and occupation of Lebanon are real historical conditions in Hezbollah’s formation.
  • The United States invasion of Iraq and the violent order that followed are real.
  • Saudi and Emirati intervention in Yemen is real.
  • Foreign military bases across the region are real.
  • Sanctions, assassinations, sabotage, detention, covert action, and regime-change history are real.

A resistance instrument can preserve memory that the dominant security order would prefer to classify as completed.

It can make abandoned populations harder to abandon.

It can impose costs on powers that possess overwhelming conventional superiority.

It can keep an injustice politically active after diplomacy has learned to walk around it.

Then the word begins asking for bodies.

That is the transition this track has been following from Sacred Instruments into Security Instruments.

A sacred memory can obligate repair. Sacred title begins when the memory claims ownership over living continuance.

Resistance can preserve a repair claim. Resistance becomes consumption when the people become fuel for the armed form of the claim.

The Axis of Resistance contains both possibilities because this is not one thing.

  • Hezbollah is an armed organization, political party, welfare network, sectarian institution, territorial actor, Iranian partner, Lebanese power center, and product of war.
  • Hamas is a Palestinian nationalist and Islamist organization with its own constituency, governing history, internal factions, regional relationships, and long record of armed violence against civilians.
  • Iraqi armed groups entered a field shaped by dictatorship, invasion, occupation, sectarian war, the rise of the Islamic State, party politics, state payrolls, and competing clerical authority.
  • The Houthis emerged from Yemen’s own political and religious history, fought local wars long before the current regional confrontation, and developed a governing and coercive project not reducible to Iranian instruction.
    • Iran found each of these movements useful.
    • They found Iran useful too.

A network built through reciprocal usefulness can still become asymmetrical. Tehran possesses a state, industrial capacity, diplomatic reach, missile technology, training institutions, intelligence, money, and the ability to place local struggle inside a transregional doctrine. The partners possess geography, local legitimacy, deniability, operational initiative, and the ability to make retaliation appear from several directions.

One actor can fire missiles. Another can strike shipping. Another can pressure a border. Another can attack a base. Another can wait and preserve the threat of entry.

The network makes escalation divisible.

That divisibility is one of its great strategic powers.

It is also how responsibility becomes fog.

Iran can deny direct command. The local actor can claim independent response. The target can answer Iran, the group, the host state, or all three. A government may lack the power to restrain an armed organization operating from its territory and still receive military retaliation for the organization’s action. A civilian population can become the nearest available object through which distant states communicate.

This is not an incidental failure around the instrument.

This is one of the instrument’s operating conditions.

  • Ambiguity protects the center.
    • The periphery absorbs interpretation.

The phrase Axis of Resistance then adds a sacred-political unity over the arrangement. Karbala, martyrdom, Jerusalem, Palestine, anti-imperial memory, revolutionary guardianship, and the blood of prior wars become a shared moral atmosphere. The atmosphere does not erase national difference. It makes difference answerable to a common struggle.

  • At its best, that struggle says no population should be left alone beneath a superior military machine.
  • At its worst, it says no population may leave the struggle while the machine remains.

The dead become witnesses. Then witnesses become recruiters.

A village, neighborhood, refugee camp, port, border district, apartment tower, school, hospital, power station, or merchant ship enters the confrontation because the network needs places from which resistance can remain reachable.

The civilian field becomes the medium.

That was already the problem in the Levant.

The nuclear threshold widened it.

An attack on Iran no longer needed to end in Iran.

The axis had made elsewhere available.

XLVIII. Sacred Title Returns

Iran’s nuclear program is often described in secular strategic language.

Fuel cycle. Enrichment. Breakout. Deterrence. Sanctions. Verification. Air defense. Missile range. Hardened facilities. Procurement. Threshold status.

All of that belongs.

It tells the state what the program means.

  • Sovereign mastery after foreign domination.
  • Scientific achievement under sanctions.
  • Refusal of Western technological hierarchy.
  • Defense of the Islamic Republic.
  • Proof that pressure cannot dictate Iran’s development.
  • A national right guarded against arrogant powers.
  • A capability held beneath a religious prohibition on nuclear weapons.

The fatwa and the threshold sit in the same field.

  • The prohibition says possession and use are morally forbidden.
  • The material capacity says Iran will not surrender the technical path on the assurance that hostile states will respect weakness.

That combination can be coherent.

The combination also produces an authority problem.

Who interprets the prohibition?
Who determines whether the state has crossed it?
Who can inspect the material independently of the authority that declares the intention pure?
Who can correct the guardian if the guardian says the accusation itself is foreign pressure?

Sacred title appears when the revolutionary state treats its historical wound as ownership over the interpretation of the present.

The 1953 coup, support for the Shah, the hostage crisis, the Iran-Iraq War, chemical attack, sanctions, sabotage, assassinations, Israeli nuclear opacity, and military threats all become part of one inherited claim:

  • Iran has earned the right to decide which restrictions are humiliation.

The history explains the sensitivity. It cannot decide the safeguards question.

  • The dead of the Iran-Iraq War can obligate the state to protect Iran from renewed invasion.
    • They cannot certify an enriched-uranium inventory.
  • The martyrs can expose the cruelty of a world that tolerated Iraqi chemical warfare.
    • They cannot answer where nuclear material has moved during a lost inspection interval.
  • The revolution can preserve memory of foreign capture.
    • It cannot make every demand for external verification another coup.

That distinction becomes especially difficult under attack.

When nuclear facilities are bombed, foreign warnings become materially inseparable from foreign violence. The state can point to the crater and say the inspectors belong to the exact same architecture that prepared the target. The attacking states can point to the missing inspectors and say opacity proves the bombing was necessary.

Each side uses the other’s action to make correction less credible.

Sacred title enters from one direction.

Security title enters from the other.

  • Iran says sovereignty and revolutionary history grant control over the terms of contact.
  • The attackers say danger grants control over Iran’s future capability.

Both claims place living continuance beneath inherited authority.

The nuclear threshold becomes a sacred-security object.

  • It is guarded from correction by the memory of domination.
  • It is attacked in the name of preventing future catastrophe.

The field between those claims contains the inspectors, workers, scientists, nearby residents, soldiers, dissidents, believers, critics, families, neighboring populations, and everyone whose future can be narrowed by either the weapon or the war against the weapon.

  • Sacred title does not mean sacred attachment is false.
  • Security title does not mean security fear is false.

The danger begins when the attachment and fear claim the throne.

Then the material must answer to the story before the story answers to the material.

XLIX. Distributed Deterrence

A nuclear threshold is more useful when the adversary cannot isolate it.

This is one reason the Iranian case cannot be reduced to the enrichment facilities.

Iran’s deterrent field has several layers:

  • conventional armed forces;
  • the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps;
  • ballistic and cruise missiles;
  • drones;
  • air defenses;
  • naval mines, small boats, antiship weapons, and coastal systems;
  • cyber capacity;
  • regional partners;
  • energy geography;
  • the Strait of Hormuz;
  • and the nuclear threshold itself.

Each layer changes the meaning of the others.

A missile program gives a future nuclear capability a possible delivery path. The possibility of a nuclear program makes missile development more alarming. Regional partners create retaliation routes that survive damage to central command. Maritime leverage makes attacks on land answer in global trade. A dispersed industrial and scientific base makes physical destruction less final. The threshold makes every conventional strike carry a question about what Iran may decide to build afterward.

This is distributed deterrence.

The state does not rely on one invulnerable instrument.

It relies on the attacker’s uncertainty about the total response.

Uncertainty is doing security work.

That work can prevent war.

It can also make war very difficult to stop after it begins.

A simple deterrent relation has an awful clarity:

Attack here, and this answer follows.

A distributed relation has branching answers.

  • A missile may come from Iran.
  • A drone may come through another route.
  • A militia may attack a United States base.
  • Hezbollah may open or intensify a front with Israel.
  • The Houthis may threaten the Red Sea or Bab el-Mandeb.
  • Shipping may be detained or struck in and around Hormuz.
  • Gulf energy infrastructure may enter range.
  • Cyber operations may target infrastructure.
  • Nuclear cooperation may be suspended.
  • Enriched material may become harder to locate.

A patron may gain leverage from the possibility that a partner will act independently. The adversary must price the partner’s anger even when Tehran has not issued an order. Local initiative becomes part of the central deterrent. The same independence that prevents a clean command attribution makes escalation harder to terminate.

A state can tell itself that the network is defensive because the network makes attack costly.

The people living inside the network experience something else.

They live in the places where retaliation has been pre-positioned.

The weapon may be hidden in a district because the district provides concealment, support, coercive control, logistical access, or simple geography. The targeter then treats the district as military space. The armed organization treats the people as its constituency, shield, responsibility, audience, or unavoidable surrounding field. The civilian is asked to carry both interpretations at once.

Distributed deterrence therefore has a burden-transfer problem.

  • Iran gains distance.
    • The partner field receives proximity.

A missile launched from Yemen can protect Iran by threatening shipping and adversary infrastructure. The Yemeni civilian field receives retaliation, blockade pressure, economic damage, and the political consequences of an armed movement making transregional war part of local governance.

Hezbollah’s arsenal can make an attack on Iran more dangerous for Israel. Lebanon receives the airstrikes, displacement, destroyed housing, political paralysis, and the question of whether a state may ever regain full authority over war conducted from its territory.

Iraqi armed groups can threaten United States bases and Gulf states. Iraq receives the pressure of being both sovereign state and strategic corridor for actors whose command relations remain deliberately difficult to resolve.

The deterrent is distributed. The suffering is too.

That does not make the network strategically irrational.

It makes the moral account larger than Tehran’s survival.

The nuclear threshold sits at the center of this architecture because it changes how much force adversaries believe can be used before the entire field reorganizes.

The bomb does not need to exist.

The possibility that attack will make it exist can already deter.

The possibility that Iran will answer conventionally across several regions can already deter.

The possibility that the attackers will strike again if the program rebuilds can already coerce.

The threshold is now acting through every instrument around it.

L. The Levant Leverage Field Returns

The Levant Leverage field began when one locus’s continuance became another actor’s bargaining power.

Iran’s regional strategy expands that structure.

A hostage remains a hostage. This word should not be stretched over every strategic relation until the crime disappears inside metaphor.

The larger leverage field contains other captured continuances.

  • A Lebanese civilian field exposed to war because Hezbollah’s arsenal is part of regional deterrence.
  • An Israeli civilian field exposed to rockets, drones, and attacks justified through resistance to Israeli state violence.
  • Palestinians exposed to Israeli military destruction, occupation, blockade, displacement, and the armed strategies of organizations claiming to fight for their liberation.
  • Syrians whose country became a route, battlefield, client relation, revolutionary wound, and site of competing foreign military projects.
  • Iraqis living inside the afterlife of invasion, militia power, Iranian influence, American bases, Islamic State violence, and a state still negotiating its monopoly over force.
  • Yemenis trapped between Houthi rule, regional intervention, economic collapse, and the conversion of maritime attack into global political speech.
  • Gulf populations placed inside retaliation against American and Israeli power because their governments host bases, cooperate militarily, or occupy strategically useful territory.
  • Iranian civilians placed under sanctions, airstrikes, mobilization, repression, infrastructure damage, and the state’s claim that external danger requires internal unity.

The leverage machine does not require any wound to be fictional.

It requires the wounds to become spendable.

A civilian population becomes pressure on a government.
A port becomes pressure on trade.
A missile becomes pressure on negotiations.
A hostage becomes pressure on military action.
A blockade becomes pressure on a population.
A nuclear threshold becomes pressure on the states considering attack.
A military strike becomes pressure on the state considering enrichment.

The security field begins exchanging continuance.

This is why the phrase regional escalation can be too clean.

Escalation sounds like a line rising on a chart.

The lived process is a routing operation. Damage is moved across places until the actor with enough leverage obtains a different decision.

The civilian field becomes the communications medium.

Iran’s Axis of Resistance can expose the brutality of Israeli and American power while reproducing the exact same underlying instrument: make another population carry enough danger that the stronger actor must change course.

Israel and the United States can describe attacks on Iranian facilities as prevention while using Iranian exposure to force surrender of technical capacity and political ambition.

Gulf governments can host the military architecture intended to deter Iran while asking Iran to keep their territory outside the retaliation field.

Every actor wants its security relation recognized and its burden transfer treated as background.

Modal Path Ethics just refuses the privilege.

  • No hostage-taking is liberation.
  • No civilian-field destruction is security.
  • No regional partner is terrain.
  • No nuclear threshold grants authority over the people living beneath it.
  • No preventive strike turns the civilian consequences into a technical footnote.

The threshold was approaching war because the region had already become an instrument for making its consequences felt.

The attacks would activate what had been distributed.

LI. Prevention Doctrine

Preventive war begins before the target has completed the feared act.

That is its defining burden.

The attacker does not claim to answer a weapon already used.

It claims to answer a future becoming too dangerous to permit.

The doctrine has a powerful intuitive form:

  • A hostile state is approaching a capability capable of catastrophic harm.
  • Delay reduces the chance of successful intervention.
    • Diplomacy is failing, reversible, deceptive, or too slow.
    • Once the capability becomes operational, deterrence may be the only remaining tool.
      • Deterrence may fail.
      • Therefore, force used before completion can prevent a much worse field later.

Israel has acted through versions of this logic before.

Its 1981 attack on Iraq’s Osirak reactor and its 2007 attack on a suspected Syrian reactor became part of a doctrine in which hostile regional nuclear capability is treated as intolerable before it reaches operation.

The examples encourage confidence because the attacked states did not answer those specific strikes with immediate regional nuclear war.

They also create a very dangerous story:

The aircraft can perform verification through destruction.

A bomb can establish that a facility has been damaged.

It cannot establish the complete state of a nuclear program.

Physical capability has several components:

  • buildings;
  • power;
  • centrifuges;
  • feed material;
  • enriched stock;
  • conversion equipment;
  • metallurgy;
  • diagnostics;
  • research records;
  • procurement networks;
  • trained personnel;
  • hidden sites;
  • command decisions;
  • and knowledge distributed across institutions and people.

An air campaign can destroy some of these.

The more successful it is, the more delay it may create.

Delay can be morally significant. A year without a weapon is a different field from a year with one. Several years may preserve diplomacy, leadership change, new inspections, regional de-escalation, or another technical arrangement.

Delay is still delay.

A preventive strike can make the feared future less reachable in the short term.

It can also increase the target state’s desire to acquire the very capability being prevented.

The logic is obvious from the target’s side.

  • A threshold did not prevent attack.
  • Negotiation did not preserve the facilities.
  • Sacred prohibition did not generate trust.
  • Safeguards did not protect declared sites.
    • Latency created danger without final immunity.
      • Therefore, possession may appear safer than approach.

This conclusion is not inevitable.

Iranian leaders could instead conclude that this program imposes intolerable national cost. They could accept deeper limits, external fuel arrangements, permanent inspection, or dismantlement in exchange for security and reconstruction.

The attack changes which argument has evidence.

  • The hard-liner can point to the crater.
  • The diplomat can point to the lost agreement.
  • The inspector can point to the missing inventory.
  • The civilian can point to the roof that is no longer there.

Preventive doctrine claims urgency because the future may close.

It must still account for the futures force closes while attempting to keep one weapon out of reach.

LII. Knowledge Survives the Building

A nuclear facility is a place where knowledge has become machinery.

That is why it can be bombed.

The machinery has coordinates. It requires power, halls, pipes, control systems, feed, vacuum equipment, cascades, workshops, storage, access routes, cooling, security, and people arriving through gates.

Destroying the place matters.

The phrase knowledge survives the building must never be used to pretend physical destruction is symbolic.

  • A centrifuge cascade cannot enrich uranium after it has been broken into components and buried beneath concrete.
  • A conversion line cannot process material after its critical equipment is destroyed.
  • A tunnel entrance collapsed by penetrating weapons changes access.
  • A dead scientist cannot continue the work.
  • A damaged industrial base requires time, money, replacement parts, new sites, new personnel, and new risk.

Matter resists slogans.

The attacker can purchase real delay with force.

The problem is that the program is larger than the target list.

Scientific knowledge exists across people, documents, institutions, universities, suppliers, military organizations, industrial memory, computer systems, and tacit practice. Some of it can be reconstructed from global technical knowledge. Some equipment can be manufactured. Some can be smuggled. Some stockpiles can be moved before attack. Some facilities can be dispersed, buried, disguised, or rebuilt with the lessons of the previous strike embedded in their design.

The program after the strike is not the old program minus damaged buildings.

It is a program generated by the strike.

That does not mean it will be stronger.

It may be technically devastated, politically divided, starved of money, penetrated by intelligence services, and unable to restore critical capability for years. The state may lose leaders, managers, supply chains, and confidence. The attack can expose security failures severe enough to reorganize the regime.

The surviving program will still be different in the direction of the pressure placed upon it.

More secretive.
More militarized.
Less inspectable.
More closely attached to national survival.
More likely to treat every external request as targeting support.

A civilian nuclear bureaucracy may move toward military custody because the military is the institution still authorized to operate under attack. Inspectors may become security liabilities. Records may be withheld. Material may be dispersed beyond the sites where repeated measurement had once been possible.

The preventive strike attacks a material sequence.

It also enters the sequence.

Modal Path Ethics therefore asks a stricter question than whether the bombing worked.

What program did the bombing make reachable?

The answer cannot be known on the morning after the strike.

That is why the inspector matters.

  • The aircraft can see the explosion.
  • The safeguards system can, under functioning conditions, reconstruct the material relation over time.

When the attack removes both the machinery and the measurement relation, public certainty becomes easier while technical certainty becomes harder.

  • The targeter says the program has been destroyed.
    • The target says the knowledge and will remain.
      • The inspector says access is required.

The threshold begins living inside the difference.

LIII. June 2025

On June 13, 2025, the first major rupture arrived.

Israel attacked Iran, including several nuclear facilities.

The International Atomic Energy Agency stopped in-field verification activities for safety. The attacks continued through June 24. On June 22, the United States struck Fordow, Natanz, and Esfahan.

This article began with the last measured stockpile because the war made that date morally active.

Before the attack, the Agency estimated Iran held 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched up to sixty percent uranium-235. The number described a dangerous threshold and an evidentiary achievement. Inspectors had a relation to declared material.

After the attack, the facilities had a damage assessment.

The material had an interval.

The International Atomic Energy Agency confirmed extensive damage at Esfahan and impacts at the entrances to underground tunnels. It could not immediately determine the extent of damage at Fordow because the facility was built deep inside a mountain and the weapons used were designed to penetrate. Iranian authorities reported no elevated off-site radiation levels after the United States attacks.

The attacking states had another danger in view.

They believed the Iranian threshold had become intolerable.

The public argument quickly sought completion.

Facilities had been obliterated.
Capability had been set back.
The threat had been removed.

The language wanted a neat switch again.

  • Before: nuclear danger.
    • Strike.
      • After: no nuclear danger.

The material field did not offer that convenience. It almost never does.

The Agency lost access to the affected facilities and associated nuclear material. Iran enacted a law suspending cooperation. Inspectors were withdrawn for safety. Later agreements and partial inspections recovered some contact at unaffected facilities and specific locations. The critical inventory remained unresolved.

  • The attack had damaged the program.
    • It had also damaged the answer.

That dual effect is the center of the war.

Preventive war is often defended as action in the face of uncertainty.

Except here, action produced more uncertainty around the object it was supposed to settle.

The strategic result may still have included real delay. Facilities were damaged. Equipment was destroyed. Personnel and command systems were disrupted. Iran’s program could definitely not continue exactly as it had on June 12.

The political result was wider.

  • Iran had received proof that threshold status did not protect its territory.
  • Israel and the United States had received proof that they were willing and able to strike declared nuclear sites directly.
  • The International Atomic Energy Agency had received proof that its verification mission could be interrupted by the military decisions of states outside its control.
  • Regional actors had received proof that the conflict could move from proxy confrontation to direct attack.

The nuclear threshold had entered war.

It had not yet finished entering it yet.

LIV. The Inspectors Leave

The inspector is one of the least cinematic figures in war.

This is a major qualification for ethical usefulness.

The inspector does not solve the nuclear field by displaying overwhelming power. The inspector asks for a declaration, opens a door, checks a seal, compares an inventory, samples dust, reviews a record, photographs equipment, returns later, and writes a report whose most important sentence may be that the material balance remains unresolved.

There is no fireball here.

There is continuity.

Continuity is what the attacks broke.

When inspectors left Iran at the end of June 2025 for safety, they did not take the nuclear material with them.

They took the live external relation to it.

Iran later allowed access to some facilities unaffected by the attacks. Inspections resumed at Bushehr. A September 2025 arrangement attempted to establish practical safeguards procedures after the attacks. The Agency conducted some visits at unaffected facilities.

The affected facilities and their associated material remained outside the required verification relation.

By March 2026, the Director General said the Agency had gone more than eight months without access to Iran’s previously declared inventories of low-enriched and highly enriched uranium. The verification was long overdue. The Agency could no longer provide assurances regarding the non-diversion of declared nuclear material from peaceful activities at the affected facilities.

This sentence is narrower than a claim that Iran had diverted material into a weapon. It is also much more serious than a bureaucratic inconvenience.

A safeguards system does not exist to tell the world that diversion definitely happened after access is lost.

It exists to preserve enough contact that diversion can be detected, excluded, reconstructed, or placed under accountable uncertainty.

Once continuity is lost, the field acquires verification debt.

The old measurements remain valid for the dates on which they were made.

They no longer describe the entire later path.

The Agency then has to rebuild a baseline:

  • what material survived;
  • where it was moved;
  • what containers were damaged;
  • what chemical forms changed;
  • what enrichment levels remain;
  • what equipment was destroyed;
  • what new equipment was installed;
  • what records survived;
  • what production occurred during the gap;
  • and which declarations can be reconciled with physical evidence.

That is why trust cannot carry the field.

  • Iran asks the Agency and outside powers to understand the consequences of attacks on safeguarded facilities.
  • The Agency asks Iran to fulfill legal safeguards obligations regardless of the wider conflict.
    • The attacking states ask why Iran should receive time and privacy around damaged sites containing dangerous material.

Every position contains a real claim.

No claim can substitute for access.

The inspectors leaving was therefore not a side effect to be repaired after the war. This was one of the war’s central strategic outcomes.

The material became harder to govern precisely when the political pressure around it became most intense.

The threshold gained opacity.

LV. February 2026

On February 28, 2026, the threshold went fully regional.

The United States and Israel launched a new campaign against Iran. The stated aims included Iran’s nuclear and ballistic-missile programs. The campaign also carried open language of political transformation.

Iran’s Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei, was killed in the strikes.

That death alone would have reorganized the sacred and institutional field.

The office of Leadership stood at the center of the constitutional system, military command, revolutionary guardianship, nuclear prohibition, and the political theology by which the state explained its continuance. Killing the officeholder during an external attack did not simply remove a decision-maker.

That placed succession, martyrdom, regime survival, revenge, and national defense inside the same event.

The attack was therefore almost designed to activate the deepest machinery already established in revolutionary guardianship.

Foreign force had killed the guardian.

The state could present the war as the final confirmation of its entire historical account.

So Iran responded across the region.

It attacked Israel, United States bases, and sites in neighboring states. Hezbollah opened attacks from Lebanon, followed by Israeli operations there. Iranian-aligned armed groups entered or threatened other fronts. The Strait of Hormuz was largely closed, obstructed, or made too dangerous for normal commercial operation. Merchant vessels and seafarers became part of the war.

The distributed deterrent had activated.

This did not mean every action was commanded from one room in Tehran.

The death of the Supreme Leader, damage to command systems, local initiative, partner strategy, prior contingency planning, and political pressure inside each organization all shaped the response. The network was doing what distributed systems do under attack:

Continuing through multiple nodes.

The result was not clean resilience.

It was a widening field of targets.

United Nations Security Council resolution 2817 condemned Iranian attacks on neighboring Gulf states and threats or actions aimed at closing, obstructing, or interfering with navigation through Hormuz. The International Maritime Organization reported attacks on merchant vessels, deaths and injuries among seafarers, ship detentions, possible mines, and thousands of people trapped aboard vessels inside the Gulf.

Iran could answer that United States and Israeli attacks had begun the war.

That answer belongs in the causal history.

It does not make a tanker crew an American air base.

It does not turn a residential building in Bahrain into a nuclear facility.

It does not make a port worker responsible for the death of Iran’s leader.

The same refusal applies in the other direction.

The United States and Israel could describe the campaign as necessary to end nuclear and missile danger.

That description does not make an Iranian family a centrifuge cascade.

It does not make an inspector’s absence evidence of a completed bomb.

It does not make regime change an incidental technical measure.

This war revealed the full threshold structure.

The nuclear issue had become inseparable from:

  • leadership succession;
  • ballistic missiles;
  • regional armed networks;
  • Gulf bases;
  • Israeli security;
  • Lebanese war;
  • Iraqi sovereignty;
  • Yemeni maritime power;
  • world energy supply;
  • shipping law;
  • sanctions;
  • reconstruction;
  • and the question of whether attack would finally produce the Iranian decision to build a weapon.

The nuclear threshold had not become a bomb.

It had become a war system.

LVI. The Conflict Opens

The conflict did not proceed as one continuous line of total war.

It moved through strikes, retaliation, pauses, mediation, threats, maritime incidents, indirect talks, broken understandings, and repeated announcements that the next arrangement might finally hold.

This pattern matters.

A ceasefire is often imagined as silence after violence.

In a threshold war, ceasefire becomes another instrument for managing capability, shipping, sanctions, reconstruction, regional partners, and the possibility of renewed attack.

Pakistan announced a temporary pause in April 2026 after weeks of regional fighting. Israel-Hezbollah hostilities continued. United States-Iranian and Israeli-Iranian attacks recurred. On June 17, the United States and Iran signed a fourteen-point memorandum of understanding intended to create a path toward ending the war.

No official agreed text was published in a stable public form. Accounts released or briefed by the parties described a sixty-day process and several immediate exchanges:

  • an end to military operations across the relevant fronts;
  • lifting the United States blockade of Iranian ports;
  • arrangements for safe shipping through Hormuz;
  • sanctions relief for Iranian oil exports;
  • discussion of frozen assets;
  • reconstruction support;
  • and negotiations over Iran’s nuclear and missile programs, inspection, and enriched material.

The agreement also carried immediate structural weaknesses.

Hezbollah and Israel were not signatories to the United States-Iran instrument. Iraqi groups, the Houthis, Gulf states, and other actors whose conduct could collapse the ceasefire stood partly outside its legal relation. The terms around Hormuz depended on competing accounts of navigational authority and security. The nuclear baseline remained unresolved. The United States and Iran did not agree on who had violated the arrangement when incidents occurred.

The memorandum gave the conflict somewhere to go.

It did not give every actor one command.

Late June demonstrated the problem.

An attack on a vessel near the Strait caused the International Maritime Organization to pause an evacuation corridor. The United States struck Iranian coastal military infrastructure, describing the action as a response to attacks on commercial shipping. Iran described the strikes as a violation of the memorandum and answered with missiles and drones toward United States military infrastructure in the Gulf. The United States struck again. Qatar hosted indirect talks. Iran and Oman discussed implementation of the shipping provisions.

The ceasefire became a dispute over what the ceasefire required.

By July, further maritime attacks, regional militia activity, and renewed threats had again narrowed the diplomatic field. The International Maritime Organization continued working through a dangerous evacuation and safe-passage problem. Shipping remained below pre-war levels. Thousands of seafarers had already been trapped or evacuated through emergency arrangements.

As of August 2, 2026, the war remained inside another attempted turn toward negotiation.

President Donald Trump has said he will hold off on threatened new United States strikes while regional mediators seek a rapid arrangement to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and address Iran’s nuclear program. Iran says talks with Oman concern a new shipping route and rejects the claim that the Strait will simply return to its pre-war status. No final agreement has been reached. Israel says it retains the right to strike if Iran rebuilds its nuclear or ballistic-missile capacity.

The diplomatic and military sequence remains live. Its factual endpoint must be checked again immediately before publication.

The ethical structure is already stable enough to name.

  • Every pause is burdened by the absence of a verified nuclear baseline.
  • Every inspection proposal is burdened by the possibility of another attack.
  • Every shipping agreement is burdened by the use of Hormuz as leverage.
  • Every sanctions promise is burdened by the memory of the United States leaving the previous agreement.
  • Every Iranian restraint claim is burdened by unresolved material and prior safeguards failures.
  • Every American or Israeli security claim is burdened by the damage preventive force did to verification.
  • Every regional partner is burdened by the fact that the memorandum may ask organizations and governments outside it to deliver compliance.

The conflict opens each time one actor tries to close only its preferred part.

LVII. The Strait Becomes a Lever

The Strait of Hormuz is narrow enough to make geography feel like authority.

A large share of the world’s traded oil and liquefied natural gas passed through it before the war. Iran occupies the northern side. Oman occupies the southern side. International shipping lanes cross a space where coastal geography, international law, naval power, merchant traffic, insurance, energy markets, military bases, and regional sovereignty meet in very little water.

This is excellent leverage terrain.

Iran did not create the world’s dependence on the Strait.

The global energy system created that dependence through production patterns, ports, pipelines, shipping, consumption, and the decision to route so much continuance through one maritime throat.

Iran can still use the throat.

During the 2026 war, attacks, detentions, threats, the purported closure of the Strait, and danger around shipping sharply reduced safe transit. The International Maritime Organization reported no safe passage at points in the conflict, dozens of verified attacks, deaths and injuries among seafarers, ships trapped inside the Gulf, and the need for an evacuation framework.

The strategic effect was immediate.

Energy prices rose.
Insurance risk rose.
Gulf governments feared attacks on infrastructure.
The United States faced pressure to restore navigation.
Oman became central to mediation because the waterway could not be governed from one shore.

Iran’s geography had become bargaining power over a global field.

This is the Levant Leverage structure at maritime scale.

Closing the Strait can therefore impose cost on adversaries.

It also transfers cost through millions of weaker loci whose only relation to the conflict is dependence on the systems moving through the water.

The same is true of military attempts to force the Strait open.

A naval blockade, escort mission, strike on coastal systems, mine-clearing campaign, or attack on boats may preserve navigation. It can also widen combat, damage ports, kill civilians, and turn the coast into a permanent target zone.

The repair question cannot be answered by choosing between Iranian control and American control.

This waterway needs an instrument that neither side can convert into title.

  • Recognized navigational rights.
  • Oman-Iran coordination.
  • International monitoring.
  • Maritime hotlines.
  • Vessel identification.
  • Protected evacuation corridors.
  • Mine reporting and clearance.
  • Incident investigation.
  • Restrictions on attacks against civilian shipping.
    • Consequences that do not require each incident to become another air campaign.

The current negotiations over a route through Iranian and Omani waters contain part of this shape.

They remain unstable because the Strait is still serving two incompatible functions.

  • The world needs it as infrastructure.
  • Iran needs it as leverage.

A security settlement must reduce the value of closure without denying Iran’s own exposure to hostile naval power at its coast.

Until then, the Strait will keep converting geography into threat.

LVIII. The War Against Possibility

Preventive war is war against a future that has not fully arrived.

This does not make the future imaginary.

A nuclear threshold can be materially real, technically advanced, strategically active, and dangerous before weapon possession. Iran’s sixty-percent stockpile, enrichment infrastructure, centrifuge development, unresolved safeguards issues, missile forces, regional network, and shortened material path all belonged to extance.

  • The feared continuation was reachable.
    • The attacker then acts against reachability itself.

This is one of the hardest security problems Modal Path Ethics can face.

  • The framework cannot say that only completed harms may be prevented.
    • That would make ethics wait politely until every catastrophe became actual.
  • It also cannot say that a sufficiently frightening projection authorizes destruction.
    • That would allow every powerful actor to kill the future it dislikes and call the absence of proof a success.

The question is what evidence, imminence, severity, reversibility, alternatives, and correction mechanisms make force the least-closing path.

In the Iranian case, the attacking states could point to:

  • a large stockpile of highly enriched uranium;
  • enrichment far beyond ordinary civilian fuel requirements;
  • unresolved undeclared nuclear material and activities;
  • reduced monitoring;
  • advanced centrifuges;
  • missile capabilities;
  • hostile state rhetoric;
  • attacks by regional partners;
  • and the possibility that delay would make military intervention much harder.

Iran could point to:

  • its status as a non-nuclear-weapon state under the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty;
  • the absence of a publicly established decision to build a weapon before the attacks;
  • a religious prohibition on nuclear weapons;
  • prior verified compliance with the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action before the United States withdrew;
  • attacks, sabotage, assassinations, sanctions, and Israeli nuclear opacity;
  • and the sovereign right to peaceful nuclear technology under safeguards.

This is how the field produced a war against possibility.

The attack may have prevented a nearer weapons path.

It also made several harmful continuations more reachable:

  • civilian death and infrastructure destruction;
  • regional retaliation;
  • attacks on shipping;
  • internal repression under emergency;
  • leadership martyrdom;
  • reduced safeguards access;
  • material dispersal;
  • withdrawal from cooperative arrangements;
  • proliferation pressure in neighboring states;
  • and the Iranian conclusion that only a completed deterrent can prevent repetition.

The war against possibility cannot be judged only by whether the feared future was delayed. It must be judged by the field generated around the delay.

A delay purchased through destruction may still be Better than allowing imminent catastrophe.

That category does not grant innocence.

It leaves remainder.

Here, the remainder includes

  • an unknown inventory,
  • a regional war,
  • dead civilians and soldiers,
  • damaged facilities,
  • trapped seafarers,
  • a broken inspection relation,
  • a killed Supreme Leader,
  • intensified sacred authority,
  • and a new negotiation conducted beneath the threat of renewed strikes.

The military action entered the nuclear sequence.

The sequence now has to be repaired with the action inside it.

LIX. Prevention Destroys Verification

The central failure can now be stated without compression.

The attack meant to prevent a hidden weapon made the program harder to inspect.

This is not the same as saying the attack created all opacity.

Iran had already concealed activities, failed to declare material and locations, restricted monitoring, removed or deactivated equipment, and used access as leverage. The verification field was damaged before June 2025.

The attack changed the type and scale of the damage.

A safeguards dispute became a war-zone reconstruction problem.

The Agency now had to distinguish concealment from destruction, movement from evacuation, diversion from rubble, security exclusion from strategic denial, and lost records from withheld records.

The states conducting the strikes possessed intelligence.

Intelligence is not a substitute for safeguards.

Intelligence agencies answer national leaders. Their methods are secret. Their evidence may be fragmentary, selectively released, compromised by source protection, or interpreted through threat assumptions. Intelligence can discover hidden activity that inspectors miss. It can also generate claims that outside states cannot independently test.

Safeguards answer a different need.

They create a shared evidentiary relation capable of supporting international judgment across hostile parties.

The bombing privileged the first instrument while degrading the second.

That may feel necessary during war.

It is disastrous as a permanent architecture.

If nuclear non-proliferation depends on national intelligence services bombing what they believe international inspectors cannot see, the regime has already collapsed into competing secret certainty.

The loop closes around verification.

  • Iran hides because it expects attack.
    • The attackers strike because Iran hides.
      • The strikes make further hiding rational.
        • Further hiding makes further strikes politically reachable.

No actor has to be irrational for the system to become fucking insane.

This is why the International Atomic Energy Agency is more than a technical office in this article.

It is the institution trying to keep the loop from owning the material.

Its work is imperfect. It depends on state cooperation. It operates under legal authorities that can be narrow. It can be manipulated, delayed, accused, pressured, and denied. Its reports can be read selectively by every side.

It still supplies the only broadly legitimate instrument capable of returning to the uranium without first winning the war.

Prevention destroyed part of that relation.

The repair must restore it before any political declaration of success can become trustworthy.

LX. The Threshold Hardens

A threshold is supposed to remain between conditions.

That is its strategic value.

Iran can preserve technical capacity without openly possessing the weapon. It can gain bargaining power without accepting the full diplomatic, legal, military, and proliferation consequences of nuclear-weapon status. Its adversaries must consider the possibility of a shorter dash while Iran retains room to deny that a dash has begun.

War hardens the threshold by reducing the value of staying there.

  • Before the attacks, latency could be described as a hedge.
  • After the attacks, latency had failed to protect the facilities, the leadership, the territory, or the inspection relation.

The argument for possession becomes stronger:

States with nuclear weapons are attacked differently.

This is the lesson North Korea offers every threshold state.

It is also the lesson Russia’s war has placed at the center of the Security Instruments track. Existing possession puts a ceiling over correction. The ceiling may not prevent all conflict, sanctions, covert action, or conventional attack. It makes regime-destroying war radically more dangerous.

Iran had now experienced the opposite condition.

It possessed enough threshold capability to invite preventive war and not enough completed deterrent to make preventive war unthinkable.

This is the worst strategic middle.

The attack can therefore produce two opposing conclusions inside Iran.

1. The surrender conclusion

The program has imposed catastrophic cost.

Facilities have been destroyed. Leadership has been killed. The country has faced strikes, sanctions, shipping crisis, energy disruption, and regional war. Nuclear latency did not preserve security. The rational path is deep restraint, permanent inspection, removal or export of enriched material, limits on missiles, and a settlement capable of reopening ordinary continuance.

2. The possession conclusion

The program was attacked because it stopped short.

The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action was reversible. Safeguards did not protect the sites. International law did not prevent attack. A fatwa did not generate trust. Regional partners could impose cost but could not prevent the campaign. The rational path is to acquire a deterrent quickly, secretly, and under military control before another strike closes the path.

Both conclusions have evidence.

The political struggle between them will shape the next nuclear field.

The attackers face their own split:

A. The diplomacy conclusion

Force created delay and leverage. The next step must convert that delay into an inspectable settlement before Iran rebuilds under deeper secrecy.

B. The repetition conclusion

Iran cannot be trusted to remain constrained. Any reconstruction must be attacked. Every denial of access should shorten the military clock. The campaign must become a standing prevention regime.

Both conclusions also have evidence.

The threshold hardens when the possession conclusion and repetition conclusion begin generating one another.

  • Iran moves capability underground because it expects another attack.
    • Israel threatens another attack because capability is moving underground.
  • The United States conditions relief on disposal and access.
    • Iran conditions access on protection from attack and durable relief.
  • Regional partners keep retaliatory routes active.
    • Gulf states seek de-escalation while remaining inside the American security architecture.
      • The International Atomic Energy Agency asks to return to a material field everyone else has turned into leverage.

This is just not stable deterrence.

This is a production line for the weapon.

A settlement must therefore do more than demand that Iran choose peace.

It must construct a path on which restraint is safer than possession and verification is safer than opacity.

Otherwise the war will have taught the threshold exactly what it is for.

LXI. The War’s Ruling

The nuclear threshold became a war before it became a weapon.

Iran built forward defense because the conventional field left it exposed.

The distributed network raised the cost of attack.

It also placed Lebanon, Iraq, Syria, Yemen, Palestine, Israel, the Gulf, the Red Sea, and merchant shipping inside the retaliatory architecture.

The Axis of Resistance preserved contact with real occupation, intervention, domination, and abandonment.

It also gave armed claims a path for consuming the people they claimed to defend.

Sacred memory guarded the revolution against foreign capture.

It also made external correction easier to classify as another form of capture.

The nuclear program remained short of declared possession.

The threshold still acted.

It reorganized sanctions, military planning, alliances, missiles, proxy strategy, enrichment, secrecy, and fear.

Then the facilities were attacked.

The strikes damaged centrifuges, buildings, tunnels, equipment, command, and the program’s ability to continue as before.

They did not erase knowledge. They interrupted inspection.

The inspectors withdrew. The affected inventory entered an unresolved interval. The states conducting the attack claimed success more quickly than the material could be reconstructed.

In February 2026, the war widened again. Iran’s Supreme Leader was killed. Iran retaliated across the region. Hezbollah entered from Lebanon. United States bases and neighboring states became targets. The Strait of Hormuz became leverage over global continuance. Merchant crews became casualties and stranded populations inside a security argument they did not author.

A June memorandum gave the conflict somewhere to go.

It did not yet give the field a durable exit.

As of August 2, another pause in threatened strikes depends on negotiations that have not produced a final agreement. The Strait remains contested. The nuclear inventory remains unresolved. Israel retains a declared willingness to strike renewed capability. Iran continues to treat deterrence as the answer to pressure.

The preventive war did not settle the threshold. It hardened the choice around it.

  • Iran may conclude that only possession prevents repetition.
  • The attackers may conclude that only repetition prevents possession.

Each conclusion generates evidence for the other.

That is the loop any settlement must break.

The question is still not whether Iran should be trusted. It is how to make trust unnecessary without making verification impossible.

The war has added one condition:

Verification must also become safer than the weapon.

The peace must enter the missing inventory, inspector protection, enrichment limits, durable sanctions relief, non-attack guarantees, missile and regional tracks, Israel’s nuclear opacity, Gulf security, internal Iranian freedom, and the architecture required to make restraint more reachable than possession.

The threshold went to war.

Now, the peace has to find the material.

LXII. The Question Is Not Trust

The peace did not have the material.

It had announcements, intermediaries, threats, pauses, memoranda, denials, shipping negotiations, proposed dates, disputed clauses, and several men announcing that another man had agreed to something the other man’s government then said had not happened.

This was not an encouraging replacement for the inspector.

The June 17 Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding gave the nuclear field a narrow route back toward contact. According to the United Nations briefing on the agreement, the United States and Iran had already reached understandings concerning the disposition of enriched material, on-site downblending under International Atomic Energy Agency supervision, and further negotiations over enrichment and Iran’s nuclear needs.

That was real progress.

Then, the wider conflict kept entering the room.

Attacks on shipping, reprisals, strikes, disputed obligations in the Strait of Hormuz, sanctions, regional attacks, threats of renewed bombing, and contradictory public accounts of negotiations repeatedly made the memorandum look less like a bridge and more like one plank being carried through gunfire.

On August 3, the President of the United States said negotiations would begin that afternoon. Iran’s Foreign Ministry said no negotiations were underway and no meeting had been scheduled. Tehran acknowledged discussions with Oman about the Strait of Hormuz, not a renewed nuclear negotiation with Washington.

A negotiation does not exist because one side has announced it.

An agreement does not exist because a leader wants the market, military, electorate, or evening news to behave as though it does.

The threshold has already spent far too many years inside this error.

  • One side announces peaceful intent.
    • Another announces hidden weaponization.
  • One side announces total destruction.
    • Another announces reconstruction.
  • One side announces compliance.
    • Another announces breach.
      • The material remains somewhere beneath the language, carrying whichever answer the field can still prove.

This is why the governing question has never been whether Iran should be trusted.

Trust can help diplomacy begin. It cannot carry a safeguards regime.

Trust changes with elections, assassinations, succession, war, intelligence leaks, domestic scandal, and the next photograph of a damaged building. Trust is a relation among people. A nuclear agreement must survive people who reasonably expect one another to defect.

The opposite of trust is not suspicion.

It is procedure.

  • A declaration that can be checked.
  • A stockpile that can be counted.
  • A camera whose missing hours become a defined event.
  • An access request with a deadline.
  • A denial with a dispute route.
  • Sanctions relief triggered by verified steps rather than mood.
  • A non-attack obligation that remains binding when military planners become impatient.
  • A breach standard known before the breach.
  • A return path after partial failure.

A durable agreement is an argument preloaded with evidence. It assumes that each side will sometimes believe the worst. Then it keeps the field answerable anyway.

The nuclear threshold cannot be repaired by asking Iran, Israel, or the United States to become less historically themselves before entering the room.

The room has to work while all three arrive carrying the histories that made the threshold valuable.

LXIII. The Missing Inventory

The first repair is not a promise.

It is an inventory.

Before the attacks, the International Atomic Energy Agency had a material account. This account was incomplete in important regions. Safeguards questions remained unresolved. Iran had stopped provisionally applying the Additional Protocol. The Agency could not provide the broad assurance it once sought about the absence of undeclared nuclear material and activity.

Still, it knew far more than it knows now.

It knew the reported stockpile. It knew enrichment levels. It had declared facilities, seals, surveillance, sampling histories, production records, and a chain of visits through which one day’s answer could be compared with the next.

The attacks broke this temporal relation.

By June 2026, the Agency reported that it had lost continuity of knowledge across Iran’s declared nuclear facilities. The loss extended beyond enriched uranium. It reached centrifuges, rotors, bellows, heavy water, uranium ore concentrate, and other parts of the production field. Some knowledge, the Agency warned, could not simply be restored by walking back through the door later.

A later photograph does not reveal what moved during the missing year.

A damaged cylinder does not identify its prior contents.

A collapsed hall does not say which centrifuges were destroyed, removed, rebuilt, cannibalized, or buried.

A satellite image can show a truck.

It cannot weigh the uranium inside the argument surrounding the truck.

The settlement must therefore begin with a baseline reconstruction rather than pretending the old baseline survived.

That reconstruction needs to account for:

  • uranium by location, enrichment level, chemical form, and container;
  • material believed destroyed, contaminated, buried, moved, or inaccessible;
  • centrifuge cascades, stored machines, rotors, bellows, and production capacity;
  • feedstock, depleted material, uranium metal, heavy water, and ore concentrate;
  • records damaged or removed during attacks;
  • environmental samples at declared and suspect locations;
  • equipment manufactured during the inspection gap;
  • material and activity connected to unresolved safeguards questions;
  • and the difference between what Iran declares, what the Agency observes, and what remains unknown.

The June memorandum’s reported plan for on-site downblending under Agency supervision is one of the few available transitions with the correct shape.

It does not ask the world to believe the 60-percent stockpile has vanished.

It makes the material enter a visible process that changes what it can quickly become.

That step still depends on finding the stockpile.

You cannot downblend an estimate.

A workable baseline may need to contain openly marked uncertainty bands, contested quantities, inaccessible locations, and a staged process for resolving discrepancies. Demanding a perfect first declaration can make concealment more attractive if every mismatch becomes automatic proof of a final hidden program. Accepting any declaration at face value converts reconstruction into theater.

The agreement must reward fuller disclosure without turning disclosure into immunity for every prior safeguards failure.

This is delicate.

An inventory is evidence, liability, bargaining power, and vulnerability at once. Iran may fear that a precise declaration becomes a targeting package. Its adversaries may fear that an incomplete declaration gives the program time to disappear into new rooms.

Both fears are now supported by history.

The repair instrument has to hold them simultaneously.

The material must become findable without becoming targetable.

The declaration must become correctable without becoming disposable.

The first peace is the restoration of a shared answer to the smallest and hardest question in the entire field:

Where is it?

LXIV. Inspector Safety

The inspector is often described as though this person were a neutral camera with a passport.

But the inspector has a body.

The inspector needs an airport, a road, a vehicle, a hotel, a secure communications channel, protective equipment, radiation information, functioning power, access credentials, interpreters, escorts who do not control the visit, and enough physical stability around the facility that opening the door does not become a wager against another airstrike.

The war made those conditions uncertain.

That fact now belongs to nuclear verification.

Iran cannot be required to produce continuous access while attacks remain available without warning against the facilities, transport routes, scientists, escorts, data systems, and inspectors through which access occurs.

The International Atomic Energy Agency cannot serve as a human shield placed inside a target set. Nor can monitored status become immunity beneath which Iran hides military activity unrelated to the safeguarded program.

The protected object has to be defined.

A renewed agreement should create a special non-attack regime around declared and monitored nuclear facilities. That regime should include:

  • coordinates and facility boundaries agreed in advance;
  • protected access routes and inspection windows;
  • rapid deconfliction channels;
  • notification of nearby military operations;
  • independent radiation and structural-risk assessment;
  • secure evacuation procedures;
  • protection for Agency equipment, seals, records, and communications;
  • and immediate investigation if a monitored site, inspection team, or access route is attacked.

The obligations have to run in both directions.

Iran must not use safeguarded facilities as cover for unrelated military assets, command functions, or weapon storage. It must provide accurate safety information and refrain from using access procedures to expose inspectors to avoidable danger. Attacking states must not treat the Agency’s knowledge of a site as assistance with target development. Intelligence services must not impersonate inspectors or convert inspection access into covert action.

Those boundaries sound so obvious.

A long history of sabotage, assassination, covert procurement, military attack, hidden facilities, and manipulated access has made obvious boundaries so expensive.

Inspection is an invitation into vulnerability.

A state opens doors, identifies equipment, reveals routes, discloses inventories, and allows outsiders to learn how the system is built. If that disclosure makes attack easier, the next state considering transparency will learn the wrong lesson.

A camera cannot verify through smoke.

A seal cannot survive as evidence if every party reserves the right to destroy the room holding it.

Inspector safety is therefore not an administrative detail beneath the real nuclear agreement. It is one of the agreement’s nuclear limits.

Without safe return, continuity cannot return.

Without continuity, the threshold remains governed by intelligence estimates, public threats, and whichever leader reaches the microphone first.

LXV. Zero Enrichment

The strongest restriction is easy to state.

Iran enriches no uranium.

No operating enrichment cascades. No domestic stockpile capable of rapid further enrichment. Reactor fuel supplied from abroad under long-term guarantees. Spent fuel removed. Research reorganized around activities that do not reproduce the threshold.

This arrangement would radically simplify verification.

The inspector would no longer need to distinguish permitted low enrichment from forbidden higher enrichment within the same industrial architecture. Advanced centrifuge development would no longer sit one policy decision away from rapid production. The material threshold would move farther from the weapon threshold. Israel, the Gulf states, the United States, and other actors would receive a clearer signal than any declaration of peaceful intent can provide.

The case for zero enrichment became stronger after continuity was lost.

It also became harder to implement after the facilities were attacked.

Iran’s nuclear program has been built into national sovereignty, scientific achievement, resistance to technological hierarchy, and the claim that pressure cannot determine which capacities Iranians may possess. The United States left the prior agreement while the Agency was still verifying Iranian performance of its nuclear restrictions. Israel and the United States then attacked safeguarded facilities. External suppliers have previously been experienced as political dependencies rather than neutral service providers.

In that field, zero enrichment does not arrive as a technical adjustment.

It arrives as forced dependency after war.

That does not make the proposal invalid.

It reveals what the proposal would have to carry.

A credible zero-enrichment settlement would require more than an Iranian shutdown. It would need:

  • guaranteed fuel supply through more than one provider;
  • a legally protected reserve against interruption;
  • automatic compensation for politically motivated denial;
  • international participation in civilian nuclear research;
  • verified decommissioning rather than destruction;
  • durable sanctions relief;
  • non-attack guarantees;
  • and a dispute process that prevents one supplier or one election from turning fuel into leverage.

An arrangement that demands full dependency from a state whose security story is organized around foreign denial is structurally brittle.

The arrangement can still work if dependency is replaced with shared obligation.

Fuel would have to be supplied as an enforceable part of the settlement, not a favor renewed by the same governments that retain the power to revoke banking access, insurance, shipping, and technical support.

Zero enrichment may remain the clearest route away from breakout capacity.

Clarity alone does not make a route reachable.

If the requirement is imposed without an answer to Iranian vulnerability, the agreement will teach the state to preserve hidden capacity against the day supply is withdrawn.

The zero must therefore apply to coercive leverage too.

LXVI. Capped Domestic Enrichment

The alternative keeps a domestic fuel cycle and makes the threshold wider in time.

Iran could retain limited enrichment at a very low level for declared civilian needs while surrendering the stockpile, scale, machine inventory, and flexibility that made rapid escalation possible.

The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action demonstrated the basic architecture:

  • a low enrichment ceiling;
  • a small stockpile;
  • strict limits on operating centrifuges;
  • restrictions at Fordow;
  • redesign of the Arak reactor;
  • monitoring of centrifuge manufacturing and uranium supply;
  • and removal or dilution of excess material.

A successor agreement would have to be stronger where the earlier instrument was temporary or vulnerable.

The enrichment ceiling should remain far below 20 and 60 percent. The permitted stockpile should be tied to defined civilian use rather than a general right to accumulate. Surplus material should leave Iran or be downblended under Agency observation. Advanced centrifuge research and manufacturing need declared limits, monitored inventories, and a rule preventing stored machines from becoming an uncounted reserve. Uranium metal work requires special restraint because it can enter weaponization concerns even when enrichment remains the public argument.

The time horizon matters.

  • A short agreement delays the threshold and schedules the next panic.
  • A durable agreement should place core verification and stockpile limits on a long or indefinite basis, while allowing carefully staged review of industrial restrictions.
    • Review is not automatic expiration.
    • The limit should change only after verified performance, regional conditions, and a negotiated successor arrangement make change safe.

The purpose is not to produce one holy breakout estimate.

Breakout is one compressed measure inside a longer sequence. The deeper goal is to make warning time a public institution.

If Iran changes enrichment level, installs prohibited machines, obstructs monitoring, moves material, or begins activities inconsistent with the declared program, the field should reveal the transition early enough that diplomacy, inspection, and reversible pressure remain available.

Capped enrichment has one advantage over zero enrichment:

  • Iran retains enough scientific and industrial agency that compliance does not appear as complete surrender of the fuel cycle.

It has one major danger:

  • The threshold remains physically present.

The agreement therefore has to make the threshold less useful. A larger hidden stockpile, rapid machine installation, surveillance interruption, or unexplained material movement must produce predictable consequences before the event can be normalized as another bargaining step.

The instrument succeeds when domestic enrichment stops functioning as emergency leverage.

It does not need to erase Iranian technical knowledge.

It needs to keep that knowledge from becoming a short road to an uninspectable weapon.

LXVII. Multinational Enrichment

A third route shares the fuel cycle.

A multinational enrichment consortium could place Iranian technicians, foreign participants, international oversight, and shared custody inside one operating institution. The facility might remain in Iran, exist elsewhere in the region, or divide production, fuel fabrication, and storage across more than one site.

The attraction is clear.

Iran avoids total dependence on an external supplier. Other participants gain continuous presence inside the program. Ownership, records, procurement, staffing, and material custody no longer belong to one state alone. A change in enrichment level becomes an institutional event involving several parties rather than a national switch moved behind a closed door.

Shared participation can make concealment harder.

It can also create new forms of capture.

  • Who appoints management?
  • Who controls physical security?
  • Who can suspend production?
  • What happens if one participant withdraws?
  • Can Iran expel foreign staff during a crisis?
  • Can another government block fuel delivery for political reasons?
  • Who owns the centrifuges?
  • Where is excess material stored?
  • What happens after sabotage?
  • Which court or commission decides whether an incident was technical failure, covert action, or breach?

A consortium can become shared custody.

It can also become a shared hostage.

The design therefore needs clear separation among national authority, facility management, International Atomic Energy Agency safeguards, and dispute resolution. No state should possess unilateral control over both operation and evidence. Procurement records should be visible to all participants. Material accounting should remain under Agency authority. Emergency shutdown should require defined conditions. Withdrawal should trigger an orderly transition rather than instant national possession of the entire system.

The consortium should also avoid becoming a prestige enclosure for governments while Iranian universities, workers, and the wider public receive the costs without meaningful participation. Scientific cooperation can expand the field if it creates legitimate civilian pathways. It can contract the field if every technical opportunity passes through security institutions already dominant in the nuclear program.

Multinational enrichment is often treated as a clever compromise waiting for reasonable people.

It is an institution.

Institutions need budgets, law, staffing, succession, liability, security, transparency, and a theory of how they survive the first serious dispute.

The consortium works only if shared control remains more reliable than national breakout and more dignified than permanent dependency.

That is a demanding shape.

The threshold has made easy shapes unavailable.

LXVIII. The Additional Protocol

The Additional Protocol is less dramatic than a bomber.

This is one of its clear advantages.

Under a comprehensive safeguards agreement, the International Atomic Energy Agency verifies declared nuclear material and activities. The Additional Protocol expands the information and access available to the Agency so it can investigate a broader field: research and development, uranium mining and milling, centrifuge manufacturing, locations beyond declared nuclear facilities, and indicators of undeclared activity.

Iran provisionally applied the Additional Protocol under the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action and stopped doing so in February 2021.

The loss became more severe after the attacks.

The Agency now lacks continuity across declared facilities and lacks the expanded declarations and complementary access needed to reconstruct the wider production field. Returning only to the old facility doors would therefore restore too little.

A successor agreement should require ratification and continuing implementation of the Additional Protocol, along with the modified Code 3.1 obligation for early provision of design information on new nuclear facilities.

That sounds procedural.

It changes reachability.

Early design information lets the Agency enter before a facility becomes a completed surprise. Complementary access lets inspectors test whether a suspicious location belongs to the declared story. Environmental sampling can preserve evidence after equipment has moved. Monitoring centrifuge production helps distinguish a damaged program from a hidden reserve built during the gap.

Access disputes must have a short, defined route.

Iran should receive the exact basis for a request and safeguards against fishing expeditions unrelated to the nuclear file. The Agency should receive timely access or a rapid adjudication process that cannot be delayed until evidence is gone. A joint commission can manage procedure without gaining the authority to overrule technical findings for political convenience.

The agreement should also protect confidential information. Safeguards data must not become public spectacle, commercial theft, political leaking, or military targeting support. Inspectors need independence from national intelligence services and credible consequences for misuse of protected information.

The instrument must be allowed to find the question before the question is already a crisis. That is the deep purpose of the Additional Protocol.

It does not prove innocence forever.

It makes hidden activity harder to carry through time without contact.

A threshold program lives in the gap between capability and declared purpose.

The Additional Protocol narrows the gap by making more of the production field answerable.

LXIX. Sanctions Relief That Can Survive One Election

Iran learned one devastating lesson from the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action.

  • Compliance did not guarantee continuity.

The United States withdrew in 2018 while the International Atomic Energy Agency was still reporting Iranian implementation of the nuclear restrictions. Sanctions returned. European governments opposed the withdrawal and could not reproduce the economic exchange Iran expected. The agreement’s nuclear limits remained technically available while the economic path supporting them disappeared.

The centrifuges learned to wait for the next election.

Any successor settlement that depends on presidential waivers, executive discretion, fragile banking guidance, and companies willing to risk later punishment will reproduce the same failure.

Sanctions relief must become as inspectable as enrichment.

That requires defined economic transitions:

  • specific assets released after verified nuclear steps;
  • oil-sale and shipping permissions that activate automatically;
  • protected payment channels capable of ordinary commercial use;
  • clear licensing for civilian nuclear cooperation, medicine, food, aviation, and infrastructure;
  • multilateral reporting on whether relief has actually reached the permitted transactions;
  • and predefined consequences if a party restores sanctions without an independently verified material breach.

The legal form matters.

Where legislation is required, the agreement should seek legislation rather than hide every obligation inside temporary executive authority. Where full repeal is politically unreachable, longer waivers, statutory safe harbors, escrow mechanisms, and multilateral guarantees can reduce the ability of one administration to erase performance already delivered by another.

Snapback also needs repair.

The old mechanism was designed to prevent a violator from vetoing the return of sanctions. That strength also made the process vulnerable to contested legal use and political interpretation. A renewed mechanism should distinguish urgent temporary measures from final restoration, require a technical finding, and preserve a path for correction before the whole agreement is consumed.

Sanctions are not switches outside the field.

They alter currency, medicine, insurance, shipping, food prices, employment, corruption, smuggling, elite consolidation, and the relative power of institutions already skilled at surviving isolation. Relief can likewise strengthen the state sectors best positioned to capture it.

The agreement therefore needs distributional monitoring. Civilian exchange should become easier before security institutions receive unrestricted access to capital. Humanitarian exemptions must function in practice rather than exist as sentences banks remain too frightened to use.

  • A deal that can be erased by one election is a pause with stationery.
  • A deal that delivers no visible economic change teaches Iranian citizens that inspection purchases nothing.

The exchange must survive long enough for compliance to become materially real.

LXX. Non-Attack Guarantees

Iran cannot be expected to make its program maximally visible while every visible site remains an available target.

This is not a claim that verification should purchase immunity for the Islamic Republic.

It is a recognition that inspection and attack pull the field in opposite directions.

Inspection asks Iran to identify facilities, material, equipment, personnel, routes, vulnerabilities, and production relationships. Preventive war turns the same information into an operational map. If a state complies and is struck anyway, secrecy becomes a rational defensive instrument.

The agreement needs a defined non-attack guarantee for declared and monitored nuclear facilities, associated inspection routes, and material movements conducted under Agency supervision.

The guarantee should bind Iran too.

Protected facilities cannot be used for nuclear weaponization, unrelated military command, missile storage, or concealment of forces that would turn monitored status into a shield. Iran must not attack inspectors, tamper with Agency equipment, or create a manufactured safety crisis to block access. If protected status is abused, an independent investigation should determine the breach before military response becomes automatic.

The guarantee cannot rest solely on an American statement.

Israel has repeatedly acted outside agreements to which it was not a party when it judged the threat intolerable. Gulf states, whose territory and airspace can become routes or targets, also belong to the security field. A viable instrument therefore needs direct or indirect commitments from every actor capable of destroying the verification architecture.

That may require:

  • a Security Council or multilateral guarantee;
  • formal Israeli participation in a non-attack arrangement, even if Israel does not join the wider nuclear agreement;
  • regional commitments against facilitating strikes on monitored facilities;
  • rapid investigation of suspected breach;
  • and a defined sequence of diplomatic, economic, and defensive responses before attack.

A non-attack guarantee is not a promise that force can never become lawful under any condition.

It is a rule that force cannot substitute for verification while verification remains available.

  • If Iran expels inspectors, diverts safeguarded material, begins prohibited weaponization, or places a protected facility outside the agreed regime, the field changes.
    • The agreement should already know what consequences follow.
  • If another party attacks while Iran remains in verified compliance, that is also a breach with consequences.

This symmetry is essential.

Restraint cannot be a duty imposed on the threshold state while preventive power remains discretionary for everyone else.

Inspection is an invitation into vulnerability.

The agreement must prevent vulnerability from becoming target acquisition.

LXXI. Missiles

A nuclear device and a deliverable nuclear force are different objects.

Missiles connect them.

Iran’s missile program also exists independently of the nuclear question. Missiles compensate for limited conventional airpower, support regional deterrence, threaten bases and infrastructure, and have been used in direct and partner warfare. They are central to Iranian security doctrine even if every gram of enriched uranium is downblended tomorrow.

This is why the missile file cannot simply be ignored.

It is also why total missile disarmament cannot be loaded into the nuclear agreement as the price of restoring inspectors.

A state facing Israel’s airpower, United States forces, Gulf arsenals, and repeated attacks will not surrender its principal conventional deterrent while receiving uncertain protection in return. Making that surrender a precondition would keep the nuclear material invisible while negotiators argue over the entire regional balance.

The tracks should be linked and differentiated.

The nuclear agreement can prohibit work specifically associated with nuclear delivery and require declarations concerning systems relevant to the agreement’s weaponization constraints. A parallel missile arrangement can address:

  • test notification;
  • launch hotlines;
  • geographic safety zones;
  • transfer of missiles and production systems to armed partners;
  • attacks on civilian infrastructure and shipping;
  • limits on certain ranges or payload classes if reciprocal regional limits become reachable;
  • and transparency around exercises that adversaries may misread as preparation for a first strike.

Reciprocity remains difficult.

Iran will ask why exactly its missiles are treated as uniquely destabilizing while Israeli strike aircraft, Gulf air forces, United States bases, and other regional missile inventories remain available. Its adversaries will answer that Iran’s missile use, threats, and partner transfers create a distinct field.

Both claims contain real structure. A regional missile process should therefore move toward common notification and restraint rather than permanent exceptional rules for one state.

The missile file must be linked strongly enough that a nominally peaceful nuclear program cannot prepare a usable weapon system outside the agreement.

It must remain separated enough that one stalled argument over conventional deterrence does not destroy the material account.

The threshold has already shown what happens when every grievance is placed on one narrow bridge.

The bridge collapses.

LXXII. Proxies and Partners

Iran cannot sign for Lebanon.

It cannot sign for Yemen, Iraq, Palestine, Syria, or every armed organization with which it has built a relationship.

This is important because great-power diplomacy loves to turn local actors into clauses.

A government promises to restrain a partner. The partner receives no seat. The local conflict remains unresolved. When the partner acts, the agreement is declared broken. Civilians living around the organization become the enforcement surface for a bargain made elsewhere.

Iranian influence is still real.

Iran can restrict weapons transfers, financing, training, intelligence support, targeting assistance, and political encouragement. It can stop using partner forces as distributed retaliation for attacks on Iranian interests. It can support ceasefires, demobilization arrangements, state integration, and local political processes rather than treating permanent armed autonomy as regional depth.

The United States, Israel, Gulf states, and other external actors carry reciprocal obligations.

They can reduce airstrikes, covert action, proxy funding, blockade, occupation, and support for armed groups aligned against Iran. They can stop treating every local government as a security platform in the competition with Tehran. Regional states can protect their sovereignty without allowing their territory to become an undeclared launch surface.

The nuclear agreement should therefore include a regional non-escalation commitment with defined categories:

  • no attacks on commercial shipping;
  • no attacks on diplomatic facilities;
  • no partner attacks on nuclear or energy infrastructure;
  • no transfer of systems prohibited by the parallel missile arrangement;
  • and rapid attribution procedures before retaliation expands.

Then the local conflicts need local instruments.

Lebanon requires a Lebanese settlement. Yemen requires a Yemeni settlement. Gaza and the wider Palestinian field require political repair that cannot be reduced to Iranian policy. Iraqi armed groups belong inside Iraq’s contested sovereignty. Syria cannot become one more corridor discussed only by external powers.

No peace architecture can obtain regional quiet by making local peoples silent.

The Axis of Resistance developed partly because real wounds were left without effective repair. Disarming the axis while preserving occupation, siege, domination, and abandoned political claims would leave the recruitment field intact.

Allowing every wound to authorize permanent armed leverage consumes the people the resistance claims to defend.

The regional track must therefore reduce both abandonment and consumption.

  • Iran’s partners are not chess pieces.
  • They are also not exemptions from Iranian responsibility.

LXXIII. Israel’s Nuclear Opacity

The regional field contains a nuclear arsenal that is rarely permitted to become an ordinary sentence.

Israel does not publicly confirm or deny possession. It remains outside the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. Its nuclear opacity supplies deterrent value while avoiding the inspection demands placed on Iran as a non-nuclear-weapon state party to the treaty.

This asymmetry does not erase Iran’s safeguards obligations.

Iran accepted those obligations. Undeclared material, restricted access, highly enriched uranium, and the use of latency as leverage remain auditable regardless of Israel’s status.

The asymmetry still shapes every Iranian argument about permanent restraint.

A regional order cannot indefinitely demand that Iran prove absence while allowing Israel to receive strategic value from refusing to declare presence. The arrangement will continue teaching Iranian security institutions that treaty participation creates vulnerability while opacity creates power.

The immediate nuclear agreement does not have to solve Israeli disarmament before inspectors return to Iran.

Making that the first condition would ensure that neither field moves.

The asymmetry needs an active repair horizon:

  • regional participation in the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty and movement toward its entry into force;
  • declarations and transparency measures on fissile material;
  • support for negotiations toward a Middle East zone free of nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction;
  • regional nuclear-risk-reduction talks;
  • and eventual movement from opacity toward accountable restraint.

Israel’s strongest defense must be stated.

It faces actors and movements that have denied its legitimacy, attacked its civilians, and called for its destruction. It exists in a region with repeated war, weak collective-security guarantees, and profound uncertainty about the durability of political settlements.

Opacity was built as an answer to vulnerability.

That is exactly why it belongs in this track.

A security wound can explain an instrument without granting the instrument permanent sovereignty over everyone else’s future.

Israel’s arsenal contributes to Iran’s threshold incentive. Iran’s threshold contributes to Israeli prevention doctrine. Each side cites the other’s danger while protecting the instrument that makes the danger credible.

A durable settlement has to begin lowering that reciprocal necessity.

The goal is not equal exposure. It is equal movement toward a field where nuclear ambiguity is less valuable than verified restraint.

LXXIV. The Gulf Security Forum

The Gulf already has a security architecture.

It communicates through bases, interception, sanctions, naval patrols, drone attacks, intelligence liaison, air-defense purchases, partner forces, emergency diplomacy, and the price of oil.

The architecture is active.

It is simply terrible at speaking before something explodes.

A standing Gulf security forum would give the regional field an ordinary room.

The forum should include Iran, the Gulf Cooperation Council states, Iraq, and other directly affected regional actors, with participation or support from Oman, Qatar, the United Nations, and relevant maritime and nuclear institutions. The exact membership can evolve. The essential move is to create a durable institution where incidents do not need to become military messages before they become discussable.

Its agenda should remain practical:

  • maritime incident notification;
  • safe-passage procedures;
  • mine and unexploded-ordnance response;
  • hotlines among naval and air-defense commands;
  • identification of drones and missiles crossing regional airspace;
  • notice of large exercises and launches;
  • protection of nuclear and energy infrastructure;
  • environmental response to attacks on tankers, refineries, and reactors;
  • treatment and evacuation of civilian seafarers;
  • base-access transparency;
  • non-interference complaints;
  • and procedures for independent attribution after an attack.

The forum should not require political friendship.

It should be designed for governments that expect manipulation.

Oman and Qatar have repeatedly acted as intermediaries because the field needs actors able to carry messages across hostile relations. Mediation is valuable. A region cannot depend forever on emergency courier work by whichever state still answers both phones.

The Strait of Hormuz makes the need obvious.

A maritime disagreement there enters global energy prices, insurance, shipping schedules, civilian employment, food costs, and military posture within hours. The waterway cannot remain a lever that every conflict may pull.

A safe-passage framework needs common rules, neutral observation, and an agreed response to attacks or interdiction. Iran’s legitimate role as a littoral state does not create title over global commercial passage. Outside naval power does not create title over Iranian waters or the wider Gulf security order.

The forum’s deepest function is continuity.

  • A crisis meeting ends when the crisis leaves the headlines.
  • A standing institution retains the minutes, unresolved claims, technical contacts, and small agreements through which the next crisis may become less terminal.

The Gulf does not need one more grand peace photograph.

It needs a room that stays open.

LXXV. Internal Iran Is Not a Bargaining Chip

A nuclear agreement may preserve millions of Iranian lives.

It does not certify the Islamic Republic as just.

Women remain subject to coercive law and enforcement. Dissenters, journalists, labor organizers, students, religious minorities, ethnic minorities, political prisoners, and families of the dead remain inside a state capable of treating correction as foreign penetration. Internet shutdowns, executions, surveillance, detention, and force do not become secondary because the centrifuge account has improved.

These fields must remain visible without being fused into one coercive demand.

The United States and its partners should not condition every nuclear transition on regime transformation, maximal human-rights performance, regional capitulation, and a final settlement of Iranian political life. That package would make nuclear visibility unreachable and give the security state further evidence that inspection is one stage of regime change.

The nuclear agreement also cannot become a silence purchase.

Sanctions relief should not require foreign governments, international organizations, or Iranian civil society to stop naming repression. Nuclear compliance does not grant immunity from human-rights monitoring, targeted sanctions against responsible officials, asylum protection, independent reporting, or support for ordinary communication.

The distinction runs both ways.

Internal repression does not authorize attacks on nuclear facilities, collective economic punishment, or a war designed to produce political collapse. A repressive government still governs a field full of people who can be killed, displaced, impoverished, irradiated, or made more dependent on the same institutions outsiders claim to weaken.

The relief architecture should therefore be designed to reach civilian life.

  • Banking channels that ordinary firms can use.
  • Medicine and medical equipment without impossible compliance risk.
  • Aviation safety.
  • Education and scientific exchange outside prohibited activity.
  • Communications access.
  • Reconstruction of civilian infrastructure.
  • Transparent reporting on who receives released assets.

A nuclear agreement can preserve Iranian continuance without endorsing the state that governs it.

That is the correct moral shape.

The Iranian people are not leverage for obtaining a better nuclear bargain.

They are not leverage for preserving the regime either.

The threshold belongs to the state.

The field carrying its cost is much larger.

LXXVI. The Fatwa and the Inspector

The fatwa can stay.

So can the inspector.

Ayatollah Ali Khamenei’s prohibition against the production and use of nuclear weapons has been presented as a religious and political commitment of the Islamic Republic. That commitment belongs in the architecture as an internal restraint. A state whose governing legitimacy is religious should be asked to let its sacred prohibition bind policy, doctrine, law, education, and succession.

The inspector does different work.

The inspector does not determine whether the weapon is sinful.

The inspector determines whether material, equipment, and activity remain consistent with the declared program.

These instruments should cooperate without impersonating one another.

  • The fatwa cannot answer for a missing cylinder.
  • The inspector cannot manufacture an Iranian moral consensus against nuclear possession.

A durable settlement could formalize the sacred prohibition through domestic legislation, public doctrine, and institutional obligations that survive one leader. Parliament, jurists, military authorities, scientists, and the wider public should be able to cite the prohibition against a future attempt to cross the weapon threshold.

That internal commitment still requires external evidence.

The history of Sacred Instruments supplies the rule:

A sacred instrument expands the field when it returns the agent to extance with more care and greater availability to correction.

The prohibition therefore proves itself partly by welcoming verification.

  • If the weapon is forbidden, evidence of non-possession should strengthen the sacred claim rather than insult it.
  • If inspectors identify unexplained material or activity, the religious commitment should require correction rather than demand immunity from the question.

The inspector also needs correction.

Agency findings can be limited, delayed, politicized in reception, or interpreted beyond what the evidence establishes. National intelligence can be wrong. Sampling can produce anomalies that require investigation rather than instant war. The agreement should preserve technical review, independent analysis, and a distinction between unresolved evidence and completed guilt.

No instrument receives the throne.

  • The fatwa binds the state internally.
  • Safeguards bind the material externally.
  • Law defines obligation.
  • Inspection supplies contact.
  • Public contestability exposes contradiction.
  • Diplomacy keeps correction from becoming attack.

The sacred and technical can interoperate.

They become dangerous when either demands exclusive authority over reality.

LXXVII. The Verification Matrix

A serious agreement must know how it fails.

Most political documents know how success is announced. They contain signing ceremonies, implementation days, staged relief, and sentences about mutual respect. Failure arrives later as improvisation.

The threshold cannot afford improvisation.

The agreement needs a verification matrix applied to every major obligation:

  • What nuclear material is declared?
  • What material remains missing or uncertain?
  • Which facilities are accessible?
  • How quickly must access occur?
  • What evidence supports a request?
  • What happens after denial?
  • How is the post-attack baseline reconstructed?
  • Who verifies material believed destroyed?
  • How are centrifuges, rotors, bellows, and new production counted?
  • What monitoring survives temporary evacuation?
  • How is inspector safety protected?
  • Which enrichment activities are permitted?
  • What happens to excess stock?
  • Which sanctions are lifted after each verified step?
  • What proof shows that relief has actually reached permitted trade?
  • What military action counts as breach?
  • What happens if a monitored facility is attacked?
  • What happens if Iran withdraws from the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty?
  • What happens if another party leaves the agreement without a verified Iranian breach?
  • Can Iran correct a safeguards discrepancy before the entire settlement collapses?
  • Can other parties correct a sanctions or non-attack breach before Iran resumes escalation?
  • Who adjudicates intelligence claims that the Agency cannot confirm?
  • What data becomes public, protected, or escrowed?
  • Can the arrangement survive leadership succession?
  • Does each step reduce the strategic value of actual weapon possession?

The matrix must be reciprocal.

Iranian obligations will be more intrusive because the nuclear material is in Iran and the safeguards questions concern Iran. Reciprocity does not require identical tasks. It requires that every party’s performance become visible and consequential.

If Iran downblends material, the Agency verifies it.

If the United States releases assets or licenses trade, an independent body verifies whether the promised channels function.

If a facility is attacked, a defined investigation begins.

If Iran denies access, the clock begins.

If intelligence alleges hidden weaponization, the allegation enters a technical route before becoming a bombing justification.

The matrix should distinguish anomaly, non-performance, and material breach.

  • An anomaly is evidence requiring investigation.
  • Non-performance is failure to complete an obligation within the agreed process.
  • Material breach is a transition serious enough to threaten the agreement’s central purpose.

Collapsing those categories invites strategic panic. Treating all three as harmless invites concealment.

The agreement must give each event the correct amount of machinery.

That is how trust becomes unnecessary.

The field does not have to believe the parties are good.

It has to know what happened next.

LXXVIII. Contestability

The agreement cannot depend on one Supreme Leader.

It cannot depend on one American president, one Israeli prime minister, one International Atomic Energy Agency director general, one Omani mediator, one intelligence estimate, one parliamentary majority, or one military commander deciding to remain patient.

Every one of those actors will change.

The instrument must survive succession.

Contestability means the agreement contains lawful routes for disagreement without forcing disagreement to become abandonment.

  • Inspectors can request review of disputed evidence.
  • Iran can challenge the scope or handling of an access request without erasing the access obligation.
  • Other parties can allege breach without unilaterally converting allegation into final judgment.
  • Sanctions relief can be audited.
  • Non-attack guarantees can be investigated.
  • Regional states can report violations.
  • Civil society and technical experts can identify implementation failures.
  • Legislatures can debate the agreement without being able to erase completed exchange through one symbolic vote.
  • A successor government can seek amendment through procedure rather than withdrawal by announcement.

The Joint Commission model from the prior agreement offers part of this structure. A successor institution needs stronger independence, clearer timelines, and a protected technical role for the International Atomic Energy Agency. Political participants should negotiate consequences. They should not vote a material fact into or out of existence.

Contestability also requires public discipline.

Some data must remain confidential for security and safeguards reasons. Enough aggregate information should still be published that citizens, legislatures, researchers, and affected states can tell whether the instrument is operating. Secrecy cannot swallow the entire field and leave only leaders announcing victory.

The August disagreement over whether negotiations had even been scheduled is a small warning.

  • A leader announced talks.
  • The other state said there were none.
    • Markets moved.
    • Military action paused.
      • The underlying relation remained uncertain.

A press conference cannot be the protocol.

The agreement should preserve an official common calendar, written agendas, confirmed participants, authenticated records, and public notices when sessions occur or obligations activate.

This sounds almost embarrassingly administrative after five months of war.

Good. Administration is what keeps reality from being governed entirely by threat theater. A system with no route to challenge its own answer has already become a security instrument sovereign over correction.

That is the failure this entire track has been approaching.

The settlement must remain capable of being wrong without becoming dead.

LXXIX. No Threshold Peace

A threshold peace would stop the shooting and preserve the structure that made the shooting likely.

Iran would retain an unverified or rapidly rebuildable capability. Israel and the United States would retain preventive attack as an active doctrine. Sanctions would remain available as emergency leverage. The Strait of Hormuz would remain a regional bargaining surface. Armed partners would remain distributed retaliation. Inspectors would return only where the next crisis permitted. Every side would call the pause proof that pressure worked.

Then, the threshold would begin generating the next war.

No Threshold Peace means the settlement cannot freeze ambiguity and call the absence of bombing repair.

The threshold has to become:

  • visible enough that material movement is detected;
  • wide enough in time that diplomacy can act before panic;
  • less useful as Iranian leverage;
  • less necessary for Iranian security;
  • safer to inspect than to hide;
  • harder to attack without consequence;
  • and connected to a regional field where retaliation does not automatically travel through civilians, shipping, and partner territories.

This requires several instruments operating together.

The inventory finds the material.

Downblending or removal changes what the material can quickly become.

Enrichment limits slow the path.

The Additional Protocol makes the wider production field more visible.

Sanctions relief makes compliance materially legible.

Non-attack guarantees keep visibility from becoming vulnerability.

Missile and regional tracks reduce the wider deterrence pressure.

A Gulf forum gives conflict somewhere to go before it reaches the target list.

Internal contestability prevents the Iranian state from treating every correction as foreign capture.

Regional nuclear dialogue begins addressing the asymmetry that makes threshold status attractive.

No single instrument can carry the field.

This is why the next agreement cannot be a larger slogan about peace.

It needs several narrow machines with clear handoffs.

One verifies material. One delivers relief. One protects access. One manages maritime incidents. One addresses missiles. One holds regional armed relationships. One preserves human-rights scrutiny. One opens the longer route toward regional nuclear restraint.

The architecture should be complex because the field is complex.

Complexity is dangerous when it hides responsibility. It becomes useful when each component has one job, one evidence relation, and one correction path.

As of August 3, 2026, the diplomatic field remained unstable. The United States says talks are available. Iran says no direct nuclear talks are underway. Oman continues discussions over the Strait. The inventory remains unresolved. Threatened strikes have been paused before and resumed before.

No article can supply a completed peace that the world has not produced.

It can identify the shape the peace must have.

  • The material becomes visible.
  • Restraint becomes reciprocal.
  • Correction becomes safer than attack.
  • Possession becomes less useful than compliance.

That is the path away from the threshold.

Anything smaller is an interval.

LXXX. The Ruling

Iran did not need to possess a nuclear weapon for the weapon to begin acting.

Enrichment reorganized sanctions.

Ambiguity reorganized war plans.

The fatwa reorganized the state’s public moral claim.

The Axis of Resistance reorganized distance.

The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action reorganized visibility.

The United States withdrawal reorganized Iranian confidence in exchange.

Iran’s nuclear escalation reorganized breakout time.

The attacks reorganized inspection.

The threshold became leverage.

Iran’s security fears are real. The 1953 coup is real. The Shah’s security machine is real. The Iran-Iraq War, chemical attack, assassinations, sabotage, sanctions, Israeli nuclear opacity, and repeated military threats all belong to the field.

Those wounds do not grant the Islamic Republic title over its neighbors, its partners, the Strait of Hormuz, or the Iranian people.

The religious prohibition against nuclear weapons matters. It does not answer for missing material.

Iran’s safeguards failures matter. They do not turn preventive war into inspection by other means.

The Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action succeeded where it made trust unnecessary. It counted material, limited machines, extended time, and gave sanctions relief an observable exchange. It failed where temporary limits, fragile economic delivery, regional conflict, and unilateral exit were allowed to consume the narrow instrument keeping the threshold visible.

Then the facilities were attacked.

Structures were damaged. Inspectors left. Records broke. Material entered uncertainty. The action intended to prevent the weapon made the threshold harder to measure and possession easier to justify.

The repair is therefore not one demand. It is an architecture:

  • reconstruct the inventory;
  • protect the inspectors;
  • downblend, remove, or tightly cap the material;
  • restore expanded access;
  • make sanctions relief durable;
  • protect monitored facilities from attack;
  • separate the missile and regional tracks without abandoning them;
  • keep Israel’s nuclear opacity inside the long-term account;
  • preserve Iranian civil society from both domestic repression and foreign instrumentalization;
  • and build procedures strong enough to survive leaders who distrust one another.

Russia showed how possession places a ceiling over correction.

Iran shows that the approach to possession can reorganize the field before the weapon arrives.

The threshold deters. It invites attack. The attack strengthens the case for possession. The possibility of possession strengthens the case for attack.

That loop is the first form of the nuclear veto. The veto begins before the bomb.

The next article enters the instrument that places civilization inside the threat.
