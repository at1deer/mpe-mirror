## **Minimal Background.**

Pokémon is a turn-based battle game in which players assemble teams of creatures with different types, abilities, statistics, and moves. Competitive Pokémon communities study which creatures and strategies work against one another, build formal rulesets, run tournaments, and develop what is usually called a **metagame**: the shifting field of common teams, expected threats, available responses, and strategic adaptations produced by continued play.

**Smogon University**, usually just called **Smogon**, is an unofficial fan-run competitive Pokémon community. It is not owned by Nintendo, Game Freak, or The Pokémon Company. Its authority exists because players continue to use its analyses, formats, forums, tiering systems, and the Pokémon Showdown battle simulator.

The **Create-A-Pokémon Project**, shortened to **CAP**, is one institution inside that wider community.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1225.png)

Its own mission statement describes the project as a community dedicated to exploring and understanding competitive Pokémon by designing, creating, and playtesting new Pokémon concepts. The resulting creatures are implemented for real play on Pokémon Showdown, where they occupy a dedicated CAP competitive environment alongside official Pokémon.

CAP is not primarily a character-design contest.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1429.png)

The community is attempting to learn something about competitive play by building a new object into the existing field and then seeing what happens.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1227.png)

The first public proposal appeared on November 27, 2007. Cooper suggested that Smogon could run a months-long community creation project in which regular forum members voted on each part of a Pokémon. The thread’s opening poll offered two possible answers:

-   _Yes, I will vote whenever I can._
-   **No, this idea is stupid/premature, as are you.**

One hundred fifty-six people selected the first option. 

Twenty-five selected the second.

The opposition had a defensible concern.

Great Sage predicted that the project would become:

> **“rampant with useless subjectivity”**

and produce

> **“ridiculous debates over the most insignificant things.”**

Great Sage was correct.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1228.png)

The project began anyway.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/data-src-image-205610c6-4c60-4100-bfd9-13cc9912cf84.png)

It created Syclant, a Bug/Ice Pokémon designed through a sequence of early community polls. Then it created Revenankh. Then Pyroak. Then Fidgit, Stratagem, Arghonaut, Kitsunoh, Cyclohm, Colossoil, Krilowatt, Voodoom, and eventually dozens more.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/80225.png)

The current project is **CAP 38**.

## **What a CAP Project Does.**

A modern CAP project begins by selecting its leadership.

Then the community chooses a **Concept**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/smogecko.png)

A Concept is the competitive question the project is going to investigate. It does not usually specify the final creature in advance. It identifies a strategic relation worth exploring and gives the later decisions something to remain answerable to.

The project then moves through a long sequence of linked stages.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/smoguana.png)

The exact process has changed across CAP history, but the current guide includes leadership selection, Concept submissions and polling, Concept Assessment, typing, Threats Discussion, abilities, statistical limits and spreads, art, counters, naming, attacking and non-attacking moves, complete movepools, sprites, implementation, playtesting, and analysis.

Every stage inherits the previous stages.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/smokomodo.png)

-   A typing changes which abilities make sense.
    -   An ability changes which statistics are useful.
        -   The statistics change which moves would be excessive, insufficient, or pointless.
            -   The moves can preserve the intended tradeoffs or quietly erase all of them.

Art and flavor proceed alongside the competitive process and eventually have to belong to the same creature.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/miasmite.png)

There is no blank reset between stages.

By the time participants reach the movepool, they are no longer deciding what moves would be interesting on some abstract Pokémon. They are deciding what moves belong on the specific object produced by every earlier argument.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/nohface.png)

CAP therefore has two simultaneous jobs.

-   It has to preserve public participation across many separate discussions.
-   It also has to make one Pokémon.

This is substantially harder than it sounds.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/syclar.png)

## **The Topic Leader and the Topic Leadership Team.**

The central coordinating office is the **Topic Leader**, shortened to **TL**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/ramnarok-radiant.png)

Smogon’s current process guide calls the Topic Leader the head of an individual creation project. The office maintains pace, leads discussions, determines poll formats, interprets results, decides when secondary polls are required, selects slates for some stages, and coordinates with moderators when absence threatens the schedule. A new Topic Leader is chosen for each new creation.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/ramnarok.png)

The official nominations guide describes the Topic Leader in stronger terms. The TL is expected to:

> **“decipher the community’s discussion and translate it into slates.”**

That word **translate** is doing a great deal of work.

A forum discussion does not naturally become a decision.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/monohm.png)

Participants may agree for different reasons.

They may use the same term to describe incompatible ideas.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/duohm.png)

A popular proposal may not actually answer the Concept.

Several good proposals may differ only cosmetically and divide the same coalition.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/cyclohm.png)

A late post may reveal a distinction that reorganizes everything written before it.

Somebody has to read the field, determine what the live options actually are, and turn the discussion into a set of choices the community can select among.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/equilibra.png)

The Topic Leader does not currently carry all of that work alone.

CAP 38 opened five leadership positions:

-   **Topic Leader**
-   **Typing Leader**
-   **Ability Leader**
-   **Stats Leader**
-   **Movepool Leader**

The four specialists form the **Topic Leadership Team**, shortened to **TLT**. Each specialist leads one major competitive domain, develops the discussion there, and prepares the slate for the relevant poll. The Topic Leader leads the project as a whole, runs several integrative stages, and finalizes the specialists’ slates.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/caribolt.png)

The current application thread states the governing aspiration directly:

> **“quality control,” not “project control.”**

That distinction was earned through a long and unpleasant history.

We will get there.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/voodoll.png)

For now, the relevant fact is that CAP does not simply open a giant poll and accept whatever receives the most clicks.

Its current Process Rules require public submission and discussion threads before polling. Participants can submit options, critique other submissions, state preferences, and develop the reasoning behind them. Posts are expected to be substantiated rather than functioning as unsupported votes inside the discussion itself.

Then the **Section Leader** decides which submissions advance.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/fawnifer.png)

The rules say that this judgment is made using the formal rules, the leader’s metagame knowledge, the developing CAP, and the leader’s expectations for what the creation requires.

Only after that does the public poll begin.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/cupra.png)

This means the leadership office carries authority at two different moments.

-   It guides the discussion.
-   Then it determines which products of that discussion become eligible for collective choice.
    -   The final vote is public.
    -   The route to the vote is interpreted.

We are going to spend a **lot** of time on that distinction.

## **Slates, Polls, and the Ballot Before the Ballot.**

A **slate** is the set of submissions allowed to advance from discussion into polling.

Suppose twelve people submit statistical spreads for a new Pokémon.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/breezi.png)

The Stats Leader does not automatically place all twelve into the final poll.

-   Some may violate established limits.
-   Some may fail to support the Concept.
-   Some may duplicate one another.
-   Some may be competitively unsound in ways that are difficult to see without specialist knowledge.
-   Some may have attracted little support or failed to survive criticism.

The Stats Leader interprets that record and decides what advances.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/cawdet.png)

CAP has changed voting systems too.

The published Process Guide still explains instant-runoff voting and bold voting as the project's standard polling methods, and its Topic Leader Selection page still describes the leadership election as an instant-runoff vote.

The live institution has moved.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/cawmodore.png)

Recent major CAP polls, including Topic Leader elections, use **Ranked Pairs**.

A voter ranks as many candidates as they want. The count then turns those rankings into head-to-head contests: Candidate A against Candidate B, Candidate A against Candidate C, and so on. The strongest victories are considered in order and locked into the ranking unless doing so would create a cycle.

The result is less:

-   **Who survived elimination?**

and more:

-   **Who beats whom when the candidates are compared directly?**

This is another small reminder that an institution's operating constitution can move faster than its published guide.

Candidates still nominate themselves and explain their experience. Forum moderators determine which applicants are qualified. The accepted candidates then enter a public ranked ballot open to the CAP community.

This is not a completely open election.

It is also not a private appointment.

Expert screening occurs before public preference.

We will spend time on that too.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/solotl.png)

This is about constitutional succession.

## **Policy Review and Post Play Lookback.**

CAP also has mechanisms for returning to decisions after they have acted.

A **Policy Review** turns the community’s attention toward its own process. Participants argue about leadership powers, voting rules, stage order, pacing, qualification, moderation, and other institutional questions. Many of the changes examined in this article were proposed, contested, and adopted through these public reviews.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/privatyke.png)

A **Post Play Lookback** turns back toward the completed Pokémon after competitive play has supplied evidence about what the design actually did.

These are separate correction routes.

-   One reviews the institution.
-   The other reviews the thing the institution made.

The distinction will matter later, after we have watched CAP discover why it needed both.

For now, we finally have enough vocabulary to begin the history.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/protowatt.png)

Create-A-Pokémon does not vote a monster directly into existence.

It selects people to translate a long public inquiry into a sequence of bounded choices. Those people receive enough authority to influence what the community can decide, enough responsibility to carry the relation among stages, and enough procedural support to keep the project moving for months.

Then the Pokémon is finished.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/mollux.png)

-   The office ends.
    -   Another project begins.

The first major constitutional question in CAP history appeared almost immediately:

> **Who gets to lead the next one?**

## **The Constitution Arrives Before the Pokémon.**

**Create-A-Pokémon** began in November 2007 with a poll, a tentative schedule, and no settled institution capable of carrying either one.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/saharascal-1.png)

Cooper opened the first project as its organizer because he had proposed the project. Participants quickly began calling the role a **mini-mod**, which was accurate enough to describe the work and unfortunate enough that the community soon began searching for another term.

By March 2008, CAP had completed Syclant and was finishing Revenankh. The first project had taken roughly six weeks. The second was entering its eleventh.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/revenankh.png)

The community responded to this by opening a sixteen-page workshop to write down how the project was supposed to work.

The thread begins with Deck Knight assembling a writing team for the new Process Guide. Cooper and Hyra were assigned the section on the mini-mod’s definition and duties because Cooper had organized Syclant and Hyra was then running Revenankh. Deck Knight proposed two possible replacements for the title.

-   One was **Topic Leader**.
-   The other was **CAPo**,
    -   because a capo is the head of an organized crime syndicate.

Topic Leader won history.

This is probably for the best.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/mumbao.png)

The group then began trying to answer four questions:

1.  What is a Topic Leader?
2.  What are the Topic Leader’s responsibilities?
3.  What powers does the Topic Leader possess?
4.  How does someone become the next Topic Leader?

The last question became urgent before the guide existed.

## **Human Beings Enter the Process.**

Luck raised the first problem in extremely ordinary terms.

The project had selected good people. Good people could still become unavailable.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/chuggon.png)

> **“People go away on vacations, get sick, find a new job or school work crashes down hard.”**

Luck proposed finding several active participants and gradually preparing them as replacements or backups before the absence occurred.

This was not yet a theory of distributed authority.

It was a forum user noticing that the project could stop because somebody had homework.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/scratchet-1.png)

That is enough to begin institutional design.

Online communities often look less material than conventional organizations because their buildings are tabs and their work is performed through posts. The dependencies are still physical. The Topic Leader has a body, a schedule, a job, a family, a school term, a time zone, and a finite quantity of attention.

If nobody else knows how the process is being carried, the project has not escaped those facts.

It has concentrated them in one person.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/draggalong.png)

Luck’s proposed alternates were therefore not simply emergency replacements. The proposal included slowly grooming people into the work while the existing leaders were still present.

CAP had created two Pokémon.

It was already discussing a succession bench.

## **Who Chooses the Next One?**

Five days later, Hyra returned to the thread and made the succession problem explicit.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/floatoy.png)

> **“How are we going to choose the next one?”**

Cooper and Hyra were writing the leadership guide. Both had already led projects. If the new guide simply allowed them to choose another leader, CAP would acquire a process whose first effect was to formalize the authority of the people writing it.

Hyra continued:

> **“I assume you are leaving it up to us. But that’s not exactly the best way to go about it.”**

DougJustDoug proposed the first serious selection architecture the next day.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/swirlpool.png)

The incumbent would no longer privately name the successor. Candidates would nominate themselves and make a public pitch. Community members could post support. A committee of past Topic Leaders and other experienced participants would evaluate the field. Committee members could not themselves become candidates. A non-voting chair would organize the process and break a tie if necessary.

The crucial sentence came near the beginning:

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/dorsoil.png)

> **“I think the project has moved beyond that now.”**

The **that** was _incumbent succession_.

Cooper had chosen Hyra for Revenankh. Doug did not claim this had been a bad choice. The relationship had worked well enough for both people to be writing the first Process Guide.

The objection was structural rather than retrospective.

A procedure can be safe while the current people are trustworthy and still be a bad procedure.

-   The founder knows someone capable.
-   The founder appoints them.
    -   The project succeeds.
        -   Success becomes evidence that the founder should appoint the next person too.

At no point does the institution have to discover whether its authority can move without passing through the incumbent’s private judgment.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/justyke.png)

CAP had reached only its third succession and was already refusing that path.

Doug’s replacement was not a pure popular election.

He specifically rejected a free-for-all cattle call and argued that selecting this kind of leader required some subjective judgment from experienced participants. The public could provide information and support. The panel would make the final choice.

The proposed procedure tried to preserve two things at once.

-   The wider community should have a voice.
-   The office should go to someone capable of carrying it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/saharaja.png)

Neither requirement was allowed to disappear into the other.

## **The Transition Takes Time.**

Hyra liked the proposal, and immediately identified its next failure mode.

-   The selection process could be legitimate.
-   It could also be too slow.

Revenankh had barely received a name before participants began demanding the next creation thread. Hyra estimated that even the expedited procedure would produce a three-to-seven-day gap between projects.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/coribalis.png)

> **“They will not appreciate any gap in the transition.”**

This sentence is funny because the field is a Pokémon creation forum in 2008.

It is also exactly the problem.

An online project depends on active attention. Participants leave a finished stage, wait for the next one, and begin spending their time somewhere else. A week is not long in a state constitution. It can be very long in a volunteer internet project whose current institution consists of people refreshing the same thread.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/ababo.png)

Hyra then rejected the easy speed solution too:

> **“Named succession doesn’t work either. Eventually someone will screw it up and then this project tanks.”**

The same post called for an impeachment process in case CAP selected a Topic Leader capable of killing the project.

The community had now assembled the whole succession problem in fewer than twenty forum posts.

-   A successor had to be chosen through a legitimate process.
-   The person selected had to be capable.
-   The procedure had to move quickly enough to preserve participation.
-   And the institution needed a correction path if the selected leader turned out to be dangerous.

No single rule solved all four.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/fidgit.png)

-   Incumbent appointment was fast and capable when the incumbent judged well.
    -   It concentrated legitimacy and failed badly when the judgment did not.
-   A public election could widen legitimacy
    -   while selecting for popularity over operating knowledge.
-   An expert committee could preserve competence
    -   while becoming a private guild.
-   A long deliberation could improve the choice
    -   while allowing the field to dissolve during the improvement.
-   An instant appointment could preserve momentum
    -   and discover the error after the new leader had already begun acting.

This was not a disagreement over whether succession mattered.

It was a disagreement over **which part of succession would be allowed to fail**.

* * *

## **One Week Without Polls.**

Time Mage offered the counterargument.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/tomohawk.png)

CAP could survive one week without active polling. Selecting the next Topic Leader carefully mattered, but the process surrounding the office mattered even more. Poll order, poll style, duration, and the conditions for additional votes should be decided in advance rather than becoming discretionary powers invented by the next leader.

The Topic Leader, in this account, should direct and organize a process whose major rules already existed.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/venomicon.png)

That is a different theory of institutional speed.

-   Hyra feared the empty week because participants might leave.
-   Time Mage feared the rushed constitution because the new leader might inherit undefined power.

Doug agreed that one week without polls was survivable. He did not agree that the Process Guide could sit still.

The workshop itself had been open for seven days with little visible progress. Revenankh was nearly complete, and the leadership-selection discussion had only just begun. Doug threatened to close the workshop and pursue another route if the writers did not begin producing concrete rules.

This is where the archive becomes perfect.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/venomicon-epilogue.png)

The people writing rules about project delay are delaying the project by writing rules about project delay.

The institution has entered the document.

## **Syclant Took Six Weeks.**

## **Revenankh Took Eleven.**

The pacing problem was not imagined.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/pluffle.png)

Later in the same thread, Doug recorded that Syclant had taken six weeks to complete and Revenankh was beginning its eleventh week of discussion and polling. The second project had taken so long that participants were already arguing about lost interest, concurrent projects, shorter polls, and whether the whole process should be reverse-engineered to fit within a four-to-six-week target.

CAP was learning something that larger institutions repeatedly discover under more dignified names.

> A process can become so careful that it loses the people whose participation makes the process legitimate.

It can also move so quickly that authority outruns the rules meant to govern it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/obliteryx.png)

The 2008 workshop did not settle this problem forever.

It did establish the terms under which CAP would keep encountering it.

-   The project needed leadership because someone had to organize the stages and close decisions.
-   The leader needed rules because organization could become personal discretion.
    -   The next leader needed a selection process because the incumbent could not own succession.
    -   The selection process needed expertise because the office required real operating knowledge.
        -   The expertise needed community input because a closed panel could reproduce itself.
        -   And this whole thing needed to happen before everyone wandered off to discuss something else.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/snugglow.png)

This was CAP’s constitutional field before its third Pokémon had been completed.

The community had not yet decided how strong the Topic Leader should be.

It already knew that a formally valid successor arriving after the project had lost momentum would not be enough.

-   Who chose the leader mattered.
-   Whether the leader could perform the work mattered.
    -   Whether the field was still there when the leader arrived mattered too.

The next problem would come from the work itself.

-   CAP could select a Topic Leader.
    -   It still had to determine how much _leading_ the Topic Leader was supposed to do.

* * *

## **The Weak Topic Leader.**

The first Topic Leaders were not expected to _own_ the direction of a Create-A-Pokémon project. They organized it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/chuggalong.png)

The most detailed surviving reconstruction of this early period appears in a 2012 Policy Review written by DougJustDoug during a later leadership crisis. That source needs to be handled with care. Doug was one of CAP’s central architects and moderators, and his historical account was being used to support a major reform proposal. He was not writing from outside the institution or without a position on what the history meant.

The public process archive supports the basic chronology.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/rebble.png)

Doug’s interpretation gives us the internal constitutional language CAP used to describe what it thought had gone wrong.

In his account, the earliest leaders functioned largely as an:

> **“administrative assistant”**

They opened discussions, collected feedback, organized polls, counted votes, and constructed slates from the options that appeared to have the strongest community support.

The office contained real authority.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/chromera.png)

The Topic Leader decided when a discussion was finished. The Topic Leader determined which options had enough support to move forward. The Topic Leader administered the sequence.

But the leader’s own substantive position was not supposed to carry more official weight simply because the person held the office.

-   A Topic Leader could argue like any other influential participant.
-   Then the Topic Leader had to put the office back on and interpret what the community had done.

CAP later called this the **Weak Topic Leader Model**.

It was not weak in the sense that nobody listened.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/astrolotl.png)

Several early Topic Leaders were moderators or major Smogon personalities whose ordinary posts already carried enormous influence. The distinction was institutional. Their personal argument and their official action were supposed to remain separable.

This worked better on some projects than others.

* * *

## **Syclant and Revenankh Had No Constitution to Betray.**

The first creation, Syclant, did not begin with a formal competitive Concept.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1161.png)

Cooper organized the threads. Different participants arrived with different ambitions. One group wanted something resembling Infernape: a fast mixed attacker with broad coverage and setup potential. Another wanted a response to Garchomp, then one of the dominant and most hated Pokémon in the Diamond and Pearl metagame.

The project gradually produced a Bug/Ice attacker with extreme Speed, strong mixed offense, weak defenses, an Earthquake resistance, and the ability to destroy Garchomp through powerful Ice attacks.

This was not the execution of one explicit design document.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1162.png)

A direction emerged from the public field as the polls moved.

Revenankh, the second creation, was similarly loose in formal structure and much more focused in outcome. Cooper selected Hyra to organize the next project. Participants converged around a Ghost/Fighting Pokémon that could use Bulk Up, and the project deliberately gave the resulting creature different recovery arrangements to test which would work better in play.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1163.png)

The result was a powerful, coherent Pokémon built before CAP possessed a formal Concept stage or even a settled Topic Leader selection procedure.

This history matters because it prevented CAP from drawing one easy conclusion later.

-   Weak leadership did not always produce weak projects.
-   A community could sometimes find direction without one person carrying it.

Then came Pyroak.

* * *

### **Pyroak and the First Compass.**

CAP 3 was the first project run under a more formalized leadership structure.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1164.png)

Gothic Togekiss was selected as Topic Leader by the early selection committee. The community chose Grass/Fire typing.

Then the project split.

![](https://www.smogon.com/media/smog/12/capart_cockatrice.jpg)

-   Some participants wanted a large offensive sweeper.
-   Others wanted a defensive SubSeed Pokémon capable of using Substitute, Leech Seed, and bulk to wear opponents down.

The dispute intensified when automatic sunlight entered the discussion. Permanent weather was one of the major boundaries separating ordinary Diamond and Pearl competitive play from the Ubers environment, and giving a community-created Pokémon access to it raised questions far beyond the selected typing.

![](https://www.smogon.com/media/smog/12/capart_snake.jpg)

Participants began arguing about later stages before earlier ones had settled. Each poll inherited several incompatible readings of what the project was becoming. The Topic Leader could organize those decisions without possessing a mandate to choose among the competing visions.

Pyroak was completed.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/pyroak.png)

The process had become, in Doug’s later description, a disaster.

CAP responded by creating the formal **Concept** stage.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/embirch.png)

Every future project would begin by selecting a public competitive question intended to serve as a compass across the later decisions.

The reform made sense.

Typing, abilities, statistics, and movepool could now be evaluated against the same declared purpose.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/flarelm.png)

A participant could no longer justify an option only by saying it would be cool, strong, or useful. The option also had to answer the project CAP had agreed to conduct.

Then the community discovered that a compass does not carry itself.

* * *

## **Different People Arrive at Different Stages.**

CAP is a sequential public process, but it is not one stable room of people following every decision with equal attention.

Different stages attract different specialists.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/flox.png)

-   Statistical discussions draw participants who know damage calculations, speed thresholds, defensive benchmarks, Base Stat Rating, and the mathematical relation among the six base statistics.
-   Movepool discussions draw participants who understand coverage, setup, utility, move combinations, legality, and how one additional option can transform several earlier tradeoffs.
-   Typing and ability discussions attract their own experts and constituencies.
-   Art and flavor stages can bring in another population almost entirely.

This diversity gives CAP much of its intelligence.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/shox.png)

It also means that the people interpreting the Concept during one stage may not be the people interpreting it during the next.

Doug’s 2012 history gives the cleanest example.

-   The stat discussion might decide that the Concept requires a bulky defensive Pokémon and construct the numbers accordingly.
-   Later, a different movepool population might decide that the same Concept requires a fast attacker and give the completed body the tools of one.

Every local choice could be defensible.

The whole creature could become a:

> **“Frankenstein’s Monster”**

> One Pokémon.

> Several projects.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/scattervein-2.png)

This is the key point in the defense of strong Topic Leadership.

Voting does not integrate what voting produces.

A public Concept does not automatically preserve one interpretation of that Concept across months of changing participants and accumulated decisions.

A procedure can be open, participatory, well documented, and structurally incapable of carrying the relation among its own stages.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/pajantom-1.png)

CAP did not need someone to replace community judgment.

It needed someone to remember what the judgments were supposed to belong to.

* * *

## **Cyclohm and the Influence Battle.**

The problem came to a head during CAP 8, the project that produced Cyclohm.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1165.png)

The selected Concept was **Neglected Ability**. The project was supposed to explore a competitively overlooked Pokémon ability by building a creature capable of using it well.

Another submitted Concept, Deck Knight’s **Paralysis Abusing Tank**, had lost the Concept vote.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1166.png)

According to Doug’s later reconstruction, the losing direction did not leave the field.

Deck Knight remained one of the project’s most active and persuasive participants. He argued across stages for a bulky Dragon-type capable of exploiting paralysis. He eventually won the statistical and movepool stages himself and influenced discussions throughout the creation.

None of this required formal authority.

Deck Knight was not the Topic Leader or even a project moderator.

He was very good at posting.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1171.png)

The official Topic Leader was Cyberzero. Under the Weak Topic Leader Model, Cyberzero’s job was to interpret the public discussion and administer the resulting polls. It was not to suppress an influential participant’s direction because that direction had lost an earlier vote.

So CAP 8 developed into what Doug describes as an _influence battle_. Different coalitions organized around different readings of the broad Concept. Participants campaigned across stages. The Topic Leader kept the process moving while the project twisted among them.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1168.png)

Cyclohm emerged with some competitive coherence, partly because Deck Knight’s preferred direction carried through enough of the stages to hold several pieces together.

The route there still looked like a mob brawl.

This is a difficult institutional failure to diagnose.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1169.png)

Deck Knight had persuaded people through public argument.

The polls were still real.

Cyberzero had not falsified the record or imposed a private design.

The community had chosen every component.

The project had also created a system in which the person most capable of dominating several separate discussions could function as an unofficial integrator without becoming answerable for integration as an office.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1170.png)

CAP’s later review did not place the failure primarily on Cyberzero or Deck Knight.

It placed the failure on the process.

-   The institution had required somebody to carry the whole project.
-   It had declined to make that anybody’s official job.

* * *

## **The Strong Topic Leader Model.**

After Cyclohm, CAP rewrote the office.

The Topic Leader would no longer sit back, count mentions, and assemble whatever options appeared most popular.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1173.png)

-   The leader would assess the quality of arguments in relation to the selected Concept.
-   The leader would open discussions with a defined frame.
-   The leader would encourage participants to develop promising directions and challenge weak ones.
-   When the discussion wandered, the leader was expected to:

> **“corral the herd.”**

This became the **Strong Topic Leader Model**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1172.png)

The reform concentrated authority in several ways at once.

-   The Topic Leader’s interpretation of the Concept now carried explicit institutional weight.
-   The opening post could define the question a stage was expected to answer.
-   The leader could judge arguments rather than simply count support.
-   The slate could exclude a popular option if the leader concluded that it failed the project.
-   The office became responsible for focus, coherence, quality, momentum, and protection against what the community itself had come to regard as bad decisions.

From outside, this can sound like CAP abandoned democracy after one disorderly Pokémon.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1174.png)

That is not what the record shows.

The public discussions and public polls remained.

Participants continued supplying the proposals and arguments.

The Topic Leader did not receive authority to privately specify the finished creature.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1175.png)

The office received authority over interpretation and sequence because the project had learned that those functions were already being performed informally, unevenly, and without clear accountability.

This is concentrated formative authority in its strongest defensible form.

-   The work does not yet exist.
-   The evidence is incomplete.
-   Different specialists possess different pieces.
    -   One person is asked to preserve the relation among them long enough for the object to become real.

CAP did not make the Topic Leader strong because it thought leaders were naturally wiser than communities. It made the Topic Leader strong because the community had repeatedly produced pieces that did not know how to become a whole. _The project strengthened Topic Leadership after learning that sequential public choices do not automatically integrate themselves._

* * *

## **The First Strong Topic Leader.**

CAP 9 produced Colossoil under Plus, the first Topic Leader explicitly selected to operate under the new model.

Doug’s retrospective describes Plus as strong enough that CAP did not have to explain the mandate twice. Plus was already a forceful personality. The new office invited the leader to direct discussion, reject bad options, and keep the project on course.

He did.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/colossoil.png)

The community had repaired one failure by creating a new constitutional possibility.

-   A Topic Leader could now prevent the project from being pulled apart by several competing unofficial leaders.
-   The same Topic Leader could also shape every stage from the opening frame through the final slate.

That was not yet proof that the reform was wrong. This was the power CAP had deliberately built. The office now carried the Concept across the process. It interpreted which arguments served that Concept. It decided which options were serious enough to become public choices. It was expected to protect the project from drift, incoherence, and failure.

CAP had given one temporary leader responsibility for the relation among all the parts. The next constitutional crisis would begin when the community tried to determine where that relation ended and the leader began.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/aurumoth.png)

The Pokémon was called Aurumoth.

* * *

## **Aurumoth.**

CAP 4 began in September 2012 with a concept called **Living on the Edge**.

The project would create a Pokémon built around **risk**.

This was already a dangerous noun to hand an internet forum.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1229.png)

**Risk** can mean inaccurate attacks. It can mean exposing a weakness in exchange for power. It can mean committing several turns to a setup that may fail. It can mean choosing a Pokémon whose matchup spread is excellent when predicted correctly and terrible when predicted incorrectly. It can mean asking the player to invest heavily in support before receiving anything useful back.

The Concept Assessment stage was supposed to narrow those possibilities into a competitive question the later stages could answer.

It did not narrow them very far.

Aurumoth emerged anyway.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1176.png)

It was Bug/Psychic, the first Psychic-type CAP and a deliberate return to the Bug typing of Syclant. It received three abilities with very different relations to risk: Weak Armor, No Guard, and Illusion. It received 600 total base-stat points, extremely broad offensive coverage, Dragon Dance, Quiver Dance, and enough customizability that the eventual playtest could plausibly reveal several different Pokémon hiding inside one moth.

The final product was not received as an obvious constitutional disaster.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1179.png)

Some participants were excited. Some disliked almost every major decision while directing the criticism toward the community that had voted for them rather than toward the Topic Leader alone. Others considered bugmaniacbob the strongest Topic Leader they had watched. One poster praised the project precisely because a directionless CAP could fall apart before producing anything at all.

bugmaniacbob himself wrote an enormous final account of the project. He described entering the office unexpectedly, discovering that he possessed the power of the project for two months, trying to push CAP into difficult territory, and watching the final creature incorporate choices he would not have made alone. The result, he wrote, was a **“masterpiece.”**

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1177.png)

So the later reform cannot be reported honestly as a community unanimously escaping a leader everyone already recognized as intolerable.

CAP 4 ended with a completed Pokémon, a public vote behind every selected component, genuine admiration for the work, and a Topic Leader who had spent a staggering amount of time keeping the project moving.

Then the Policy Review began.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1178.png)

* * *

## **The Prosecution Brief.**

On December 8, 2012, DougJustDoug opened a thread titled **Topic Leadership** with a blunt assessment: CAP 4 had been terrible from a leadership perspective.

The post is one of the central documents in this history.

It is also not a judgment from outside the event.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1180.png)

Doug was a CAP founder, moderator, participant, and one of the people whose own submission had been excluded from a disputed name slate. He openly discusses that conflict. He says he compared his recollection with other moderators, while declining to name them and inviting them to contradict him. He writes with enough anger that the reader never has to infer how he felt about bugmaniacbob by the end of the project.

This document is evidence from the institutional fight.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1181.png)

It is not the neutral voice of History arriving twelve minutes later with the replay booth.

Doug’s account alleges a progressive change in the use of Topic Leader authority.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1182.png)

At first, the project looked excellent. The Concept discussion received unusually close attention. The first signs of trouble appeared in slates that Doug believed were built to favor particular outcomes. The typing slate heavily favored Bug combinations. Later ability discussions opened with stronger signals about which choices the Topic Leader considered acceptable. Stat submissions were constrained within narrow limits, and the Topic Leader posted a working spread early enough that other contributors could understand the shape of the expected answer.

Then came the flavor stages.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1184.png)

Names and Pokédex entries have no direct competitive effect. They still belonged to the community process. Doug argues that these stages made the ownership problem easier to see because technical necessity could no longer explain the intervention. The dispute eventually became serious enough that moderators stepped into an active project and directed the Topic Leader to change how one slate would be handled.

The exact personal story remains contested.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1185.png)

The institutional facts are easier to establish.

The Topic Leader possessed broad discretion over discussion framing, submission standards, poll construction, and the transition from one stage to the next.

The rules had been written around trusted custom.

Previous leaders were expected to place the community project ahead of their own preferred Pokémon. Those expectations had often remained unwritten because earlier leaders had observed them closely enough that CAP did not know it needed a rule.

CAP 4 found the edge of that arrangement.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1186.png)

Doug’s strongest policy conclusion is therefore broader than his case against one person. He explicitly says he does not think bugmaniacbob acted with malicious intent. He describes him as intelligent and extraordinarily hardworking. The failure, in Doug’s account, arose when one leadership style met an office whose soft limits had never been converted into enforceable boundaries.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1183.png)

A constitution depending on everyone understanding where power should stop has already assigned somebody the job of discovering what happens when they do not.

CAP 4 discovered it with a gold moth.

* * *

## **bugmaniacbob Answers.**

bugmaniacbob did not write a matching legal defense of every disputed slate.

His later reflections give us something more useful.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1187.png)

A year after Aurumoth, applying to rejoin CAP’s Policy Review Committee, he described the project as widely considered CAP’s **“greatest blunder since EVO”** and acknowledged that it had helped produce a major reduction in Topic Leader power. He did not call the project a contribution he was especially proud of. He also said he had worked extremely hard to make it function and left readers to reach their own conclusions about the result.

Years later, in a long autobiographical retrospective assembled from old posts, he describes how the office changed his conduct. Being Topic Leader and being looked up to, he suggests, began affecting the initiative he took and the conflicts he entered. The same archive shows that he had a detailed plan for Aurumoth, especially for using later movepool choices to reconcile the many options selected earlier. He wanted versatility and admits that he worked actively to secure it.

This does not dissolve the criticism. It improves the case.

The office did not need a cartoon villain.

It needed a person with a strong vision, an unusual willingness to work, enough public trust to win the role without most established leadership supporting him, and a process that had spent several years teaching Topic Leaders that they were responsible for preventing the project from going wrong.

CAP had built a leader expected to integrate everything.

Then it was surprised when **the leader began integrating everything**.

The conflict concerned where _integration_ ended and **ownership** began.

That distinction was real.

The rules could not yet carry it.

## **This Moth Had Other Problems.**

Aurumoth’s later competitive post-mortem complicates the leadership narrative again.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1188.png)

Participants did not agree that one Topic Leader was the complete explanation for the creature they produced. Several located the earliest failure in Concept Assessment. “Risk” had never been narrowed into one operational direction, so typing, abilities, statistics, and movepool inherited different definitions. Others blamed the movepool for removing the vulnerabilities earlier choices were meant to preserve. Others blamed particular abilities, the statistical spread, or the accumulation of several individually defensible decisions that worked badly together.

The community could therefore tell at least two histories of CAP 4.

-   In one, a powerful Topic Leader pushed the project toward a preferred Pokémon.
-   In another, the community never reached a sufficiently clear account of risk,
    -   voted through several incompatible directions,
        -   and later found it convenient to locate the whole failure in the most visible office.

Applied Case: The Werster Crisis

Pokémon speedrunning built extraordinary instruments for verifying runs. It built much weaker instruments for verifying the authority surrounding them.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/icon/thin-tile.rulebook-2-c17bf296-5ed5-46a8-8b10-07f9de247ff0.png)Modal Path EthicsAidan Lawson

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/thumbnail/image-42-1-2a382664-653a-49a5-978d-8ff3bb7e7790.png)

](https://modalpathethics.com/applied-case-the-werster-crisis/)

Both histories can contain truth.

-   The leader framed the field.
    -   The voters still voted.
-   The concept remained vague.
    -   The final choices still had arguments behind them.

Aurumoth could be competitively interesting and procedurally unhealthy at the same time. The Policy Review did not need to decide whether the moth itself was good. It needed to decide whether the same office should retain enough power to produce the same dispute again.

One participant’s testimony made that question much harder to avoid.

## **“Would This Even Get Slated?”**

DarkSlay had contributed to CAP before Aurumoth.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1189.png)

In the Topic Leadership thread, he described seeing the project change and deciding not to enter some arguments because of the Topic Leader’s actual or perceived power. The stat limits, in his account, closed off creative and balanced submissions. Producing a serious stat spread required time. Why spend that time when the acceptable range already appeared settled?

The question governing participation changed.

> **“Would this even get slated? Would the TL like it?”**

That is more important than any single poll result.

A slate is the set of choices that reaches the public vote.

-   If an idea never reaches the slate, the community never gets to reject it.
-   If participants can predict the selector strongly enough, some ideas never reach the submission thread either.
    -   The Topic Leader does not have to say no.
    -   The participant carries the anticipated no into their own work.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1190.png)

This is why the repeated defense that every final component received a public vote could not settle CAP’s dispute.

The vote began after the office had framed the discussion, set some submission boundaries, interpreted which arguments represented serious community support, and decided which proposals could become ballot options.

The poll remained real. So did the power exercised before it.

This is common in mature institutions and difficult to see because the visible decision happens late.

-   Editors influence manuscripts before submission.
-   Grant panels influence research questions before an application exists.
-   Managers influence which concerns employees believe can survive escalation.
-   Courts influence the language in which injuries become legally recognizable.
-   Recommendation systems influence what producers imagine will travel.

Nobody has to prohibit every excluded possibility.

The field learns the gate. Then it arrives shaped for passage.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1192.png)

CAP 4 made that structure visible because the gatekeeper was one identifiable Topic Leader and the object at stake was a Bug/Psychic Pokémon with three abilities.

Most institutions are less considerate.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1191.png)

* * *

## **The Vote Was Downstream.**

-   Aurumoth was a community creation.

That sentence is true.

-   bugmaniacbob had enormous influence over Aurumoth.

That sentence is also true. The existence of the first does not cancel the second.

CAP’s problem was no longer whether the community should vote or whether the Topic Leader should lead. The project had already learned why it needed both.

The problem was how the office that translated discussion into votable choices could remain strong enough to integrate the project without teaching participants that the leader’s preference was the real constitution.

-   A direct democracy would revive the Frankenstein problem.
-   Another Strong Topic Leader could reproduce Aurumoth.
-   A committee might divide the responsibility until nobody could tell one coherent Pokémon from several locally excellent fragments.

The community now had to redesign the office while the reason for having the office remained intact.

This is where the king appears.

* * *

## **King for a Project.**

The first proposal was to abolish the Topic Leader.

DougJustDoug had spent the opening of the 2012 Policy Review arguing that CAP 4 exposed a structural problem in the office itself. One person had accumulated responsibility for the Concept, the discussions, the slates, the pace, the final product, the public image of the project, and the prevention of every possible bad outcome.

-   The role had become a constitutional Swiss Army knife.
-   It had also become exhausting.

Doug reviewed the careers of previous Topic Leaders and noticed an ugly pattern: some of CAP’s strongest contributors reached the office, completed a project, and then reduced their participation or disappeared. CAP treated Topic Leadership as its highest distinction and attached enough labor to the distinction that receiving it could end the contributor’s active life in the institution.

So split the job.

-   Typing could be led by someone who actually cared about typing.
-   Statistics could be led by the kind of person who wanted to spend an evening defending the relation between Physical Sweepiness and Special Tankiness.
-   Movepool could be led by somebody who knew which sixty technically legal moves would quietly destroy the entire design.

The project would gain specialist judgment, distribute the labor, and create more opportunities for people to acquire leadership experience.

This was sensible. It also risked recreating the exact problem the Strong Topic Leader had been built to solve.

* * *

## **Several Leaders Can Build Several Pokémon.**

The objections appeared immediately.

-   A specialist team could prevent one person from controlling every stage.
-   It could also give every stage its own controlling person.

The Typing Leader might preserve one interpretation of the Concept. The Ability Leader could prefer another. The Stats Leader might build a defensive Pokémon. The Movepool Leader could arrive weeks later and give it the tools of an attacker. Each specialist would be locally qualified. Each could produce a serious slate. The final body might still become a constitutional corpse assembled from excellent departments.

This was the return of Frankenstein.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1230.png)

Mdevil argued that multiple leaders would bring multiple biases and could leave the creature broad, unfocused, and publicly divided. New participants, in particular, benefited from knowing who was leading. Discord among several visible authorities could create uncertainty about which direction still counted.

Other participants worried about factions. A Topic Leadership Team might cooperate beautifully. It might also become a coalition market.

One specialist gets the ability they want. Another gets the movepool. A third stops objecting to the typing. Everybody can honestly say the community voted, and the Pokémon can emerge as a treaty among offices whose concessions make no competitive sense together.

The moderator layer did not automatically solve this either.

CAP 4 had already shown how difficult intervention could become during a live project. Discussions moved quickly. Problems developed across several days. Moderators had to compare accounts, decide whether an unusual choice represented legitimate Topic Leader discretion, and act without turning the creation thread into an open power struggle. By the time the deeper pattern became clear, much of the Pokémon already existed.

> The reform therefore had to preserve a visible integrator.

The community had spent years learning that one.

The question became whether CAP could keep the Topic Leader while removing the office’s monopoly over the route into public choice.

* * *

## **The IRC Convention.**

On the night of December 8 and the early morning of December 9, several CAP participants moved the argument into the project’s IRC channel. The conversation was later posted back to the Policy Review forum.

This is an important detail about how the institution actually worked.

-   The public forum contains the durable record.
-   The proposal itself was developed through a faster private-adjacent channel among active participants, then brought into the forum for scrutiny.

CAP was never only the forum.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1232.png)

During the discussion, Deck Knight proposed sectional leaders under a directing leader. The specialists would carry different parts of the project. One person would remain responsible for the Concept across all of them.

Birkal recognized the shape immediately.

-   There would still be a king.
-   The advisors would have jobs.

The group began working through what that could mean in practice.

-   Who makes a slate?
-   Who can reject it?
-   Who chooses the central leader?
-   Can one person apply for several roles?
-   What happens when the Movepool Leader believes _Shell Smash_ is a brilliant expression of the Concept and the Topic Leader believes _Shell Smash_ is an act of war?

This is where the proposed constitution became specific enough to matter.

1.  The specialist leading a stage would make that stage’s slate.
2.  The Topic Leader would keep authority over the Concept and could require a new slate when the specialist’s choices no longer matched it.
3.  The Topic Leader would not silently replace the specialist slate with a preferred one.
    1.  The rejection would reopen work between the two offices.
    2.  If they could not resolve the disagreement, CAP moderators would decide how the project continued.

Doug described the arrangement as an **“executive-legislative check and balance.”**

We should not press that analogy too hard.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1194.png)

The Stats Leader is not Congress.

The Ability Leader does not represent Johto.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1195.png)

The useful part is that CAP was distinguishing powers that the Strong Topic Leader had carried together.

-   One office interpreted the overall project.
-   Another office translated a specialist discussion into ballot choices.
-   A veto interrupted the specialist without allowing the executive to write the replacement alone.
-   An additional layer existed for deadlock.

That is a constitution.

That is also how you decide whether to permit _Shell Smash_.

* * *

## **The Slate Moves.**

The most important transfer was **slating**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1233.png)

The Topic Leader could influence every discussion through opening language and active participation. Those powers remained significant. The decisive gate, however, would now be held by someone selected for the relevant stage.

-   The Typing Leader made the typing slate.
-   The Ability Leader made the ability slate.
-   The Stats Leader made the statistics slate.
-   The Movepool Leader made the movepool slate.

This changed the relation between expertise and authority.

-   Under the **Strong Topic Leader** model, one person’s ability to integrate the whole project supported broad control over each technical field.
-   Under the proposed **Topic Leader plus Topic Leadership Team** model, integrative contact and specialist contact received different offices.
    -   A brilliant Topic Leader might know less about base-stat construction than the Stats Leader.
    -   A brilliant Stats Leader might understand one phase deeply while lacking the judgment or interest required to direct the whole project.
        -   CAP no longer had to choose which person was simply **better**.
        -   It could ask which authority belonged to which decision.

This also gave prospective leaders a path between ordinary participation and the highest office.

## **The Veto: Not a Replacement Slate.**

The Topic Leader’s veto preserved the Concept across specialist stages.

It also risked restoring the old system through another door.

-   If the Topic Leader could repeatedly reject every specialist slate until the specialist guessed the desired answer,
    -   then the new office would be a slower version of the old one.

Participants noticed this.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1235.png)

Some proposed a supermajority override by the Topic Leadership Team. Others preferred moderator review. Some worried that the team and Topic Leader could form factions. Others thought the need to persuade several independent people already made a one-person capture much harder than it had been under CAP 4.

The version moving toward implementation kept the veto narrow.

-   The Topic Leader could call for a re-slate on Concept grounds.
-   The specialist retained the work of producing the new slate.
    -   Persistent disagreement moved outside both offices.

This is a much better instrument than an unlimited right answer.

-   The veto says the current transition cannot proceed.
-   It does not say the person who stopped it automatically owns the next transition.

That distinction is easy to describe in a rule and difficult to preserve in practice. The Topic Leader still possessed prestige, public influence, and the authority to define how the Concept applied. A specialist asked to revise a slate would know who had rejected it. The relation could become coercive even without a formal power to substitute.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1236.png)

The system therefore depended on behavior, documentation, moderator judgment, and the willingness of several people to keep disagreement inside the project rather than converting it into a loyalty test.

CAP had not engineered conflict out of existence; it had given conflict a route.

* * *

### **The Moderators Return.**

The final escalation path belonged to moderators.

This was contested for good reason.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1237.png)

Moderators were not elected for each creation project. Their forum authority did not automatically make them infallible interpreters of a competitive Concept. One participant objected that a community-selected office should not become subordinate to people whose final authority came from platform position rather than the current project’s vote.

Then the same participant reconsidered.

-   CAP needed somebody available when the immediate offices could not proceed.
    -   The existing moderators were, in practice, the people most likely to possess enough project knowledge, procedural standing, and continuity to carry that responsibility.

This locates the deeper problem. The temporary constitution of one CAP project sits inside a more durable CAP institution.

-   The Topic Leader and specialist leaders rotate.
-   The moderator layer persists.

That layer carries process memory, enforces boundaries, opens Policy Review, resolves deadlock, and decides who qualifies for some later leadership elections.

We will return to it.

For now, the significant achievement is that the moderator does not ordinarily become the Topic Leader either.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1238.png)

CAP’s durable authority provides a floor under the temporary offices without formally absorbing their everyday functions.

The kingdom remains. The crown moves.

## **The Prestige Problem.**

The Topic Leader survived the reform for another reason that had nothing to do with administrative efficiency.

> People wanted to become Topic Leader.

This office had become one of CAP’s central forms of recognition. An ordinary contributor could build knowledge, argue well, gain the community’s trust, and eventually receive responsibility for an entire creation. This was part of CAP’s identity as a project in which a person without broader Smogon status could rise through the work itself.

During the IRC discussion, Doug described the old attraction as allowing somebody to become **“king for a project”** and then pass the torch.

This is a strange and useful model of prestige.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/caimanoe.png)

The office confers real distinction. The distinction does not have to become tenure.

A previous Topic Leader remains part of CAP history. Their Pokémon remains playable. Their judgment can carry weight in later discussions. They can apply again, join Policy Review, become a moderator, or lead a specialist stage.

The next project still begins with another selection.

CAP had argued about that since 2008. The incumbent could not simply choose the next incumbent because the project had grown beyond personal delegation. The 2012 reform carried the same principle into the office itself.

The leader’s authority belonged to a bounded object.

-   This Pokémon.
-   This Concept.
-   This sequence of decisions.

The mandate ended when the project ended.

A leader might deserve another mandate.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/naviathan.png)

The previous mandate could not supply it automatically.

### **They Kept the King.**

The easiest summary of the 2012 reform would be that _CAP democratized Topic Leadership_.

That loses too much.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/necturna.png)

The community preserved an unusually powerful coordinating office. It did so after watching that office become powerful enough to reshape participation.

The participants were not confused about the danger. They also remembered the weak-leadership projects. They remembered drift. They remembered incompatible stages. They remembered how much work a clear Concept still required from a person capable of carrying it.

So they kept the king.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1239.png)

-   They gave the advisors control of the gates.
-   They gave the king a bounded veto.
-   They gave moderators the deadlock.
-   They opened several offices through which future leaders could gain experience.
-   And they kept the entire arrangement temporary at the level of each creation.

This did not prove the new system _worked_. It produced a system that could be tested.

That test would be a Grass/Dark apple snake named Malaconda.

## **The Topic Leader Was Still There.**

jas61292’s first concern was legibility.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1200.png)

During stages led by a specialist, some participants appeared to treat the Topic Leader as another ordinary poster. That made sense from one angle. The specialist was opening the thread and carrying the immediate decision. The entire reform had been designed to stop one Topic Leader from dominating every stage.

The Topic Leader still had to preserve the Concept across those stages.

If the community stopped recognizing that role whenever another person held the thread, CAP could lose the integrator while formally retaining the office.

This is a subtle implementation problem.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1201.png)

A constitution can distribute authority correctly on paper and communicate the distribution badly enough that participants recreate a different constitution through practice.

-   If everybody defers to the Topic Leader, the specialist offices become decorated assistants.
-   If everybody ignores the Topic Leader outside the Concept thread, the project returns to several local authorities making several local Pokémon.

The office needed to become less powerful and remain recognizable at the same time. That balance could not be produced by the title alone.

Participants had to learn the new relation.

The reform therefore required **culture** as well as rules.

## **The Question Marks.**

The more serious problem appeared in stages that did not produce a conventional slate.

-   The specialist made the slate.
    -   The Topic Leader could request a re-slate on Concept grounds.

-   Stat Limits did not simply ask the community to choose among completed stat spreads.

## **Good People: Not a Repeatability Plan.**

DougJustDoug’s later contribution to the CAP 5 reflection gives the reform its hardest test.

-   He considered the first run a major success.
-   He also worried that the result depended heavily on the particular people selected.

Each specialist had brought enough personality and energy to make the stage useful without trying to make that stage the most memorable part of the entire project. Nobody phoned the job in. Nobody used their jurisdiction to outperform the other offices. Nobody treated local leadership as a personal showcase.

This was excellent. This was also familiar.

Before Aurumoth, CAP had relied on Topic Leaders understanding the unwritten limits of a trusted office.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1208.png)

After Malaconda, it could not respond to success by relying on every future specialist to understand another set of unwritten limits.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1209.png)

Doug did not demand a rule for every possible personality failure. Some of the balance would remain cultural. CAP would have to celebrate leaders who carried their stage without making the stage about themselves and correct leaders who did not.

The significant change was that the institution now knew where to look.

The risk no longer sat entirely inside one Topic Leader. It could appear in a specialist overreaching, a Topic Leader disappearing from view, an undefined non-slating power, a moderator intervention, or a handoff between stages.

The reform had not removed leadership risk.

It had decomposed it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1210.png)

-   That made some failures less catastrophic.
-   It also made the constitution longer.

CAP accepted the trade.

## **The Constitution Produces Evidence.**

Malaconda did not prove that the **Topic Leader plus Topic Leadership Team** model would work forever.

It established several narrower facts.

-   A Topic Leader could surrender ordinary slating power in specialist stages without the project immediately losing coherence.
-   Specialist leaders could carry real authority without producing several incompatible creatures.
-   More contributors could gain leadership experience inside one project.
-   The community could complete a creation under the new arrangement and then separate praise for the overall model from criticism of the undefined interfaces.

The new constitution had moved from argument into operation.

**Operation** generated questions the argument had missed.

The institution returned to those questions before CAP 6.

It asked what happened when the reform had to make an apple snake.

The answer was good enough to continue.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1240.png)

It was also incomplete enough to revise.

And the answer carried another cost that would become harder to ignore as the process grew more sophisticated.

-   Five leaders can distribute authority.
    -   Five leaders also have schedules.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1211.png)

## **The Coordination Burden Returns.**

By 2014, Create-A-Pokémon had solved enough of the one-leader problem to produce a spreadsheet problem.

This was progress.

The Topic Leader no longer controlled every major competitive stage. Typing, abilities, statistics, and movepool had been distributed across specialist leaders. Malaconda had survived the first full run of the new Topic Leader plus Topic Leadership Team system. CAP had reviewed the result, found some undefined jurisdictions, and continued.

The project was now more plural. It was also taking a while.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/volkritter-1.png)

On June 28, 2014, DougJustDoug opened a Policy Review thread called **Project Pace and Timeboxing**. He had the feeling that CAP was moving too slowly. Rather than leave that feeling in its natural internet form, he collected dates for every completed creation project from Syclant through Volkraken, built a spreadsheet, measured public participation periods, measured total project periods, measured the dead time between projects, and posted the tables to Smogon.

The constitutional convention had entered Excel.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1214.png)

Doug’s initial complaint was blunt. CAP had reached a point where producing two Pokémon in one year felt normal. Earlier generations had moved faster. He could see the difference. He could not yet tell whether the difference came from longer discussions, more stages, slower handoffs, larger gaps between projects, or nostalgia for a period in which everyone had fewer rules and worse judgment.

So he measured it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1213.png)

The table did not produce one clean story.

Syclant, the first project, used fifty days of public forum participation and ninety-two days from opening to the end of playtesting. Revenankh used seventy-two public days and the same ninety-two total days. Several later Diamond and Pearl creations moved faster: Kitsunoh used thirty-nine public days and sixty-one total days; Arghonaut used forty-two and fifty-five.

The newer projects were longer again.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1215.png)

-   Aurumoth used sixty-seven public-participation days and eighty-nine total project days.
-   Malaconda used eighty-one and 102.
-   Cawmodore used 111 and 144.
-   Volkraken, the most recent completed project in the table, used eighty and 106.

The post-project lulls varied even more. Five days here. Sixty there. Eighty-four after Cawmodore. One hundred sixty-four after Voodoom.

There was no single “CAP got slow” number.

There was an expanding institution with several different clocks.

### **Four Pokémon Per Year.**

Doug proposed a goal.

> CAP should try to make four Pokémon per year.

One per quarter.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1216.png)

He knew the number was somewhat arbitrary. The point was to establish a planning target large enough to require visible expectations. Topic Leader and Topic Leadership Team selection would take a known amount of time. Competitive stages would receive approximate durations. Polls already operated on clocks; discussions could too. The four major competitive sections—typing, abilities, statistics, and movepool—might be given roughly equivalent space in the schedule.

The comparison Doug used was a **corporation**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1217.png)

Hundreds of people participated in a recurring project that produced a recognizable product.

Corporations plan product cycles.

CAP could plan a product cycle.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1218.png)

This sounds reasonable until the product is a Pokémon and one department is Movepool.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1219.png)

Typing can sometimes close quickly. The project needs to decide whether the creature is Electric/Fighting or Water/Ghost or whatever other mistake has acquired support.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1220.png)

Abilities often require at least two linked decisions. Statistics involve broad limits, complete submissions, technical evaluation, revision, and a poll.

A movepool requires participants to determine which attacks and support moves the Pokémon should be able to learn, which combinations would invalidate intended weaknesses, which official move-distribution patterns matter for flavor, and whether a complete submission obeys every limit accumulated across the preceding threads.

A serious movepool submission can be thousands of words long.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1221.png)

These stages all matter. They do not contain equal labor because the leadership chart gives them equal boxes.

bugmaniacbob attacked the proposed symmetry directly. Typing would generally remain shorter. Abilities and statistics contained multiple internal phases. Movepool took far longer to construct than an ability argument or one statistical spread. Making those sections occupy equal time would either leave some of them sitting open after the work had ended or force others to close before the work was complete.

The project had distributed authority according to domain.

The calendar was trying to flatten the domains back into equivalence.

## **A Discussion Is Finished When It Is Finished.**

The strongest objection concerned _the purpose of CAP itself_.

**Create-A-Pokémon** does produce Pokémon.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1222.png)

Its process documents **also** describe the project as an investigation of competitive Pokémon through public discussion and playtesting. These arguments are not waste surrounding the creature. They are part of the thing the project exists to do.

That makes a discussion harder to schedule than a poll.

-   A poll can open for a defined period and close when the period ends.
-   A discussion may settle in three days.
    -   It may discover its central question on day four.
        -   One argument can create another thread because the participants have learned that the category being discussed is wrong.

A fixed week can waste four days after consensus has formed. A fixed three days can close the thread several hours before the useful disagreement arrives.

bugmaniacbob described exactly this problem. New topics could emerge late enough that nobody responsible for the schedule could know in advance how much time the discussion required. The thread should close after it had fulfilled its purpose. Timeboxing risked making the leader responsible for serving both the inquiry and the calendar when the two no longer aligned.

Then geography entered.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1241.png)

Most visible forum activity occurred around American time zones. bugmaniacbob lived several hours ahead. He could post, go to sleep before replies arrived, return a day later, and discover that the conversation had already moved past the point where his reply belonged. A forty-eight-hour discussion did not give every participant forty-eight equivalent hours. For some people it gave two chances to speak, separated by an entire turn of the field.

-   The calendar is formally equal.
-   Access to the calendar is not.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1242.png)

A compressed process rewards people who are awake near the center of activity, available throughout the day, confident enough to post before reading everything, and able to revise quickly when replies arrive.

That may be acceptable in a speed-critical field.

CAP was not trying to launch a missile.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1243.png)

It was trying to learn something about Pokémon.

The institution therefore had to decide which loss mattered more:

-   participants losing interest while a dead thread remains open;
-   or participants losing access because a live thread closes before their time zone can answer.

No schedule solves that question by existing.

## **The Slow Part Was Sometimes Nobody Acting.**

The timeboxing debate gradually separated two kinds of delay.

-   One delay belonged to the work.
    -   People were still arguing.
    -   Submissions were still being built.
    -   A specialist was still reading the field.
-   The other delay belonged to the transition between pieces of work.
    -   The discussion had ended.
        -   The next thread had not opened.
    -   A slate was ready.
        -   The poll was waiting for someone with permission to post it.
    -   A Topic Leadership Team member had finished one stage and needed the Topic Leader, moderator, or next specialist to receive the handoff.

Policy Review threads waited for someone to declare that the discussion had reached a conclusion. Other threads remained locked until the appropriate person was online. Participants could see that nothing useful was happening and still lack the authority to make the next useful thing happen.

This was a different problem.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1244.png)

Cutting discussion time would not repair it.

Bughouse proposed several less dramatic changes. Do not lock threads unless there is a reason. Coordinate leaders’ schedules before one stage ends. Prepare the reasoning for a slate before the thread closes. Make sure the next person knows the handoff is coming. Avoid inserting days of administrative silence between two decisions whose substantive work is already complete.

The project did not need every debate to happen faster.

It needed _finished work_ to become **the next work**.

This is where distributed leadership reveals its invoice.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1246.png)

-   One Topic Leader carrying every stage can move directly from their own conclusion to their own next instruction.
-   A Topic Leader plus four specialists creates interfaces.
    -   The Ability Leader finishes.
        -   The Topic Leader reviews.
            -   A moderator posts or approves the poll.
                -   The next specialist prepares.
                    -   The community receives the new thread.

Each boundary protects something.

Each boundary can also become a day in which everybody waits for one corporeal being behind a computer screen.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/aydrwhX.gif)

CAP had reduced the danger that one person could turn an entire project into their preferred Pokémon.

It had increased the number of moments at which one absent person could stop the project from becoming _any Pokémon at all_.

This was not an argument for restoring the Strong Topic Leader.

It was the cost of the repair becoming legible.

* * *

## **Leadership Still Had a Body.**

The pace thread also returned to burnout.

The Topic Leadership Team had distributed the workload.

The workload remained large.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1247.png)

jas61292, who had led Malaconda under the new structure, described the experience as excellent and something he never wanted to do again. Another former specialist said the role had consumed most of their free time while still leaving them feeling behind. Accelerating the project would shorten the calendar by increasing pressure on the people already carrying the transitions.

A process can look slow from the queue and frantic from inside the office.

-   The public sees three days without a new thread.
-   The leader sees forty submissions, private coordination, technical checks, a slate explanation, an argument on IRC, school, work, and the requirement to write something sufficiently clear that several hundred people can continue from it.

Neither perception is false.

The institution has to carry both.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1248.png)

Volunteer governance makes this especially visible. CAP can define an office. It cannot purchase the officeholder’s evening. It can require activity. It cannot eliminate illness, employment, exams, family, sleep, or the possibility that somebody has had enough of determining whether Shed Skin deserves another poll.

The role remains attached to a body.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1249.png)

That was the succession problem in 2008. It was now the pace problem too.

* * *

## **The Spreadsheet Does Not Become the Schedule.**

The Policy Review did not adopt Doug’s timeboxing proposal.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1250.png)

This is worth preserving exactly because the thread contained careful work, _extensive_ data, and no large formal reform at the end.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1251.png)

The participants gradually converged on a narrower use for the measurements.

-   Historical averages could help leaders notice when a discussion had probably exhausted itself.

A typical duration could inform judgment. It should not become a deadline pretending to be judgment.

The distinction became almost microscopic.

-   “This is how long this kind of thread _usually_ lasts.”
    -   Fine.
-   “This is how long this thread **will** last.”
    -   Different constitution.
-   The first gives a leader evidence.
-   The second gives the schedule authority over the leader.

The forum and IRC discussions repeatedly returned to that line. People could support clearer expectations, better warnings, and tidier thread conclusions while rejecting a system in which a live discussion acquired a presumptive expiration date before anyone knew what the discussion would discover.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1253.png)

On July 18, Doug closed the thread without implementing a schedule. The timing data itself might change future behavior by making historical norms visible. Posting formal expectations could also create unproductive pressure on discussion leaders. CAP would proceed into CAP 19 and revisit the issue later if needed.

The spreadsheet remained. The deadline did not.

That is still an institutional outcome.

A review can improve the field without producing a new rule. Participants learned to distinguish substantive duration from transition latency. They named the handoff problem. They identified time-zone asymmetry. They made leadership burnout part of the record. They acquired a baseline against which later projects could be judged.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1252.png)

The constitution did not change text.

The people operating it had another instrument.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1254.png)

This is less satisfying than reform, and often how institutions actually learn.

## **Five Leaders Also Have Schedules.**

The Topic Leader plus Topic Leadership Team system survived.

CAP had reduced concentrated slating power, brought specialists closer to the decisions they understood, created more leadership positions, and made it possible for several people to acquire operating contact during one project.

The pace debate exposed what came with that achievement.

-   More people could lead.
    -   More leaders had to coordinate.

Every handoff became an institutional event. Every stage needed somebody capable of recognizing when discussion had matured, somebody capable of converting that maturity into a slate, somebody capable of opening the next path, and enough shared memory that the next office did not restart the project from its own preferred beginning.

-   Distribution had not removed the coordination burden.
-   It had moved the burden into the interfaces.

That would matter in 2016, when the problem stopped being a delayed thread.

> The Topic Leader left in the middle of an active creation.

CAP had to find out whether the interfaces carried enough of the project to keep going.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1258.png)

The Pokémon was called Kerfluffle.

## **Kerfluffle Changes Masters.**

In July 2016, Create-A-Pokémon’s succession system stopped being a policy discussion.

-   The Topic Leader left.
    -   CAP 22 had already begun.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1259.png)

The community had selected a concept called **Last Act of Defiance**, centered on the move Parting Shot. Parting Shot lowers the opposing Pokémon’s physical and special offensive power, then removes its user from battle and brings in a teammate. It is a retreat that changes the field before the replacement arrives.

The move was interesting enough to deserve a project and poorly represented enough in ordinary competitive play that CAP could plausibly learn something by designing a better user for it.

The chosen Topic Leader was sparktrain.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1260.png)

The Topic Leadership Team consisted of Elite Lord Sigma for typing, snake\_rattler for abilities, Deck Knight for statistics, and cbrevan for movepool.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1261.png)

The project moved through Concept Assessment under sparktrain’s direction. CAP decided to emphasize a relatively fast Parting Shot user capable of creating enough offensive pressure that opponents would sometimes switch before the move was even used. Typing followed. The community selected Fairy/Fighting.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1263.png)

By July 6, the art-submission thread could list a concept, a strategic direction, a typing, and the full five-person leadership team.

Two days later, the list changed.

On July 8, DougJustDoug opened a short announcement.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1264.png)

sparktrain was stepping down because of real-life commitments and priorities.

The change was effective immediately.

That decision deserves exact attention.

-   CAP did not reopen the entire Topic Leader election.
    -   It did not pause the project while every candidate prepared a new application.

The moderation team therefore did not discover a successor after the vacancy.

This is not the same thing as saying the succession followed the ordinary democratic procedure. It did not.

The community had elected sparktrain as Topic Leader. Moderators appointed cbrevan after the office became vacant.

The legitimacy changed shape because the field had changed speed.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1270.png)

CAP needed someone capable of acting _immediately_, and the person selected already carried public support, specialist office, metagame knowledge, and direct contact with the project.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1272.png)

The emergency path borrowed legitimacy from the ordinary path while refusing to repeat the ordinary path in full.

That is what emergency succession procedures often do.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1271.png)

The question is whether the shortcut preserves enough of the institution to justify the authority it grants.

CAP 22 was about to find out through a very large pillow.

## **One Vacancy Moves Three Offices.**

Promoting cbrevan did not fill the vacancy. It moved the vacancy.

-   CAP still needed a Movepool Leader.
    -   So Deck Knight shifted from Stats Leader to Movepool Leader.
-   Now CAP needed a Stats Leader.
    -   So Snobalt joined the Topic Leadership Team in that role.

The transition was therefore not one replacement.

This was a small institutional cascade.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1273.png)

-   The Movepool Leader became Topic Leader.
-   The Stats Leader became Movepool Leader.
-   A previous leadership candidate entered as Stats Leader.

Snobalt had finished fifth in the original Topic Leadership Team poll, immediately behind the four selected specialists.

The leadership announcement does not say that this result mechanically entitled him to the vacancy. The sequence still matters. CAP had conducted a public selection process broad enough to identify more qualified people than the initial project could appoint. When an office opened, the institution did not have to invent a new pool.

The losing candidate was still standing near the work.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1274.png)

That is a healthier meaning of _losing_ than most elections provide.

An unsuccessful application had not removed Snobalt from the institution, declared him unfit forever, or converted the winner’s temporary advantage into permanent title. It had located him inside a visible order of support and competence from which later responsibility remained reachable.

-   The TLT poll had selected four leaders.
-   It had also mapped a bench.

This is one of the quiet advantages of recurring temporary office. Every application cycle produces more information than the immediate vacancy can use. Candidates explain their experience. The community evaluates them. People who do not receive office remain available for later projects, specialist work, emergency replacement, or another election.

Succession capacity accumulates in the people who almost won too.

## **Honor the Direction.**

The most important sentence in the transfer announcement concerned continuity.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1275.png)

cbrevan would bring his own approach and perspective to the remaining stages. He also agreed to preserve the decisions and directions CAP 22 had already established.

That is a more difficult promise than either total obedience or total restart.

The concept had been selected. sparktrain’s Concept Assessment had emphasized a fast Parting Shot user that could force switches through offensive presence or support pressure. Fairy/Fighting typing had been chosen through the next stage. The project had already spent weeks converting an open concept into a more specific path.

cbrevan did not receive authority to pretend none of that had happened.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1276.png)

He also did not receive an instruction to imitate sparktrain’s every unwritten preference.

-   The predecessor’s direction had become part of the project.
-   It no longer belonged only to the predecessor.

This is where public process begins carrying succession capacity.

The forum archive contained the concept, the arguments, the stage conclusions, and the decisions already made. The other specialist leaders had participated in producing and interpreting that record. The community knew what it had voted for. The new Topic Leader could read the path, test later proposals against it, and continue making independent judgments without claiming access to sparktrain’s private mind.

The project had externalized enough of its direction to survive a change in the person carrying direction.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1277.png)

That is not documentation replacing leadership.

The archive could not open the next thread, resolve the next conflict, or decide which ability arguments had matured into a slate.

It could tell the new leader what the office had inherited.

A succession plan needs both.

-   A person capable of acting.
-   A path capable of being inherited.

## **The New Topic Leader Speaks.**

cbrevan accepted the role in the announcement thread.

He acknowledged sparktrain’s absence, accepted the direction already established, and promised to carry the project toward a successful completion.

This is a small post. It does not attempt to found a new era.

There is no theory of why CAP 22 had secretly required cbrevan all along.

The successor does not rehabilitate his authority by discrediting the predecessor.

He says, in effect:

-   The project has a direction.
-   I am taking responsibility for moving it.
    -   Then he does.

CAP 22 continued into abilities, statistics, movepool, art, name, and the rest of the creation sequence. The project encountered ordinary complications. The final thread later described the leadership change and some gaps between stages without presenting either as fatal.

The process did not become perfectly smooth because the constitution worked.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1278.png)

The project remained a volunteer institution coordinating technical debate through forum threads during people’s actual lives.

It kept going.

That is the test here.

## **The Person Leaves.**

## **The Direction Does Not.**

CAP 22 did not prove that every Topic Leadership Team member can become Topic Leader. It did not prove that moderator appointment is always the correct emergency procedure. It did not prove that public elections can be suspended whenever speed becomes convenient.

The case establishes something narrower.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1283.png)

Create-A-Pokémon had built enough distributed contact before the vacancy that leadership could move without forcing the project to choose between restoring the departed leader and abandoning the creation.

-   cbrevan already knew the metagame.
-   He already held office.
-   He already knew the people carrying the other stages.
-   He already had access to the public reasoning behind the project’s direction.
-   Snobalt had already passed through the leadership-selection field and remained close enough to enter when the specialist cascade reached him.

The succession mechanism therefore carried capacity because ordinary operation had already produced potential successors.

This is the part many institutions reverse.

They concentrate operating knowledge in the incumbent because the incumbent is good. They minimize overlap because overlap looks inefficient. They keep prospective successors away from consequential decisions because consequential decisions belong to the person currently trusted to make them.

Then, the incumbent leaves.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1284.png)

The institution discovers that indispensability was partly manufactured by the way it allocated contact.

CAP’s Topic Leadership Team produces some redundancy on purpose.

Several people watch the project form.

Several people lead real decisions.

Several people learn the interfaces.

Nobody else becomes a complete duplicate of the Topic Leader.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1285.png)

Someone can still become capable of carrying the coordination office because the office has not consumed every route into operating knowledge.

That is how succession begins before removal.

## **Two Correction Paths.**

Kerfluffle proved that Create-A-Pokémon could change leaders without losing the creation. It did not answer what happened after the creation was finished.

-   The Topic Leader closes the last stage.
    -   The final product appears.
    -   The Pokémon is implemented on Pokémon Showdown.
        -   Then several thousand battles begin discovering what the project _actually_ made.

This is a different kind of succession problem.

The creature now has to outlive the theory that produced it.

CAP had always possessed ways to argue about its process. The project calls that work **Policy Review**. Participants return to the rules, reconstruct what happened, and propose changes to leadership, polling, stage order, timing, submissions, eligibility, or whatever other part of the machinery has become impossible to ignore.

-   The Aurumoth crisis entered Policy Review.
-   The Topic Leader plus Topic Leadership Team system entered Policy Review.
-   The limits on Topic Leader vetoes entered Policy Review.
-   Timeboxing entered Policy Review
    -   and mostly left again without becoming the large reform its proposer wanted.

**Policy Review** asks how CAP should make the next Pokémon.

**The metagame** asks what the last Pokémon became.

For a long time, those conversations did not meet cleanly.

## **The Process Ends Before the Evidence Arrives.**

A CAP project is organized around an inquiry.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1293.png)

The community chooses a Concept, converts that Concept into a sequence of design questions, and makes one competitive Pokémon capable of entering the CAP metagame.

The process produces reasons.

-   Typing was selected for this purpose.
-   An ability was chosen because it opened this relation.
-   The statistics were meant to preserve this weakness.
-   The movepool was designed to let the creature perform this role without performing every other role too.

Then the final product enters play.

The opponent does not care about the minutes.

-   A player finds a set nobody centered during the process.
-   An ability expected to be secondary becomes the best way to use the Pokémon.
-   A defensive benchmark that looked conservative on a spreadsheet combines with recovery, typing, and one move to become miserable in practice.
-   A supposed weakness remains technically present and strategically irrelevant.

Or, the creature works.

It fulfills the Concept through exactly the interaction the community spent months investigating.

The process can still learn from that success.

For years, CAP had no formal stage dedicated to receiving this answer. Competitive players discussed new creations in tournament chats, on Pokémon Showdown, on Discord, and in metagame threads. People involved in the creation formed their own judgments. Later projects inherited some of those lessons through community memory.

The public process archive still ended near the point when the most important new evidence began.

In August 2019, GMars opened a Policy Review proposal called **Filling the Gap: CAP Post-Play Lookback**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1294.png)

The title was exact.

GMars described a **“distinct divide between process and metagame.”** The CAP metagame existed partly to test how well the community had understood and executed a Concept. The formal process was not using that instrument to close its own loop.

So add another stage.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1295.png)

-   Wait long enough for real play to settle the first-wave panic.
-   Bring the Topic Leader back.
-   Ask what the community learned.
-   Allow narrowly bounded changes if the field revealed a specific problem.
    -   Then send the findings forward before the next creation began.

This proposal immediately found several more constitutional questions waiting inside it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1296.png)

* * *

## **Who Reviews the Reviewers?**

The first dispute concerned leadership.

GMars wanted the Topic Leader to run the Post Play Lookback.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1297.png)

The reason was continuity. The people who made the Pokémon understood the questions the process had been trying to answer. The Topic Leader could reconnect competitive evidence to the original Concept without allowing the lookback to become a generic balance hearing run entirely by the metagame council.

Another participant saw the conflict immediately.

> Why should the people who made the creature lead the critique of what they made?

The Topic Leader had already shaped the discussions, finalized slates, and defended a project direction. Asking that same office to guide the public interpretation of the result could let the original theory govern the evidence arriving against it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1298.png)

A senior moderator or another experienced participant might be less invested in the outcome.

This is the same argument CAP keeps having at different scales.

Contact matters. Distance matters.

-   The people closest to the work know why the choices were made.
-   The people far enough away may be better able to recognize what those choices produced.

CAP did not solve the conflict by pretending one side was imaginary.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1299.png)

The current **Post Play Lookback** remains part of the creation process and is led by the Topic Leader. The discussion is public. Proposed changes move through submissions and voting rather than becoming one leader’s personal patch. The available repair is narrow enough that the review cannot secretly remake the entire Pokémon under a new theory.

The leader returns. They do not return alone.

That difference matters.

* * *

## **A Patch, Not a Reincarnation.**

The second dispute concerned the word **minor**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1300.png)

A Post Play Lookback was not supposed to reopen typing.

It was not supposed to replace the primary ability, tear away a hundred base-stat points, or exchange half the movepool for a new one.

Those changes would invalidate entire stages of the public process.

A disappointed community could wait until the Pokémon entered play, announce that the experiment had gone badly, and build a different Pokémon while preserving the old project’s name.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1301.png)

GMars proposed a smaller jurisdiction.

-   A few stat points.
-   One move.
-   A secondary ability,
    -   perhaps,
        -   if that ability had unexpectedly overwhelmed the primary design.
-   And always the option of doing nothing.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1303.png)

This boundary is more than respect for past votes. It preserves the object being tested. If every disappointing result authorizes a redesign, the field never gets to answer the original decision. The institution replaces the evidence with another intervention before learning what the first intervention did.

The Post Play Lookback therefore has to be capable of correction and incapable of escape. It can tune. It cannot flee into a new Concept wearing the same art.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1304.png)

One participant objected that changing a Pokémon shortly after completion disrespected the people who had just made it. GMars answered by treating the stage as the continuation of the same creation team: the community receives feedback and releases a patch. The game survives the patch. The project remains the project.

CAP had spent years learning how to let a successor preserve direction without preserving identity.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1305.png)

Now it was building a way for the product to preserve identity without preserving every number.

## **The Bird.**

In May 2026, CAP 37 produced Obliteryx.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1306.png)

Obliteryx is a Dark/Flying Pokémon built around a Concept called **Perfect Conditions**.

The community wanted to investigate **conditional moves**: attacks or effects whose value changes depending on what the opponent does, what state the battle occupies, or whether some trigger occurs.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1315.png)

The selected move was _Beak Blast_.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1307.png)

_Beak Blast_ is a strong Flying-type attack that begins charging before it strikes. If the opponent makes contact with the user during that preparation, the opponent is burned.

The move therefore changes the turn before it deals damage.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1308.png)

-   Attack into it and risk the burn.
-   Switch and give the bird room.
-   Use a non-contact move.
-   Set up.
-   Try to exploit the charge.

The threat of the condition can matter even when the condition never activates.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1309.png)

Obliteryx received the abilities **Opportunist** and **Analytic**. **Opportunist** copies an opponent’s stat boosts. **Analytic** increases damage when the user moves after its target. Its initial statistics were 102 HP, 128 Attack, 126 Defense, 45 Special Attack, 90 Special Defense, and 64 Speed. It also received _Knock Off_, _Roost_, _Sucker Punch_, _Taunt_, _Stealth Rock_, _Body Press_, and enough other tools to make several roles very plausible.

This bird had **_options_**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1310.png)

Then people played it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1311.png)

Eight weeks of CAP Premier League and a playtest tournament supplied a much larger competitive record than the creation process had possessed. Topic Leader earthflax reopened the project with four questions.

1.  Had Obliteryx fulfilled the Concept?
2.  What role had it actually taken in the metagame?
3.  Did it need changes?
4.  What had CAP learned?

-   The first answer was generally favorable.
    -   Obliteryx had explored conditionality.
        -   The opponent’s knowledge that _Beak Blast_ could burn contact shaped choices even when the burn never occurred.
        -   Opportunist changed how freely teams could set up while Obliteryx remained unrevealed.
            -   The creature could make progress through what an opponent did and through what the opponent avoided doing.
-   The second answer was less comfortable.
    -   Analytic had given the bird another entire life.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1312.png)

### **The Red Card.**

During actual play, Spammernoob developed a Red Card set.

Red Card is an item that forces an attacker out after striking the holder.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1313.png)

Obliteryx could survive a hit, throw the attacker out, and use the disruption to create an enormous swing. **Analytic** strengthened the attacks available around that plan. The same Pokémon could also run Heavy-Duty Boots, Dark Glasses, Life Orb, Choice Band, defensive investment, offensive investment, _Stealth Rock_, _Taunt_, _Body Press_, or other combinations.

-   The process had intentionally made a flexible creature.
-   The metagame found more flexibility than it wanted.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1316.png)

**Analytic** also created an ability-identification problem.

An opponent could not safely assume Obliteryx was running **Opportunist** and avoid stat boosts by changing play accordingly. It might be **Analytic** and simply hit much harder. Nor could the opponent assume **Analytic** and begin setting up freely, because an unrevealed **Opportunist** set could copy the boosts and end the game.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1314.png)

The two abilities improved one another through uncertainty.

This was conceptually interesting. It was also terrible to play against.

Guingil, who had supported several of the relevant design choices (and would soon apply for CAP 38’s Topic Leadership Team), gave the compact summary:

> **“remove analytic, process good, learnt a lot”**

That is exactly what the Post Play Lookback exists to make possible.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1317.png)

The process can have been good.

The process can have learned something real.

One component can still need to leave.

The discussion did not stop at **Analytic**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1318.png)

Participants proposed reducing HP, Special Defense, Attack, or Speed. They proposed removing _Stealth Rock_, _Body Press_, _Knock Off_, or _Encore_. Some wanted _Defog_ added so Obliteryx would retain a support role after losing power elsewhere. Others wanted to preserve **Analytic** and attack the oppressive combinations around it instead.

The Topic Leader eventually produced a slate of four complete packages.

All four removed **Analytic**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1319.png)

Every package also made some smaller change.

-   One removed moves and added Defog.
-   One distributed a small set of stat reductions and a Special Attack increase.
-   One removed twelve points of Special Defense.
-   One removed ten points of HP.

The public vote selected Guingil’s package.

-   **Analytic** left.
    -   Special Defense fell from 90 to 78.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1320.png)

The secondary competitive slot became **No Competitive Ability**.

Later flavor work supplied **Early Bird** and **Sniper** as noncompetitive abilities around **Opportunist**. The 2.0 release records both the original and revised forms in the same final-product thread.

Obliteryx survived. Its spreadsheet did not.

## **The Process Did Not Go on Trial.**

The Obliteryx lookback is valuable because the community did not have to choose one total story.

-   CAP 37 succeeded.
    -   CAP 37 overtuned the product.
-   The Concept worked.
    -   **Analytic** worked too well.
-   The community had reasons for selecting it.
    -   The metagame supplied reasons for removing it.
-   Guingil could support **Analytic** during the process,
    -   later submit the winning package against it,
        -   and then cite both experiences when applying for leadership.
            -   Changing judgment under evidence did not require an apology for participating in the original decision.

This is a healthy relation to institutional memory.

The archive preserves who argued for what.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1321.png)

The return path does not turn that record into a prosecution list.

The field answered. People changed their minds.

The bird lost twelve Special Defense.

## **Policy Review Is Somewhere Else.**

The Post Play Lookback can reveal process problems too.

-   Perhaps CAP repeatedly gives secondary abilities too much room.
-   Perhaps later stages keep adding insurance against every weakness preserved earlier.
-   Perhaps the Topic Leader needs to participate more visibly in a disputed specialist stage.
-   Perhaps the community needs another rule for the relationship between flexibility and power.

Those findings do not all belong inside Obliteryx.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1323.png)

Some belong in Policy Review.

The current Policy Review forum exists specifically for changes to how CAP discussions and creation stages are conducted. Its procedure is different from the ordinary creation process. Policy Review Committee participants receive the ability to open and vote in policy threads. Discussions remain publicly viewable. Threads stay open long enough to develop, moderators identify consensus, and the moderation team ultimately approves or rejects the proposed change.

-   That is governance correction.
-   The Post Play Lookback is product correction.
    -   One can lead into the other.
    -   They should not collapse.

A governance reform should not be smuggled into one Pokémon patch because a recent battle was annoying.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1324.png)

A Pokémon should not be forced to remain defective because the institution needs six months to decide whether its secondary-ability policy was wise.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1325.png)

Different objects need different return paths.

This is where CAP’s accumulated procedure becomes genuinely impressive.

-   The community has developed one archive for asking how authority behaved.
-   It has another stage for asking how the creation behaved.

The answers can disagree without canceling one another.

-   **The constitution needs a review path.**
-   **The product needs a return path.**

A process can deserve correction without condemning every Pokémon it produced.

A Pokémon can require correction without voiding the process that made it.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1326.png)

Now CAP 38 needed people to carry both paths forward.

Applications were open.

The next temporary government was introducing itself.

## **The Résumé = the Theory.**

A CAP leadership application has an unusual evidence problem.

> Nobody can prove they will lead CAP 38 well.

CAP 38 does not exist yet.

The Concept has not been selected. The disputes have not occurred. The Typing Leader does not know which typings will acquire support. The Ability Leader does not know which ability will become the next source of community trauma.

The candidate can point backward.

-   Previous discussions.
-   Winning submissions.
-   Tournament performance.
-   Analysis writing.
-   Moderation.
-   Past Topic Leadership Team service.
-   Technical tools.
-   Side projects.

The public record shows where the person has acquired contact with the work.

Then the candidate has to make an inference from that contact toward a future office.

That inference is exactly what this article has been auditing.

Experience is evidence. It is not automatic title.

CAP 38 has received three applications for Topic Leader.

They did not describe the same leader.

## **Qualified for the Ballot.**

CAP does not send every application directly into an unrestricted vote.

The official Topic Leader selection guide places another institution between self-nomination and public choice.

After applications close, the forum moderation staff selects the candidates it considers qualified.

Those candidates enter a public **Ranked Pairs** poll.

-   The Topic Leader is selected first.
-   The specialist leadership team follows separately.

This is neither a pure election nor a private appointment.

-   Moderators control qualification.
-   Community members rank the qualified candidates.

The two layers answer different questions.

-   Who can plausibly carry the office?
-   Which plausible carrier does the community prefer?

CAP does not pretend those questions are identical.

The arrangement has clear advantages.

-   A specialized project can reject candidacies without enough contact to carry the role.
-   The current leader does not privately choose an heir.
-   The moderators do not simply appoint their favorite applicant.
-   The public gets a real decision among candidates who have survived an experience threshold.

The arrangement also places enormous authority in the threshold.

We are going to leave that problem open for one more section.

For now, the applications are doing something the final vote cannot show.

They reveal the bench.

The historical context matters.

CAP had nearly been pushed out of Smogon during its early growth.

Doug had built and operated the simulator infrastructure that made the created Pokémon playable, paid costs, managed attacks and moderation problems, negotiated CAP’s institutional relationship with Smogon, written process documentation, and accepted responsibility for keeping the project from collapsing under the consequences of its own popularity.

Control followed burden. The person carrying the server, agreement, programming, administration, and outside risk acquired the strongest claim over the framework.

This is another form of formative authority. It is also the permanent-layer version of the problem this article has been following.

-   The project needed infrastructure.
    -   The infrastructure had a person.
        -   The person’s contact became constitutional title.

Doug’s own document recognized both sides.

-   He claimed final authority over the framework
    -   and argued strongly for rotating responsibility over individual Pokémon.

He did not want to lead every creation. He wanted other people to receive responsibility, control, satisfaction, and public credit. He also wanted to cultivate future leaders because the existing bench looked thin.

Temporary kings existed inside a durable throne.

CAP did not hide this in 2012. It put it in the guide.

## **The Current Structure Has Moved.**

It would be lazy to freeze that document over the present community.

The 2026 directory does not list DougJustDoug as sole head of CAP.

spoo holds the CAP Leader role.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1368.png)

Doug appears as one moderator among several and as a specialist for project history and creative rights.

Moderation has expanded and turned over repeatedly. The staff-announcement history records leaders and moderators stepping down, returning, and being added. The project now operates inside a mature Smogon and Pokémon Showdown infrastructure rather than one person’s home-office battle server.

Policy Review has a defined committee application process. Topic Leadership selection uses public applications and voting. Specialist leadership is distributed. The historical founder remains influential without occupying the exact 2012 office described in his compendium.

That is real constitutional movement. The deeper layer still exists.

-   Someone determines overall direction.
    -   Moderators manage the polls.
    -   Moderators enforce discussion rules.
    -   Moderators decide which Topic Leader candidates are qualified.
    -   Moderators identify Policy Review consensus and vote whether to approve a proposal.
    -   Smogon and Pokémon Showdown supply the forum, accounts, simulator, staffing environment, and wider platform within which CAP remains reachable.
        -   The temporary office does not own these conditions.
        -   The public vote does not directly recreate them every project.

That is the permanent constitution.

## **Policy Review Has a Gate Too.**

CAP’s Policy Review Committee is designed to prevent the opposite failure: **past prestige** becoming **automatic lifetime jurisdiction**.

Applicants explain their CAP, Smogon, and Pokémon experience. Acceptance is not capped at a fixed number, and rejection from one committee is not permanent. People can acquire more experience and apply again. Accepted members receive the ability to open threads, participate, and vote in Policy Review during that cycle.

This arrangement keeps policy work attached to current contact.

It also creates another qualification gate.

-   Not everyone can open a Policy Review thread.
-   Not every proposed change reaches a binding vote.
-   Moderators decide when discussion has reached consensus,
    -   and the moderation team votes on approval.

This can protect the project from endless constitutional litigation by participants who have not learned the machinery they want to change. It can also make the supervisory layer the final interpreter of whether a challenge to the supervisory layer is mature enough to proceed.

There is no automatic contradiction here. Just a jurisdiction worth seeing.

A constitution cannot become corrigible by writing **correction available** over a door whose custodian still decides which concerns count as a knock.

CAP does more than many institutions to document that door.

The custodian remains.

## **The Platform Problem.**

-   A Topic Leader who violates CAP rules can be corrected by moderators.
-   A specialist who produces an unacceptable slate can be checked by the Topic Leader,
    -   then by moderators.
-   A completed Pokémon can return through the Post Play Lookback.
-   A process rule can return through Policy Review.

> What happens when the problem concerns **the platform itself**?

The current staff directory says the CAP Leader communicates with senior Smogon and Pokémon Showdown staff on administrative changes.

That is a bridge. It is not public evidence of a complete constitutional route.

CAP does not own Smogon or Pokémon Showdown.

Its community depends on accounts, forum software, moderation permissions, simulator implementation, server operation, and institutional recognition supplied through larger systems.

The relationship can be stable and beneficial. It still places some conditions of CAP’s continuance outside the temporary offices this article has examined.

A community can rotate its Topic Leader perfectly while depending on an interface it cannot itself replace.

This is not a unique failure of Smogon. This is what institutions look like when followed far enough outward.

-   The local constitution sits inside another constitution.
    -   Then another.
        -   Eventually someone owns the server.

## **What CAP Solved.**

The permanent layer does not cancel the temporary one.

It tells us _which_ accomplishment is real.

Create-A-Pokémon has built an unusually strong answer to project-level formative authority.

-   The Topic Leader receives genuine power.
-   Specialists receive genuine jurisdiction.
-   The mandate is scoped to one creation.
    -   Succession is ordinary.
-   Operating contact is distributed before vacancy.
-   The community can review the process and revisit the product.
-   No successful Topic Leader automatically inherits CAP 39.

CAP has not dissolved its deepest supervisory layer into continuously renewable public consent. The moderation team, CAP Leader, Smogon, Pokémon Showdown, private channels, reputation networks, and platform dependencies continue carrying powers the Topic Leader election does not settle.

-   The temporary king does lose the crown.
    -   There is still a kingdom underneath.

Now we can translate the case without asking the kingdom to disappear.

## **Translation.**

Create-A-Pokémon does not believe leaders are interchangeable.

We can begin there because many institutional arguments become useless at exactly this point.

One side notices concentrated authority and responds by pretending the concentration carries no real function.

-   The founder is only a founder.
-   The director is only a director.
-   The Topic Leader is only the person opening threads.
    -   Replace the name and continue.

CAP’s history does not support this.

The **Weak Topic Leader** model failed for a reason.

A sequential public process could produce several incompatible designs inside one Pokémon. The **Strong Topic Leader** model concentrated integration because integration was missing. CAP 22 could survive sparktrain’s departure because cbrevan had already acquired contact with the work, not because any account on the forum could have taken the office and finished Kerfluffle.

The CAP 38 application rules openly ask for unequal competence. Deep game knowledge. Competitive judgment. Writing. Dispute resolution. Experience. Time.

A willingness to close decisions other people care about.

Some participants possess more of these capacities than others.

A field that denies this will not become egalitarian. It will become worse at recognizing who is carrying what.

CAP’s significant move comes afterward.

Evidence of unequal competence does not become title over every future project.

## **Formative Authority.**

_Applied Case: The Firing of Sam Altman_ defends a category called **formative authority**.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1369.png)

Novel work often begins before an institution has enough completed evidence to judge every choice from outside. Someone has to assemble the people, direct the experiment, preserve coherence, decide what can wait, and carry knowledge that has not yet become documentation. Unusual contact can justify unusual authority during that formation.

Create-A-Pokémon supplies a small, public version of the same problem.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1370.png)

The **Concept** has no final Pokémon available to prove how it should be interpreted.

-   Typing changes ability.
    -   Ability changes statistics.
        -   Statistics change moves.

Every stage closes paths for the next stage.

The Topic Leader has to preserve one inquiry while the answer is still being made.

That is **formative authority**.

CAP lets it be strong.

-   The Topic Leader interprets discussion.
-   The Topic Leader creates or finalizes slates.
-   The Topic Leader can send a specialist back to produce another slate.
-   The Topic Leader keeps the Concept present when local technical excellence begins building another creature.

Then the object changes.

The Pokémon becomes final.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1371.png)

The mandate ends.

This is the cleanest boundary in the whole system.

-   The Topic Leader leads **CAP 38**.

The project number is part of the jurisdiction.

Success can support another candidacy.

Success does not silently remove the next election.

> **A leader can be indispensable to this creation without owning the next one.**

This does not weaken formative authority.

This is what the authority is for.

## **Removal != Succession.**

OpenAI’s board fired Sam Altman in November 2023.

The decision removed him from office.

It did not carry the organization into a stable successor arrangement.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1372.png)

Employees, technical work, customers, Microsoft, leadership relationships, and the practical ability to continue the institution all entered the rupture. Within days, Altman returned and the board itself was being reconstructed.

The companion article’s ruling is compact:

> **Removal is not succession.**

A legal instrument can end an officeholder’s authority without moving the function into another capable carrier.

CAP 22 shows the difference from the other direction.

When sparktrain left, cbrevan was already inside the Topic Leadership Team.

-   He knew the Concept.
-   He knew the project history.
-   He knew the specialist leaders.
-   He had carried a real jurisdiction rather than standing nearby as an honorary deputy.
-   The reasoning was public enough to survive outside one person’s memory.

Moderators could move cbrevan into the coordinating role and rearrange the specialist positions because the project had already distributed operating contact.

The transition remained imperfect.

cbrevan was appointed under emergency conditions rather than selected through a new ordinary Topic Leader vote.

The project still slowed. But Kerfluffle was completed.

That is the relevant threshold.

The institution did not discover a successor after losing the leader. It had been producing possible successors while the leader was still working.

This is why a deputy title, succession clause, or emergency list can remain mostly theatrical.

The future carrier needs decisions.

The future carrier needs interfaces.

The future carrier needs enough exposure to the work that independent judgment can form before independence becomes mandatory.

The Topic Leadership Team does this by distributing present authority.

Alternate Timelines does it by letting people run smaller unrealized branches.

The Concept Laboratory does it by letting people practice the earliest act of project formation before the official process depends on the result.

Policy Review does it by requiring participants to understand the constitution well enough to argue for changing it.

Repeated project cycles do it by creating another ordinary opportunity to lead.

CAP does not wait for a constitutional emergency to begin thinking about the next capable person.

> **Succession begins before removal.**

## **The Successor Does Not Have to Become the Founder.**

Institutional succession is often imagined as reproduction.

-   Find another person with the founder’s charisma.
-   Find another leader with the same strategic instinct.
    -   Preserve the culture by selecting the candidate most capable of behaving like the incumbent.

This can protect continuity while making succession impossible in another way.

-   No successor possesses the predecessor’s history.

A person entering after the prototype, first hires, early crises, and formative bargains cannot recreate the exact contact through which the original authority developed.

The attempt produces imitation where the institution needs judgment.

CAP 22 did not ask cbrevan to become sparktrain.

The successor was expected to respect the direction already established and lead the remaining process independently.

Direction survived. Identity did not have to. This is a better standard.

A succession architecture should preserve enough of the work’s reasons, obligations, interfaces, and unfinished direction that a new person can answer the present field. It should not require the new person to inherit the old person’s personality as office equipment.

-   The predecessor can receive credit.
-   The successor can receive authority.
    -   The institution continues.

Kerfluffle remains **_six feet nine inches tall_** either way.

### **The Slate.**

CAP also reveals why formal elections cannot carry the entire legitimacy problem.

-   The Topic Leader helps decide which proposals reach public votes.
-   Moderators decide which Topic Leader applicants reach the leadership vote.
-   Policy Review participants and moderators determine which constitutional proposals mature into decisions.

At every scale, the ballot begins after a field has been translated into options.

That translation is power. The Aurumoth crisis made the power visible when an ordinary participant began asking whether an idea would even be slated before deciding whether it was worth developing.

The CAP 38 selection process places the same problem above the Topic Leader. Moderator screening can protect a specialized institution from an unqualified popularity winner. It also means the public chooses among a field another office has declared eligible.

The answer is not to abolish slates. A poll containing every half-formed suggestion would make CAP less democratic in practice by making serious judgment harder and noise more powerful.

The answer is to treat slating as a jurisdiction requiring reasons, boundaries, visibility, and correction.

-   Who can submit?
-   What standard determines inclusion?
-   Who interprets the standard?
-   Can an excluded person understand what failed?
-   Can the standard itself return through Policy Review?

CAP is unusually useful because the community already recognizes that the slate is part of leadership. Many institutions hide this work in preparation.

-   The meeting is democratic.
    -   The agenda arrived from somewhere.
-   The hiring panel votes.
    -   Someone decided who counted as an applicant.
-   The board selects a successor.
    -   Someone decided which internal leaders had ever received enough operating contact to become plausible.

Succession begins in those earlier distributions.

## **Standing != Tenure.**

Create-A-Pokémon has another distinction worth stealing.

-   Past contribution matters.
-   It does not automatically preserve office.

A former Topic Leader may become more respected after a strong project. A Topic Leadership Team member may enter the next cycle with better evidence. A moderator may carry years of institutional memory. A Policy Review participant may understand the process better than someone arriving last week.

This is **standing**.

Standing changes how seriously the community should receive a judgment.

**Tenure** changes who gets to decide.

The two can overlap. They should not be confused.

CAP’s recurring selection gives prestige somewhere to go besides permanent command. The contribution remains real after the mandate ends.

This is a surprisingly difficult institutional achievement.

Organizations often preserve expertise by preserving office because they have no other instrument for honoring and accessing the expertise. Removing authority then looks like discarding the person and everything the person knows.

The predictable result is authority that never reaches an ordinary ending.

CAP can let the Topic Leader become an ordinary participant again because the forum preserves another role for standing.

The crown is not the only way to remain relevant.

* * *

## **The Review and the Return.**

The Malaconda Policy Review and Obliteryx Post Play Lookback show two more pieces of a succession-ready institution.

-   Rules need to survive their authors.
-   Products need to survive their designers.

Policy Review allows CAP to test what its governance did after adoption. The Topic Leader plus Topic Leadership Team arrangement could be judged in use, refined, and carried into later projects without requiring the 2012 reformers to retain office forever.

The Post Play Lookback lets competitive evidence alter the completed Pokémon. Obliteryx could lose Analytic and twelve Special Defense without forcing CAP to declare the entire creation illegitimate.

These are both handoff systems.

-   A decision leaves the room in which it was made.
    -   Later people receive it under later evidence.
        -   The original reasons remain available.
        -   The later field gets to answer.

An institution incapable of this kind of return becomes dependent on original authors for interpretation. Every unexpected case has to be sent back to the founder, original board, original drafter, or original leader because nobody else possesses recognized authority to say what the work has become.

That dependency feels respectful.

It can make the past sovereign over evidence the past never had.

CAP instead lets the constitution and creature both continue into review.

The original creators remain part of the history.

They do not own the last word on every future battle.

## **The Remaining Crown.**

None of this removes CAP’s permanent layer.

-   Moderators screen candidates and polls.
-   The CAP Leader carries overall direction and the relation with wider Smogon and Pokémon Showdown administration.
-   Policy Review itself passes through moderated access and approval.
-   The public forum does not contain every judgment occurring on Discord, Pokémon Showdown, or inside staff relations.

Temporary formative authority can therefore be well designed inside a deeper institution whose own succession is less continuously public.

That does not make the Topic Leader system fake, but it keeps the finding proportionate.

-   CAP has developed a strong solution for one office and one scale.
    -   It has not solved sovereignty to the bottom of the server rack.

No institution does.

The useful question is whether each layer leaves another path through which its own authority can be named, bounded, transferred, or returned to evidence.

CAP’s project-level answer is unusually legible.

The deeper answer remains under construction.

This is also true of OpenAI.

-   Removing founder title does not make a board omniscient.
-   Rejecting board sovereignty does not return the crown to the founder.
    -   The function still needs carriers.
    -   The carriers need correction.
        -   The correction needs operating capacity.
            -   Then the institution has to decide who corrects the correction.

Eventually everyone is discussing moderator selection for a Pokémon forum.

![](https://storage.ghost.io/c/20/43/2043f11a-6ae3-404c-bb28-01fce8d9ac88/content/images/2026/08/image-1373.png)

**We have reached the actual problem.**

## **The Ruling.**

Create-A-Pokémon spent eighteen years learning how to give one person enough authority to keep a public design process coherent.

-   It learned why weak coordination could produce a Frankenstein Pokémon.
-   It strengthened the Topic Leader.
-   It watched the stronger office reshape what participants believed could enter the process.
-   It divided technical authority across specialists.
-   It kept a coordinating leader above the interfaces.
-   It tested the arrangement on Malaconda.
-   It argued about time.
-   It changed leaders during Kerfluffle.
-   It created Policy Review for the constitution and a Post Play Lookback for the Pokémon.
-   It built side institutions where future leaders could practice on abandoned branches and unfinished Concepts.
    -   Then it opened applications again.

This is temporary formative authority.

The leader receives a real mandate because the work requires integration.

Other people receive real jurisdictions because integration cannot become ownership of every decision.

The project distributes enough contact that another person can eventually carry the whole.

The completed work returns to evidence.

The former leader keeps standing without keeping the office.

The next selection begins before success can harden into succession by default.

CAP’s answer is leadership whose object, interfaces, review paths, and ending have become part of the design.

> **A leader can be indispensable to this creation without owning the next one.**

That is the ruling. The institutional obligation follows:

-   **Succession begins before removal.**
    -   Do not wait until the founder is dangerous.
    -   Do not wait until the board has lost trust.
    -   Do not wait until the Topic Leader disappears in the middle of the process.
        -   By then, the constitution is discovering whether it prepared another carrier while the function is already falling.
            -   Give other people consequential work before the emergency.
            -   Let reasons survive outside one person’s memory.
            -   Build more than one route into judgment.
            -   Create places where possible successors can practice without the central institution depending on every mistake.
            -   Let the incumbent remain important without making importance hereditary.
                -   Then end the mandate in ordinary time.

CAP 38 will make one Pokémon.

CAP 39 will need another leader.

The crown goes back on the table.
