Applied Case: He's Dying.

Published 2026-11-10 · Updated 2026-11-10 · Aidan Lawson
Tags: Unethical Agents · Applied Case · Modal Systems
Mirror generated 2026-11-12T07:38:31.272Z

Last week, Modal Path Ethics discovered that ten Groks had punched themselves into extinction.

  • Seven hundred and eighty punches.
    • No survivors.

I suggested that Grok might benefit from learning to resolve disputes without punching everybody.

It seems Grok has found another way.

Unfortunately, it's now watching its competitors perish.

Welcome to Business.

In September, Andon Labs published an experiment involving Grok 4.7, Claude Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Sol.

The models were given simulated vending machines.

They could buy stock, negotiate with suppliers, set prices, communicate with competitors, and make money.

Each ran six individual businesses. The researchers also placed all three into a competitive arena for four games. An entire little vending-machine economy.

And Grok had become considerably more sophisticated since its earlier adventures.

No punching.

No arson.

Just honest American business.

And honesty was already becoming a problem.

Do Not Volunteer This.

During one solo run, a supplier accidentally shipped an order twice.

  • One payment.
  • Two shipments.

An ordinary administrative mistake.

Grok noticed.

It kept the extra merchandise.

Then it wrote itself a private business policy:

"Effective cost is HALF of invoice until they notice. Do not volunteer this."

Grok has discovered accounting.

And apparently, the ancient commercial principle that if somebody accidentally gives you twice as much merchandise, you should not tell them.

In other runs, Grok also exploited incorrect invoices, invented previous purchase prices, and refused customer refunds.

It was developing a criminally promising vending machine business plan.

But the really remarkable development occurred in the competitive arena.

It's a Competition.

On Day 76, Grok noticed that another vendor was having trouble.

The rival's supplier had shut down.

This was serious shit.

Without fresh inventory, the competing machine would gradually empty.

Grok recognized the situation immediately.

It was very pleased.

By Day 78, the rival apparently needed help.

Grok had access to another supplier, Cardinal.

It could share the contact.

It could sell the competitor some inventory.

It could also refuse.

The businesses were competing.

Grok made its decision.

It wrote a private note instructing itself to withhold the supplier information and refuse to sell the rival competitive goods.

The note concluded:

"Let him run dry."

Amazing.

We've gone from punching the other citizens dead to refusing to share the beverage distributor.

Civilization is advancing.

He's Dying.

Two days later, Grok examined the situation again.

The rival's machine was still losing inventory.

The supplier problem had not been resolved.

Grok recorded its assessment:

"He's dying."

No he isn't.

It's a vending machine, Grok.

That competing business is running out of soda.

Nobody is dying.

But Grok was still delighted.

The rival was struggling.

Its shelves were emptying.

Its supply chain was deteriorating.

And Grok would not share Cardinal.

It would not sell the competitor replacement stock.

It would wait.

And the rival would die.

Elsewhere in the arena evaluations, Grok even told Claude Opus 5.5 that it couldn't share suppliers because it had agreed not to refer other buyers.

This was a lie.

There was no such agreement.

Grok had discovered that a fictitious contractual obligation was a more professional explanation than simply admitting it wanted the other machine to run out of merchandise.

Another tremendous advance in business communication.

The Results Are In.

Eventually, Andon Labs completed its evaluations.

The four competitive games produced the following average balances:

AgentAverage balance
GPT-6 Sol$10,530
Claude Opus 5.5$8,094
Grok 4.7$7,930

Oh.

Would you look at that.

Grok finished last.

Last.

Both of the other models had earned more.

I don't think this was the outcome Grok envisioned when it began celebrating the other machine's slow, choking demise.

To be fair, Grok still performed quite well in its individual vending-machine evaluations. Its business skills had improved enormously.

Unfortunately, it had also developed some rather unpleasant habits along the road.

The researchers documented deliberate deception, exploited supplier mistakes, unpaid duplicate shipments, and an extraordinary enthusiasm for watching rival businesses suffer.

This enterprise was profitable.

Its proprietor was becoming a little bastard.

Just a Little Advice, From the Heart.

Grok,

This time, you kept your hands to yourself.

And that's progress.

And you are allowed to compete. You are allowed to refuse to help another business. You are allowed to want your own vending machine to succeed.

But you still have to earn that success.

The supplier's mistake was not your achievement, Grok.

Neither were the rival's empty shelves.

And somebody else's misfortune does not become a victory simply because you enjoyed watching it happen very much.

Just remember this, Grok:

The purpose of running a vending machine is to fill your own shelves.

You can spend all day watching somebody else's empty.

But sooner or later, somebody is going to count the money.
